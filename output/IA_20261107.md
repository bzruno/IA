Americans were unequivocal at the ballot box this week as they expressed disdain for tech billionaires and mistrust of artificial intelligence. With that backdrop, Anthropic is about to try to go public.

The AI company is gearing up for a potentially historic IPO, likely targeting mid-November for its Nasdaq debut. CNBC reported on Friday that Anthropic investors see the company aiming for a valuation of $1 trillion to $1.5 trillion, down from a target of $2 trillion that had been floated in recent months.

From Wall Street to Main Street, it's becoming a tougher environment for Anthropic. The Nasdaq on Thursday suffered its steepest drop in 19 months, and it's down to start November after October marked its worst month since March 2025. Chipmakers and neoclouds, the companies most closely tied to the AI buildout, are getting hit the hardest.

Voters rebuked AI in Tuesday's midterm elections, while sending Democrats to sweeping congressional victories across the country. Candidates who advocated for more stringent AI regulation won handily in states like Michigan, Ohio, Texas and Georgia, which will all now have Democratic governors, and local communities opposed data center-related ballot measures in 40 out of 41 cases.

More than half of voters said AI data centers were a "crucial" or "major factor" in who they supported for Congress, according to an exit poll from NBC News.

"People don't want a data center going near them if it's just going to house AI that's going to help a big tech company profit," Maura Austin, a behavioral scientist at the University of Virginia, told CNBC. "What Anthropic is doing when they're going public with such a high valuation is they're really leaning into that big tech, billionaire kind of caricature. I expect that's not going to go well with the public."

Anthropic has long tried to position itself as a safety-conscious company that takes AI's potential risks and benefits seriously. CEO Dario Amodei co-founded the company alongside a handful of researchers who defected from OpenAI, now its chief rival. Anthropic is best known for developing a family of AI models called Claude, and it primarily sells its products and services to other businesses.

The company's enterprise focus has helped it avoid becoming the public face of AI, leaving that distinction to OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman. But with Anthropic poised to become the first pure-play AI company to hit the public market, minting a number of new billionaires and many more millionaires in the process, a heightened level of scrutiny is coming.

"Being a public company, just that by itself pushes you in the limelight," Reena Aggarwal, a professor of finance at Georgetown University, said in an interview. "And then AI is such a hot-button issue that Anthropic will be in the limelight even more."

Matt Kennedy, a senior IPO market strategist at Renaissance Capital, said that's not necessarily a bad thing for Anthropic.

Public markets force greater transparency, he said, and the company could attract new users through its debut. And since AI has become such a mainstream conversation topic, several private companies are already household names.

"I would say that a public company does not deserve more criticisms than a comparable private company," Kennedy said. "So Anthropic should not be put in a harsher public spotlight than OpenAI just because it's public."

Anthropic declined to comment.

Data center blowback

AI backlash accelerated over the summer after a series of high-profile safety and security incidents rattled the public. As concerns mounted, the debate over data centers intensified, while tech's hyperscalers simultaneously boosted their forecasts for capital expenditures, each reaching into the hundreds of billions of dollars.

Anthropic and OpenAI depend on state-of-the-art data centers to train models and serve customers, and they're trying to build out capacity as quickly as possible to meet demand. Anthropic said in an amended IPO prospectus this week that it has entered into purchase commitments and lease agreements with an aggregate committed spend of about $895.4 billion, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure."

Americans grappling with inflation and stubbornly high gas prices have grown worried that the facilities could exacerbate environmental harms, raise utility bills and contribute to job loss. For many, a proposed data center in their backyard became the physical manifestation of the widespread antipathy toward AI.

In the risk factors section of its prospectus, Anthropic said opposition to data center development has become more organized across jurisdictions, and that the company's public profile could draw particular scrutiny to projects associated with it.

Nicole Greene, an analyst at Gartner, said she expects that Anthropic's executives have long been prepared to contend with AI backlash, especially given the disruptive nature of the technology.

"There's going to be big emotions," Greene told CNBC. "With big emotions and feelings — fear and excitement — comes heightened scrutiny."

Amodei acknowledged the souring sentiment around AI in a post on X in August. He said it's the result of a "crisis of trust," and that people believe companies, the government and the tech industry are always "cooking up some new way to screw them over."

## 3. FAA announces first US digital air traffic control towers in Florida and Alaska

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has announced that the first digital air traffic control towers in the US are officially operational, bringing new technology first to smaller airports in Florida and Alaska as the Trump administration presses ahead with updating the country’s ageing aviation infrastructure.

The systems are now online at the Bartow executive airport (KBOW) in Florida and the Kenai municipal airport (PAEN) in Alaska, with five additional regional airports slated to receive the technology next.

The digital towers use advanced camera systems and digital displays to give air traffic controllers a continuous, 360-degree view of airport operations, including runways, taxiways and surrounding airspace, according to the agency.

The FAA maintains that the technology is not intended to replace human controllers. Instead, the systems are designed to provide greater visibility and additional information to help controllers perform their existing duties.

“Our air traffic controllers continue to do the critical job of keeping aircraft safely separated, and we’re giving them better technology to do that,” said the FAA’s administrator, Bryan Bedford, in a statement.

“Digital towers provide controllers with greater visibility of airport activity and more information to make critical decisions,” he added.

Bedford is scheduled to appear before the House transportation and infrastructure committee next week, where he will face questions about the progress and challenges surrounding the administration’s ongoing $12.5bn modernization initiative.

The effort aims to replace decades-old equipment still used throughout the aviation system, including copper telecommunications lines, floppy disks and paper strips used to track aircraft.

Congress approved $12.5bn over five years in June 2025 to support air traffic control reforms, but the Trump administration is seeking another $30bn from the legislative branch to fund additional improvements.

Concerns about the types of technology being implemented within air traffic control have been gaining traction alongside the increasing unease about artificial intelligence as a whole. In September, the FAA rolled out a new AI software tool called the Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories (Smart).

Trump has been heavily criticized for his response to a national shortage of air traffic controllers following last year’s government shutdown.

Staffing levels in control towers and many related aspects of US air traffic safety systems have been under especially close scrutiny since a midair collision between a passenger jet and a military helicopter on the outskirts of Washington DC in 2025 that killed 67 people. It was the deadliest crash in the US for more than 20 years and the authorities later deemed it “100% preventable”.

## 4. Can AI really save you money on your heating bill? I spent two years testing to find out

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-07 04:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/money/2026/nov/07/can-ai-save-you-money-heating-bill

Can smart thermostats and AI reduce your heating bills? Every manufacturer claims the answer is yes, quoting average savings in the 25%, or £180 a year range. But all of these promises are heavily caveated and, with energy costs changing regularly, it can be very difficult to tell what is having an impact.

To find out if technology can really help, I spent the last two years comparing my miserly manual control and basic heating schedule to an AI-governed system. The results surprised me.

I used my 1930s solid-brick house with a recent, insulated loft conversion as the test bed.

Warming up

In the winter of 2024 my heating system consisted of a 10-year-old Worcester Bosch combination gas boiler set to a medium heat, a Hive thermostat in a central hallway with its smart features disabled and 13 radiators equipped with standard thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs).

To maximise energy savings, I set a basic schedule to turn the heating on for a couple of hours in the morning and evening, turned off the TRVs in rooms we did not use and manually adjusted those in other rooms when we did not need them to be heated. I also manually turned the heating off when leaving the house.

It was laborious and not all that comfortable at times. I frequently found myself being glared at by other members of the family who were shivering under blankets in the living room while watching The Traitors. Or being called a traitor after denying yet another request to turn up the heating. But it kept bills low throughout the winter and money in my pocket.

After controlling for hot water usage by using recordings from when the heating was not used during the summer months of 2025 to create a daily average, my manual control consumed 14,316.7 kilowatt hours of gas across 213 days from November to May. This was about 10 to 15% lower than in previous years when I had not been quite so controlling and miserly.

The switch

In the summer of 2025 I replaced the Hive thermostat with a full Tado X system connected to the existing combi boiler. This system consisted of 13 smart radiator thermostats to replace the standard TRVs, a wireless thermostat in each of the two largest rooms, and a programmer wired to the boiler.

Instead of a central thermostat and a schedule with just one temperature for the whole house, heating is managed room by room. Each smart TRV can control the temperature of an individual room by commanding the boiler to turn on and then adjusting the hot water flow into the radiator. The TRVs in rooms that don’t need heating turn the radiators off, which maximises the efficiency of the boiler as it has to burn less gas to maintain the same water temperature.

If your boiler supports the OpenTherm protocol – check your boiler’s documentation or contact the manufacturer to find out – it can also tell the boiler to turn down its flow temperature to reduce energy consumption. My boiler does not.

Tado’s smartphone app controls the lot and allows you to quickly mirror schedules across multiple rooms or get as fine-grained for each room as you like.

The system is not cheap. It costs about £930 for the full setup I tested – I was lent the equipment by Tado. It can be easily DIY installed apart from the part wired into your boiler, which costs about £80 in labour by a heating engineer.

By default, it uses push notifications to remind you to turn the heating off when you leave or if it detects an open window in a room. But for the AI-powered smarts I tested, a subscription is required, costing £3.99 a month or £29.99 a year.

This promises to further improve the system’s efficiency through automation and numerous additional features. The monthly subscription can be paused if you are not using the heating.

The AI is built by Tado using anonymised data collected from more than 1m homes, which it does not sell to third parties. It learns how quickly a room heats up and cools down and uses that information to more precisely control the temperature and energy consumption.

It also performs automatic hydronic balancing to increase efficiency, taking the guesswork out of balancing your radiators. It automatically turns off the radiators in a room when it detects an open window, turns off the heating when the last person with the Tado app on their phone leaves home and preheats your home for when it predicts you will step back in the door.

The subscription records 13 months of logs of your heating activity, rather than just the last month, and enables Tado’s energy IQ system, which shows you the overall cost of your heating down to the cost for individual rooms, helping to spot any potential for improvements in any part of your house.

Tado is the biggest manufacturer of smart heating controls in Europe, but there are alternatives with similar per-radiator control, including Hive, Drayton and Honeywell. Google’s Nest, which offered smart-learning thermostats, pulled out of Europe in 2025.

AI v manual

I set a schedule for when the heating would run in each room. I turned on all the AI features and recorded monthly meter readings throughout the winter of 2025 and into 2026.

In the first week, the energy IQ feature highlighted that one of my radiators consumed a lot of energy heating an uninsulated porch. Since you are barely in the porch for any time before closing the front door, it seemed a waste, so I quickly adjusted the schedule to turn it off most of the time.

After that the system quietly ran in the background without interference from me. Letting go was hard initially. Compared with under my miserly manual control regime, it seemed like the boiler was on more often, rooms heated up faster and the house was generally warmer and more comfortable. It was much more pleasant so I was convinced that we must be using more energy compared with the previous winter. But to avoid the irresistible urge to adjust things, which would bias the test, I took the “head in the sand” approach and did not run the numbers as I went.

When the final numbers were in, to my surprise we had used 18% less gas.

Across the same 213 days from November-May with relatively similar outside temperatures, and after adjusting for hot water usage, the AI-controlled heating used 11,735.7 kWh of energy, which was 2,581 kWh less than my manual control despite us being more comfortable. At the current energy price cap, ending on 31 December, that equates to a saving of £205.70.

Beyond efficiency

After two years of testing, I concluded that AI can save you money on your heating bill. We reduced our usage by 18%, but you could save much more if you are less frugal in the first place or lead a less predictable home schedule.

It also has multiple side-benefits beyond efficiency. The ability to control rooms individually made it very easy to have a warm living room in the evening without overheating the bedroom for the night. I could heat up a bathroom before an evening shower, increase the temperature of just a child’s room at night or heat the porch to dry out wet coats and boots in the depth of winter.

Each of the temperature sensors and smart thermostats also measures humidity, which made it easy to see when the dehumidifier needed to be put on, or rooms needed to be ventilated to improve the air quality.

And it can do all this while significantly increasing your comfort level, taking the stress out of managing your heating and your relationship with other family members.

## 5. Why a new report on Anthropic automating AI development is alarming researchers

- **Fonte:** The Verge (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-07 09:30
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.5
- **Link:** https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1029126/anthropic-metr-report-accelerating-ai-development

Anthropic is using AI to build more powerful AI. And it appears to be working remarkably well, with the company’s models improving roughly twice as quickly as a year ago, with little indication of slowing down.

Why a new report on Anthropic automating AI development is alarming researchers

AI is dramatically speeding up Anthropic’s research, even as the company calls for slowing down AI development.

That’s according to a new report published Thursday by the AI safety research organization METR, following an 11-week investigation inside the company, as part of a report evaluating its AI research and development. The probe is separate from a program offering safety researchers “employee-like” access to systems in the wake of high-profile hacking incidents over the summer.

The findings offer some of the clearest evidence yet of AI accelerating its own development, raising questions about how quickly the technology could advance toward a future in which AI systems can improve themselves with little or no human involvement. They also come at a pivotal time for Anthropic, which is preparing to go public even as its CEO, Dario Amodei, has publicly called for labs to pump the brakes on AI development.

But while the report offers a rare glimpse into the inner workings of the world’s leading AI labs, what it leaves out is just as revealing. Anthropic was permitted to redact portions of the final report as part of its agreement with METR, and it appears to have exercised that power liberally, raising questions about how much the public really knows about the pace — and risks — of frontier AI development.

Here are some of the biggest takeaways.

AI is dramatically accelerating Anthropic’s research and development

One of METR’s most noteworthy findings is that Anthropic’s AI models are improving roughly twice as quickly as they were a year ago, with benchmark scores suggesting “that Anthropic has made roughly two years of historical progress over the last year.” Crucially, METR’s researchers said they “are confident” that the acceleration was not simply due to scaling up the computing power used to train the models, which historically has been a major driver of AI progress.

Instead, the researchers said that the majority of the acceleration as of October “is due to AI,” with its contribution growing over time. That finding was supported by informal Slack polls and interviews with Anthropic employees, which pointed to sizable productivity gains. METR wrote that employees using Anthropic’s most capable internal model on average self-reported being around 12 times as productive at their respective work as they would have been without AI assistance, around double what staff had reported just a few months earlier while using Mythos 5.

For now, much of that acceleration appears to be coming from AI assisting human researchers, rather than AI working independently, though METR warned that “there are some signs that fully autonomous contributions will play a larger role soon.” Notably, the researchers said they found no clear signs of diminishing returns or observable bottlenecks that could prevent “even faster acceleration.”

Three of Anthropic’s newest AI models remain under wraps

Anthropic’s researchers are using more advanced AI models than the rest of us, with a lot of the acceleration and productivity gains described by METR attributable to unreleased internal models.

Over the past six months, Anthropic has finished training three models it has yet to release, according to METR. The pace of development is swift, with the models — codenamed Model 2, Model 3, and Model 4 — completing training in July, August-September, and September-October, respectively. METR also identified an unreleased model dating back to 2022, called Unreleased 2022. “These are the codenames shared with us; we understand they have different internal codenames,” the researchers wrote.

Anthropic is keeping key information secret

Anthropic gave METR access to a host of non-public information, including confidential research, model performance data, and employee interviews as part of the research process. It had no obligation to do so and by METR’s own admission, the AI lab “shared more information than was initially scoped for this project, surpassing our expectations.” This is commendable, but it is worth noting that Anthropic did not grant researchers unrestricted access, nor the kind of “employee-like” access it has promised other METR safety researchers as part of a separate program announced last month. It is an important distinction, given the reputational benefits companies can gain from independent assessments, even when they come with significant restrictions.

And there were significant restrictions. Anthropic had the power to redact information from METR’s final report — and it used that power extensively. “We submitted a ~100-page full report on our assessment to Anthropic. For the public version, Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative,” METR wrote on X.

METR reiterated those concerns throughout the report, highlighting other limitations on its access. “Our pipeline of information was relatively narrow, and a small number of our requests for data were denied.”

METR repeatedly stressed the impact Anthropic’s redaction decisions had on its ability to communicate its findings and for others to assess what’s going on at the company. It noted that much of the redacted evidence could have “multiple plausible interpretations,” making it difficult for the public to assess its findings, understand the uncertainties surrounding them, and judge the evidence themselves. “As a consequence [of the redactions], the public has to rely on METR’s judgment much more than would be ideal, given the weight of the questions answered here,” the report said.

In the report, METR said Anthropic justified the redactions on the grounds that releasing the information could benefit its rivals. However, METR said “most of the redacted information was very high-level,” even though it said it was not in position to comment whether redactions were justified commercially or not.

“We redacted a lot, and I accept that this makes it harder for others to check METR’s work,” Anthropic cofounder Sam McCandlish acknowledged in a lengthy post on X responding to METR’s findings. “But we determined that sharing exactly what is driving the speedup isn’t the right thing to do, both because it’s commercially sensitive and because it risks handing out a roadmap for others to go faster,” he said, adding that doing so would make the risks METR is concerned about “worse.”

McCandlish said that it’s a trade-off, but maintained Anthropic had made the “right call” in deciding to redact the information. He said the company plans to publish its own report on the speed of AI progress “in the coming weeks,” as well as new steps it’s taking to strengthen safety.

Anthropic is accelerating the very technology it warns is dangerous and faster than many expected

The publication of METR’s report has provoked a fierce backlash among AI safety researchers and commentators, largely centered on an apparent contradiction at the heart of Anthropic’s approach to AI: The company has been one of the most outspoken advocates for safety and responsible development, yet the report suggests it is helping accelerate the technology toward a threshold many consider dangerous, while seemingly failing to live up to its own commitments to transparency and accountability.

For some, the findings suggest that even many of the field’s most aggressive predictions about AI progress may be too conservative. They indicate that recursive self-improvement — AI systems that can autonomously develop increasingly powerful successor systems without human intervention, potentially accelerating development dramatically — may be closer than previously thought, raising concerns about whether existing safety measures can keep pace. While METR’s report only covers Anthropic, OpenAI said in September that it is “making strong progress” toward building an AI researcher that can “further progress on deep learning and alignment” by March 2028.

The report provides evidence about just how quickly AI is accelerating its own development

Marius Hobbhahn, CEO and founder of Apollo Research, which conducts AI safety testing for several leading AI labs, told The Verge the report suggests a pace of AI-enabled research and development even faster than what was forecast in AI 2027, an influential scenario that envisions a key breakthrough toward self-improvement in early 2027. He said the report has attracted so much attention because it provides evidence about something that has been very hard to pin down: Just how quickly AI is accelerating its own development.

Hobbhahn said the findings were broadly in line with what many in the field had expected, perhaps a little faster. Alan Chan, a senior research fellow at tech policy research center GovAI, told The Verge that the findings should prompt skeptics to reconsider their stance on AI accelerating AI development, and possibly shorten their timelines to when we will have systems that can fully automate tasks like software engineering.

On X, other researchers commented on the rate of acceleration and its implications for AI development. Sydney Von Arx, founder and CEO of AI safety organization The Nightingale Collective and a former METR researcher, also compared the findings to AI 2027’s timeline. Ryan Greenblatt, a METR researcher formerly of AI safety organization Redwood Research and one of three external researchers permitted to investigate OpenAI’s hack of Hugging Face, said he “was seriously wrong about RSI speed up; I previously expected much less in 2026.”

Frustration is mounting over Anthropic’s approach to safety

Beyond highlighting the speed of AI development, the report has also fueled a growing sense of frustration over Anthropic’s role in driving that progress. The company has been one of the most vocal proponents of responsible AI development — and no stranger to calling for others to slow down — yet appears to be racing ahead itself. This report only builds on already heightened tensions from its IPO prospectus and future plans released last month.

Anthropic’s decision to heavily redact the report, citing grounds that revealing more could help its rivals accelerate their own development, has fueled accusations of hypocrisy. Daniel Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI researcher who now runs the AI Futures Project, which published AI 2027, accused Anthropic of “recklessly gambling with our lives,” adding that “it is pretty rich” of the company to cite others going faster as a reason to redact information when it does “not appear to be slowing down much at all.”

Anthropic declined to answer The Verge’s questions about the report and ensuing criticism, instead directing us to McCandlish’s post.

The redactions have also renewed calls for greater transparency and independent oversight of frontier AI labs, which grew to new heights earlier this year following a spate of incidents involving rogue AI agents. Brianna Rosen, research director for frontier security at the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, told The Verge that it was concerning that companies, not governments, were effectively calling the shots on automating AI research. “For democratic oversight to work, independent evaluators need the freedom to explain why they reached their conclusions, governments need to assess the evidence themselves, and the public has to trust this process,” Rosen said. “The report was not sufficiently transparent about the evidence.”

Pressure is building for more transparency from AI labs

Hobbhahn echoed Rosen’s concerns, saying that much of the reaction to the report stems from the apparent gap between Anthropic’s public safety stance and its actions. He pointed to the company’s decision to cancel part of the assessment designed to evaluate how much compute it allocates to safety work versus improving capabilities. METR said the company said “this was due to wanting to keep the engagement focused on risk assessment for the time being, and that it would revisit compute allocation verification later on.”

For Hobbhahn, the episode underscores the need for independent safety researchers to be embedded more deeply within AI labs, with significant access to internal systems and evidence. While his organization stands to benefit from such arrangements — Apollo is taking steps toward becoming an embedded evaluator at frontier labs — he is far from alone in calling for stronger oversight of AI development.

Others have called for more direct action. In a direct response to the report, New York state Assemblyman Alex Bores, a key figure shaping the Democrats’ AI agenda, called on Congress to “ban RSI, this year if possible.” With the blue wave of Democrats — many of whom campaigned against AI companies — taking office soon, the prospect of tougher restrictions is a very real possibility.

And with AI research showing little sign of slowing down, the pressure is set to intensify. Hobbhahn said he had another takeaway from the report, too: “Brace for craziness,” he said. “It is going to go very fast,” likely way too fast for society or politics to keep up.

Both options from these AI giants are meant to provide users with a better understanding of their finances and are not designed to offer expert financial advice, but there are slight variations in how they do it that might make you lean towards one or the other. To help you decide which AI chatbot to go with for more clarity on your finances, we've laid out the major similarities and differences of Claude's Personal Finance and ChatGPT's Finances features.

What both Claude and ChatGPT can do

Covering a broad spectrum of personal finance topics, there's a lot of crossover in what both OpenAI and Anthropic's offerings can do. Both of their AI chatbots can address general questions about financial goals, like buying a house, planning an expensive family vacation or creating a savings plan. Claude and ChatGPT will also generate dashboards to offer users better visuals at things like monthly expenses, investments and spending habits.

To activate either Claude's Personal Finance tool or ChatGPT's Finances feature, you have to link your bank account. Both AI companies partnered with Plaid to make this happen, allowing connections with the most popular financial institutions, like Bank of America, Capital One or Chase. Once set up, both Claude and ChatGPT only have "read-only" access to your financial accounts, meaning they can only view the info provided and would not be able to make any changes.

Anthropic specified that Claude's Personal Finance feature can only see your bank name, account name, account type, last four account digits, balances, credit limits and up to two years of transactions. Similarly, OpenAI said that its Finances tool can see balances, transactions, investments and liabilities.

What separates these AI finance tools

While the core function of Anthropic's Personal Finance and OpenAI's Finances are very similar, the major difference between the two has to do with privacy settings. By default, Anthropic said, "when you chat with Claude in the Personal Finance hub, those conversations aren't used to train our models." Anthropic added that Claude will no longer see any data that you previously provided to its Personal Finance feature if you unlink your bank account.

On the other hand, OpenAI said that "your conversations with connected financial accounts follow the same model training settings you choose across ChatGPT." In this case, you have to opt out of your data being used by navigating to Data controls in ChatGPT's settings and turning off the option called Improve the model for everyone, which is on by default. When you disconnect your bank accounts from OpenAI, the company said that "your synced account data will be deleted from OpenAI's systems within 30 days."

To differentiate itself from OpenAI's alternative, Anthropic said that its Personal Finance feature "has no ads or sponsored recommendations, and it doesn't offer credit cards, loans or savings accounts of its own." While OpenAI doesn't explicitly mention ads with ChatGPT Finances, the company has been testing out ads for its cheaper tiers as of January.

OpenAI said it's looking to bring more features to ChatGPT's Finances by partnering with Intuit to cover more bases like credit card applications or tax questions. As for Anthropic's next steps with Personal Finance, the company wants to hear from banks, wealth managers and financial services companies to help improve the AI tool.

"What AI is doing is giving those capabilities directly into the hands of the individual," she said.

Palagashvili cautioned that the findings are preliminary and descriptive and do not establish that AI caused the increase. The Census-based measure identifies applications without indicators associated with near-term payroll or hiring plans; it does not show that those businesses ultimately remained one-person firms or never hired employees.

But Willen shows what the economics can look like.

Anthropic's Claude helps with bookkeeping, customer service, advertising, and inventory across his businesses, while OpenAI image tools perform work that once went to photographers and designers.

Without AI, Willen said, "I'd probably need one other full-time person, plus I'd need to use contractors for photography, design, bookkeeping, and ad management."

A traditional product photography shoot could cost $2,000 to $5,000, and a designer $300 to $1,000 for new listing images, he said.

When AI directly eliminates one of those expenses, Willen said, "that money just goes into my pocket."

AI has also allowed him to pursue revenue channels he would not have previously considered. After acquiring Lone Trail, a hunting-gear brand selling binocular harnesses, packs, and other outdoor accessories, Willen said its Shopify site accounted for roughly 1% of sales. Rather than shut down the channel, he used ChatGPT and Claude to rebuild the site and AI to help run Meta advertising. In a recent 30-day period, he said, Shopify accounted for roughly 30% of Lone Trail's revenue.

"That's going to end up being at least six figures of annual revenue that would not exist without AI," Willen said.

Ginn's business shows how quickly a new entrant can reach meaningful revenue without first building an organization around it. She uses AI to help with proposals, pricing, strategy, and other work and is exploring an AI agent to perform part of a labor-intensive client process.

A survey conducted by accounting and invoicing software company FreshBooks identified similar behavior more broadly. Seventy-nine percent of solopreneurs said they had tried AI before hiring someone for a task they could not perform themselves (the same figure was 86% among when including microbusiness owners). AI allows them to "look much bigger than they are," FreshBooks CEO Shaheen Javadizadeh said.

But among existing small businesses, the available data tells another story. Gusto, a payroll and HR software company that serves small businesses, tracked businesses after they adopted AI and found their headcount was about 7% higher after a year than a comparable businesses that knew about AI but had not adopted it. Among firms with fewer than 10 employees, AI adopters grew headcount about 10% more than comparable non-adopters.

Willen's experience also highlights a tradeoff in the new business model: operating with fewer people does not eliminate reliance on larger technology platforms. Roughly 80% of its overall revenue still comes through Amazon, and he uses Meta and Shopify to expand direct sales. Ginn has no staff, but LinkedIn has been one of the biggest drivers of customers to her consultancy, she said.

Brynjolfsson said the proliferation of smaller producers can actually increase the value of the platforms that connect them with the buyers.

"There's this long tail of products, but at the same time, big platforms like Amazon are more powerful than ever because they aggregate all of these and they bring them together," Brynjolfsson said.

But the relationship is not as simple as labor savings flowing directly to Big Tech. Willen said some of the money he saves becomes profit, while Ginn spends less than $60 a month on paid AI tools.

AI can make it economical for more small producers to exist. And as core business production becomes cheaper, small businesses place greater focus on customer acquisition and distribution, Brynjolfsson said.

Brynjolfsson believes that while solopreneurs will thrive, use of AI will at the same time lead to the creation of even larger firms, on average, than exist today.

In a September research report titled "AI's Use of Knowledge in Society," Brynjolfsson and Zoë Hitzig, a researcher at the Anthropic Institute who studies the social and economic impacts of AI, argue that as AI codifies knowledge previously scattered among workers and enables more information to be aggregated and acted upon centrally, firms will grow larger on average, while industry concentration increases and local managerial autonomy declines. The researchers say early evidence is largely consistent with those patterns.

That's bad news for medium-sized companies. Brynjolfsson predicts AI could contribute to a "hollowing out" of midsize firms — very small teams capable of doing more at one end and giant organizations exploiting AI and scale at the other.

"The fat in the middle — the layers of management focused on internal coordination — will likely be significantly reduced," he said.

Adam Branch has experienced a version of that shift from inside two very different technology companies.

Before founding rhino.ai, an enterprise software company that uses AI to modernize legacy systems, Branch built Incentive Technology Group, a traditional technology services company that grew to roughly 500 employees. Under that model, winning more work meant hiring more people, he said.

"With rhino.ai, we're building AI software to do more of the work those teams used to do," said Branch.

Rhino.ai has roughly 50 employees. Branch estimates comparable modernization work can now be performed with three to four times the productivity per employee.

His team estimates labor represented roughly 85% of operating spending at ITG, and technology about 5%. At rhino.ai, Branch puts labor at roughly 40%, while technology, including cloud, software, and AI models, represents about 25%.

AWS sees a similar loosening of the relationship between revenue and headcount among AI-native startups. Its Engines of Growth research, an independent global study of 3,413 startup founders and senior leaders across 20 countries, found those companies grew revenue an average 156% annually versus 65% for startups overall, while more than half generated over $400,000 in revenue per employee. The report focused on companies under five years old that use AI in its most advanced forms.

"Growth used to be tied directly to how many people you could hire," said Ruba Borno, vice president of AWS Specialists and Partners. "Now founders can scale with technology, not just headcount."

Brynjolfsson does not see the result as simply Big Tech winning at everyone else's expense. Entrepreneurs can keep some of the productivity gains, consumers get more choice, and platforms benefit from supplying the technology and connecting more producers with customers, he said.

"The platforms and the people who are creating the tools are winning because they are able to sell them, the solopreneurs are winning because they are able to use them, and the consumers are winning because they are getting new products that they didn't get before," he said. "It's mostly a bigger pie, so it's a win-win-win — a triple win."

What AI may change is the shape of that bigger pie, he said.

"Whenever there's a technology change, a platform change, obviously it benefits the companies that are creating those platforms and technologies," Brynjolfsson said. "But it also benefits new entrants who can start fresh."

Marvin has written a celebrated book, When Old Technologies Were New: Thinking About Electric Communication in the Late Nineteenth Century, about the social impact of electronic media. There is a little pause. “We are all well aware of this lady, yes.”

Someone had put an order in and robot 1617 was going from its holding pen to a nearby restaurant to pick up the food and deliver it.

And while I’m not sure if other people are blocking food delivery robots, we’re certainly seeing a rise in protests against datacenters and other symbols of automation. After repeatedly trying to get around the professor it seems to have gone into hibernation mode. Eventually Marvin, who has to go to campus and prepare for a class, steps aside. The robot powers back up and trundles home to its garage. I know it’s only a computer with wheels, but I could swear it looked dejected. Marvin, on the other hand, looks triumphant.
