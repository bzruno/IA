When Notable Capital poured roughly 10% of the U.S. portion of its $1 billion-plus fund into Anthropic’s Series E, many limited partners were perplexed by the massive bet. Claude’s maker was still trailing its biggest rival, OpenAI, and DeepSeek had released its R1 model just weeks earlier, threatening to overtake closed-source competitors. Moreover, at $61.5 billion, Anthropic’s valuation was by far the highest that the predominantly early-stage venture firm had invested at in its 25-year history.

But investors at Notable, formerly known as GGV Capital before its 2024 split from its China operations, were confident in Anthropic’s future. A team that included managing partners Hans Tung and Glenn Solomon and principal Dan Cahana crunched the numbers and found that developers were increasingly favoring Claude over ChatGPT, using Claude 70% of the time, a reversal from the previous year.

“We just knew that for AI, the developer is at the center because they’re the early adopters,” Tung, a co-founder of the firm, told TechCrunch.

Having navigated the complexities of U.S.-China relations firsthand, Notable was also convinced that “mission-critical” work would always require U.S.-based, closed-source models, Tung said.

Less than two years later, Notable’s LPs are no longer questioning the outsized investment. On Wednesday, Anthropic raised $81.3 billion in the largest IPO in history, reaching a fully diluted valuation of about $1.5 trillion before its shares began trading the next day, a paper return of roughly 15 times.

Notable is, of course, not the only firm sitting on a massive paper windfall from Anthropic. Even traditional late-stage investors, who rarely see venture-scale multiples, are set for a big win.

Singaporean sovereign wealth fund GIC had been researching Anthropic throughout 2024, but it didn’t make its first investment until after Chris Emanuel, head of the fund’s tech investments, met with Anthropic CFO Krishna Rao at Che Fico, an Italian restaurant in San Francisco, in early 2025. Rao helped pique Emanuel’s interest in the company by showing that model improvements were driving rising enterprise adoption. GIC then made its first investment in Anthropic’s Series F, a round announced in September 2025 at a $183 billion valuation.

“Claude Code was just released then,” Emanuel told TechCrunch. “It was very early, but even then, we could see signs that it was really popular with developers.” GIC’s conviction in the AI lab was so high that the fund later led Anthropic’s Series G alongside Coatue at a $380 billion valuation, and returned as one of the co-leads for the pre-IPO round at $965 billion. Across the three rounds, GIC invested more than $4.5 billion in Anthropic, amassing a stake equal to about 1% of the company after its IPO, according to a person familiar with GIC’s financials.

Lillian Li, who leads U.S. growth investments at Baillie Gifford, began studying Anthropic and speaking with its team nearly two years before first investing in the company’s Series F. Baillie Gifford invested in the Claude maker once it became clear that the company was “executing consistently” on its vision while generating strong revenue and “compelling gross margins,” Li told TechCrunch.

After Baillie Gifford’s initial investment, Anthropic continued to deliver on its promises, expanding into industries such as legal services and life sciences, as well as broader applications like Cowork and cybersecurity, Li said.

“It’s actually quite incredible how consistent they have been with how quickly everything’s been changing,” she added. Baillie Gifford went on to invest a total of $1.5 billion across three funding rounds, a bet that has generated substantial paper gains for the investment manager, as well as for dozens of other backers who invested at what once seemed like astronomical valuations.

## 3. San Francisco is enjoying an AI boom, but worries about a crash are growing

- **Fonte:** BBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-22 21:08
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqe9k09j9jv4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss

- Published

Lauren Goldboss is busy. The owner of a small San Francisco dog-walking company, she has been hiring staff and buying vans.

Fuelling the growth is her new office dog-walking service Paw Perk, popular with some local AI start-ups.

Her business is an example of how the AI boom in San Francisco is benefiting other sectors.

The city saw an exodus of residents during the pandemic and its downtown was weakened by remote work, but it's now enjoying a rejuvenation, with AI being the major driver.

Leading AI firms OpenAI and Anthropic – which have been hiring top engineering talent, often on eye-popping salaries – are based here. Anthropic, the company behind the Claude chatbot, last week listed on New York's Nasdaq stock exchange.

Thousands of other AI start-ups have also sprung up, external in San Francisco.

Signs of the boom are evident in soaring rents and house prices, falling office vacancies, and buses and billboards plastered with cryptic AI adverts.

But what if there is a significant downward correction in AI share prices and a resulting big fall in investment in the industry? The International Monetary Fund last month became the latest organization to warn, external that this was a possibility.

Concerns have since continued due to the scandal surrounding Jensen Huang, the former boss of AI firm Nvidia, based south of San Francisco, causing some wider AI stock falls. However, Nvidia remains highly profitable, and Wednesday of this week it reported that its latest quarterly profits had doubled from a year earlier to $65bn (£49bn).

What are the thoughts of AI workers in the city, and should San Francisco – no stranger to tech's boom-and-bust cycles – be preparing for a possible crash?

One AI worker employed in the city is convinced that a crash is coming. "I am certain that the house of cards will come down… and I'm sure it will affect me," says the man who asked that we didn't use his name.

He questions whether leading US AI companies can generate enough revenues to justify their enormous spending commitments as cheaper, increasingly capable AI models emerge, especially from China.

But he isn't losing sleep over it. "Why worry about things I can't control?"

Another San Francisco-based AI worker, who also asked to remain anonymous, says that a significant market correction is "totally possible".

Yet he adds that most people don't think of it as the core risk. Instead, he says that the bigger concern in his circles, and the one he shares, is the existential risk that AI could pose to humanity.

"In some weird way, a market correction being the worst-case outcome feels great."

If there is an AI crash, San Francisco could be hit far more than Silicon Valley which lies 40 miles to its south.

That's because the AI boom is very much centred on the city itself rather than the wider San Francisco Bay Area, which includes Silicon Valley.

One study, external found that 60% of all venture capital invested in US AI firms from 2023 to 2025 went to the companies located in San Francisco, dwarfing the significantly smaller percentage given to companies in Silicon Valley.

But some factors suggest that the impact of a crash in San Francisco may be softer than some fear.

For all the investment they have attracted, AI companies are employing relatively few workers in the city compared to previous tech booms. So there may not be that many jobs to lose in a downturn.

"Hiring isn't necessarily on the scale as we would have thought with the amount of venture capital and new company formation," says Jeff Bellisario, executive director of the Bay Area Council Economic Institute, a business-affiliated think tank.

Ted Egan, the chief economist for the City and County of San Francisco, adds that "because of AI, it is a good time for tech again in the city, but we are not seeing tonnes of overall tech job growth".

Despite the expansion of the AI sector, the city remains about 35,000 jobs shy of its pre-pandemic peak, estimates Enrico Moretti, an economics professor at the University of California, Berkeley.

Instead, much of the spending by AI firms in the US is going towards data centres and computing power, relatively little of which are located in the Bay Area. San Francisco might be the brains of the AI boom, but it isn't the brawn.

Nonetheless, a significant correction could still be "very painful" for San Francisco, warns Moretti. Less AI hiring and potential layoffs would ripple through the local service sector, likely to affect jobs in everything from restaurants, to retail, to personal services like dog walking.

"It is the spillover on service jobs that would actually be the main effect," he says.

Office vacancies could also rise, reducing downtown foot traffic, while rents and house prices could weaken.

But AI isn't the only force driving San Francisco's recovery. Healthcare and tourism – which the city has been working to encourage, including by improving downtown safety and cleanliness – are also growing,, external which may help cushion against a possible AI downturn.

"Tourism is not fully back, but the growth has been healthy," says Egan, adding that it is a sign of confidence in San Francisco.

All told, Egan doesn't expect an AI crash to fundamentally alter San Francisco's economic trajectory.

"The city's economy has not been screaming during this AI boom, so I wouldn't expect it to grind to a halt if there was a slowdown in AI spending," he says. "I think of it primarily as a hiccup that we should expect, rather than a major course correction for the city's economy."

As to whether and how San Francisco's city government should prepare, there are limits to what it can do, note Bellisario and Moretti. But encouraging growth in sectors beyond technology and maintaining financial reserves could help.

For Goldboss, the dog-walking company owner, her philosophy is to "build while it is here". If the next tech bust comes – "always a reality in San Francisco" – she will try to adapt to what the market needs. "It is just the risk you kind of have to take," she says.

## 4. Why data centers are at the center of the Big Tech backlash

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-22 15:08
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/22/why-data-centers-are-at-the-center-of-the-big-tech-backlash/

In his new book “Hyperscale,” journalist Paris Marx investigates the impact of data centers and generative AI, taking readers inside the data centers themselves and explaining how computing infrastructure that goes back decades recently became a political landmine.

The book is well-timed, to put it mildly, as data center backlash seems to have been one of the motivators for the massive blue wave seen in the recent midterm elections. And those debates are likely to continue as Democrats wield more governmental power.

In an interview with TechCrunch, Marx described the data center buildout as “fundamentally unsustainable” and “completely out of line with a livable planet,” while also arguing that these facilities make for an obvious target amidst broader anger at the tech industry. After all, if a data center is being built in your community, “it’s very physical, it’s very material.”

“It doesn’t matter what side of the political spectrum that you’re on,“ Marx said. “You are going to feel the effects of that in your quality of life, in your power bill, potentially in your access to water, potentially in what you hear around your community, if these air conditioning units on the roofs of data centers are making a lot of noise.”

He also suggested that data center opposition is only the most visible piece of a wider movement targeting “the physical manifestations of the tech industry, […] whether it’s the opposition to Flock cameras rolling out across the United States, or the smart glasses that Meta is trying to put on people’s faces, or even the self-driving cars that Waymo is rolling out.”

During our conversation (which I’ve edited for length and clarity), Marx and I discussed data centers’ environmental impact, how tech companies are responding to community resistance, and what it’s actually like inside a data center.

I liked your description of trying to get access to the inside of a “hyperscale” data center and then eventually taking a tour [of one]. Could you talk a little bit more about what that experience was like? How did you get in? What surprised you when you actually got in?

Getting inside of a hyperscale data center was not the easiest task in the world. I spoke to some researchers who have been doing research on data centers for a number of years longer than I had, and they told me that before the generative AI moment, and before all the attention that has been put on data centers recently, that it was quite a bit easier to do so.

I sent emails to a number of companies asking if I could get inside, often didn’t get a response, other times got a no. I ended up, after visiting the outsides of some data centers in places like northern Virginia and Ireland and spots like that, I actually went down to New Zealand to go inside of a data center itself. And the thing that struck me most was the level of security.

Obviously, you would expect that this is an infrastructure that is really securitized, because the data inside needs to be kept safe, obviously [they] have these big fences and all the security cameras around the data centers. But to go inside, I had to send my passport in advance. Then when I got there, they had to check the passport, and then as we were going through the data center itself, every single door in the data center was locked unless you had a key card to unlock it.

Then, when you get inside of the server room itself, where all this computer hardware is stored in tall racks that look like cabinets — you can imagine, in your mind, that this would be kind of a sinister location, with some eerie lighting or something like that. But in truth, it’s just quite utilitarian. It’s a warm room with some air conditioning blasting into the servers from vents that are nearby.

It was really loud as well, because you have all these fans running from all these servers, it was really kind of piercing. To be inside of this room, if you were going to be in there for a while, you’d probably want earplugs or something like that.

Before I read your book, I thought of data centers as something that’s important, but a little bit boring. How did you decide they were interesting and rich enough to sustain an entire book?

I felt the same. I was aware that data centers had been out there, but it was not something that I was paying particularly close attention to. But then there were a number of stories that tipped me off to the fact that there might be more here, and that it was worth looking deeper into what was going on — the first being hearing about the water pressures created by data centers in Chile around the capital of Santiago, where companies like Google and Microsoft were building data centers, and communities were concerned about their access to water and started organizing around that.

And then hearing about how much electricity data centers were consuming in Ireland, to the point that now they consume more than 20% of the country’s electricity, and that was obviously pushing up power prices. It was putting pressures on the grid, such that the grid operator actually put a moratorium in place for grid connections for data centers around the Dublin area, back in 2022. This was even before the generative AI moment that caused a surge in construction of data centers.

And then the other one was in The Dalles, Oregon, where Google had actually built its first company-owned data centers about 20 years back. In that case, its water deal was coming up for renewal. It wanted to build more data centers there, so it needed more water. And when the residents started asking, “How much water is Google actually using?” a journalist at The Oregonian went to the city and essentially asked, “Hey, how much water is Google using? Our readers would like to know this,” he was hit with a lawsuit instead of being given that information. Because Google did not want it to be public. And then, of course, when it did come out about a year later, Google revealed that it was using 29% of the city’s water, which seemed quite significant.

That was around when ChatGPT was being released and generative AI was starting to take off. It felt like, okay, there are these pockets of places where a lot of data centers have already been constructed, where they are putting pressures on the communities that are causing people to respond, and it felt like that was going to start coming to a lot more communities. It was a good time to really start to dig in and pay attention.

You don’t just talk about the data centers’ environmental impact, but also, they [become] your way in to talk about this broader tech moment. They represent to you — particularly the hyperscale data centers — how these tech companies are doing business now.

Absolutely. The hyperscale data centers and just the scale of the construction — whether it’s the the size of the actual facilities themselves or the number that these companies are trying to build out, just feels fundamentally unsustainable.

They’re just demanding too much from the communities where they’re being located, whether that’s because of electricity or because of water use or what have you, or just the fact that they’re building out so many, that when you think about the cumulative impact and you look at the demand for water, but particularly for energy from fossil fuels, that means that it’s just completely out of line with a livable planet and an amount of computation that feels like it makes any degree of sense for us to have constructed.

I think it really goes back to the hubris of the tech industry and of these major tech companies and the tech billionaires that run them, and the fact that they have these particular visions of the future that are based on fantasies, but also on expanding their own power and influence over the world, and they don’t seem to mind that everyone else then has to pay the cost that comes with their ambitions and what they are seeking to do.

And I feel like it’s become particularly egregious with generative AI and the data centers to power them, where many of the benefits that were supposed to come of generative AI do not appear to have materialized — whether those are the economic benefits, the productivity benefits, but also the benefits of delivery of services like healthcare and education to the public, as we heard Sam Altman saying a lot in the early days of ChatGPT.

Paired with that, there has been this massive environmental cost […] but there’s also this massive and growing social cost, when you think about the mental health impacts of chatbot dependence, of all the false information that pervades social media platforms now, of the deepfake crisis, and I could go on and on and on.

It’s not surprising that there has been backlash. But I have been surprised at how widespread it’s been, and also how bipartisan — you talk about Ron DeSantis and Bernie Sanders both being opposed to data centers. Why do you think it’s become such a unifying issue?

Because it’s not an abstract issue, right? I think even when we think about generative AI or social media or something like that, [it] can feel abstract. It’s a service that you access from your phone or from your computer. It’s not something that is tangible in the world.

But when a data center is coming to set up shop in your community, it’s very physical, it’s very material. It doesn’t matter what side of the political spectrum that you’re on. You are going to feel the effects of that in your quality of life, in your power bill, potentially in your access to water, potentially in what you hear around your community, if these air conditioning units on the roofs of data centers are making a lot of noise.

I think that there was already a pre-existing frustration with the tech industry that came from the quality of the products, the effects that they’re having on our society and our lives. For some people, [they have] frustration with the way that the tech industry has been collaborating with the Trump administration and with right-wing political groups in different parts of the world. But then you add on top of that the actual effects that come of having one of these infrastructures be planned or located in your community, and that anger gets kind of directed into that physical manifestation of this thing that you have an issue with.

Obviously, the data centers are at the forefront of this, and it’s what we pay the most attention to. But we also see this increasingly with other aspects of the tech industry, whether it’s the opposition to Flock cameras rolling out across the United States, or the smart glasses that Meta is trying to put on people’s faces, or even the self-driving cars that Waymo is rolling out.

There’s a lot of frustration with the physical manifestations of the tech industry, because that is something that can really be felt more directly, but can also be challenged more directly. You can delete an app on your phone, but what difference does that really make to the company? It doesn’t often seem like very much.

The tech companies have reacted to this backlash in a few different ways. One is that Microsoft, Amazon, and OpenAI have all talked about [their commitment] that they’re not going to use NDAs anymore in their negotiations with local governments. How big a difference do you think that will make?

Not a huge difference.

I do think it’s going to be good that it will be easier for people to access information on these projects that are being located in their communities. But part of the reason why we’re seeing these shifts is exactly in response to that opposition.

We’re seeing the tech companies propose to pay for more of the electricity infrastructure that will be required for their data centers to reduce the pressure on ratepayers, because people are angry about that. They’re promising to use different water-based cooling systems in their data centers, to say that they’re able to use less electricity.

When we look at the NDAs and the decision or the announcement that they’re not going to use those anymore, I think it’s also because it’s working against them. People know what a data center is now, and if people find out that a data center is coming to town and they have questions and they’re not getting answers, that’s only going to further fuel the opposition to those data centers.

So, it’s not really a surprise to see [the tech companies] do this, and it’s a way that they can get a positive PR thing by saying, “We’re not going to use these NDAs that are working against us anyway.“

One of the other ways they’ve responded is by saying that there’s a lot of misinformation about the environmental harms, or that they’re being overstated, or that they can mitigate them with renewable technology. How valid do you think those arguments are?

I think that the industry recognizes that there is a lot of concern and they’re trying to basically move into the discussion with narratives of their own to have people question whether data centers are really a problem or not.

I feel like we don’t see the industry doing as much on the energy side anymore, particularly on the fossil fuel side, because they realize that they have kind of lost that argument. If you look back a year and a half, two years ago, they were talking a lot about renewables, about nuclear energy, you don’t hear so much of that discourse anymore.

They have pivoted, instead, to focus on the water question, where I think that they feel that they can use more abstract national figures in order to confuse the public about what’s going on. They can say, “If you look nationally, agriculture and golf courses also use a lot of water. So you don’t need to be worried about a data center coming to town because they don’t use as much, in aggregate.”

But the issue with data center water use is not a national issue. It’s a local issue. There are unquestionably going to be communities where they seek to locate data centers that are going to have plenty of water and that are not going to be concerned about a data center coming to town. But they’re also locating [data centers] in a lot of communities that are experiencing high degrees of water stress, which means that they don’t have the water to really provide to these infrastructures.

We have also seen them pivoting to move away from the evaporative cooling systems, the ones that require a lot of water — because you need to take in a certain amount of water as it gets too hot, it evaporates away, and so you can see why you need a consistent flow of water into the data center for that type of cooling system — to a closed loop system, where you basically have a certain amount of water, it stays within the data center, you keep cooling it down by using electricity to do that, and so you clearly don’t need as much water.

That is their new way to say, “Hey, don’t worry about the water use. We’re going to switch to this other system.” But the issue there, of course, is that it does require more electricity in order to keep that water cooled down, and that is often not part of what they talk about or discuss when they are proposing that as an alternative.

Then the other thing is that it’s not just water that is in those pipes or tubes going through the data center. There’s also a lot of coolant in there, a lot of chemicals, and communities where these projects are being located with closed loop cooling systems are becoming increasingly concerned about those coolants leaking out into their water supply and what that is going to mean.

In the book, you say repeatedly that you’re not against technology itself or even data centers in general, but [against] this hyperscale approach. The book is called “Hyperscale.” The last chapter is called “Human Scale.” Why do you see scale as such an important part of the problem?

Because scale is what creates the pressures, whether those are the pressures on the communities or you know the pressures on our wider electricity system and whatnot.

I don’t think that the inherent problem is data centers, because we have had data centers for a long time, and sure, there have been occasional concerns about specific projects, but data centers as a whole have not been an issue that the public has felt merited this degree of attention. So that is why I feel the hyperscale data center, and particularly the acceleration that we’ve seen in the expansion of the size of these hyperscale data centers, but also the number of data centers that these companies are building out, is notable in this moment.

Linked to that is not just the scale of the demand created by these hyperscale data centers, but also, what they are supposed to be powering? That is where the other disconnect lies. They’re demanding a lot, but if we were actually getting something good for it, this might be justified. I think that for a lot of people, and I feel like we see this reflected in polling, they’re not actually seeing the benefits from generative AI.

So then the question becomes: “Okay, we’re expending all of these resources, we’re pushing up emissions such that climate change is going to be accelerated just to build out all these data centers to meet the ambitions of generative AI that the tech companies have, but then we’re not actually getting much benefit out of that technology. So what are we actually doing here?”

One other thing that really struck a chord with me was your description about how tech executives often talk about the future as if there’s [just one] inevitable future. The only thing that’s in question is how quickly we arrive there [and] who’s going to make the most money off of it.

This is so important for me, because I do feel like how we think about the future then shapes decisions that we make. The tech billionaires, they see the future in a certain way […] and I think that they have been very successful in getting us to buy into their vision of the future for the past several decades. But that does not mean that that is the only way that the future has to be.

We can imagine a very different kind of future, and that would mean that we place our resources in different places and actually develop technology in a different way, toward different outcomes, with different incentives driving that work.

There are interesting conversations happening in different parts of the world right now around things like digital sovereignty, getting off of these technologies from the U.S. tech companies. But in some cases, those conversations are quickly captured by industry in those different parts of the world to say, “Okay, we want to get off U.S. technology, but let’s have our local companies do effectively the same thing, and just let them do the same exploitative practices and make the money that the U.S. tech companies would be making.” And I don’t think that that is the right way forward.

I also think that there is a role for public sector development of technology in a way that we’ve eschewed or even looked at skeptically for the past number of decades, because the idea was that technology had to be left to the private sector. What we’re starting to see in Europe is a bit more funding of public technology, in order to develop the services that government itself depends on, and I think that that is a good first step.

But especially when we’re looking at the Microsoft outage that happened just recently, and we’re looking at Google threatening schools with their access to Google search if they don’t accept Google AI tools, I think that there’s a real moment right now to put resources into developing something different, that doesn’t come from these major tech companies, and that hopefully can have much more public benefit than what these companies now provide us.
