## 1. Anthropic reports $17 billion in Q3 revenue, purchase commitments of $895.4 billion in revised S-1

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-02 14:52
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 9.5
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/02/anthropic-ant-s-1-quarter-financials.html

Anthropic reported updated financials as part of an amended Form S-1 filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission on Monday, giving investors a more comprehensive look into the state of its business.

The artificial intelligence company said it generated $33.24 billion in revenue for the nine months ended Sept. 30, up roughly 1,200% from the same period last year. It reported a $104 billion net loss during the period, though most of that was due to the change in fair value of convertible notes and derivative liabilities. Anthropic had a loss of $25.1 billion over the same stretch in 2025.

Anthropic unveiled its highly anticipated prospectus last week, bringing the company one step closer to a potentially historic initial public offering. The initial filing only included the Claude maker's financials through the first half of the year.

During its third quarter, Anthropic generated roughly $17 billion in revenue, up about 1,127% from the same period last year. Revenue growth slowed on a quarter-to-quarter basis, however. Third-quarter revenue increased around 47%, a steep drop from 144% growth in Q2 and 132% in Q1.

The company reported a net loss of $23.29 billion during its third quarter.

As the 5-year-old company prepares to kick off its roadshow, it will have to reckon with growing market jitters around the AI buildout. Wall Street has been worried that rising interest rates will make debt unaffordable for many data center developers and neoclouds, and quarterly reports from Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon last week added fuel to those concerns.

The hyperscalers' aggressive AI spending is eating into their cash flows, causing some investors to worry that computing capacity may not come online quickly enough to meet Anthropic's demands. Anthropic gets much of its capacity from Amazon, Google and SpaceX, but it also relies on neoclouds for some compute as it races to expand as quickly as possible.

In the amended S-1, Anthropic said it has entered into purchase commitments and lease agreements with an aggregate committed spend of about $895.4 billion, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure."

Anthropic said its compute and infrastructure expenses increased by $14.4 billion for the nine months ended Sept. 30, up 329% compared with the same period last year.

"As our compute and infrastructure capacity grows, the associated compute and infrastructure expenses have increased," Anthropic said in the filing.

The company's CEO, Dario Amodei, co-founded Anthropic alongside a handful of other researchers who defected from OpenAI, now its chief rival. OpenAI confidentially filed its prospectus with the SEC in June, but its executives have signaled that it's aiming for a 2027 debut.

Anthropic has worked to position itself as more safety-conscious than its competitors, and its S-1 includes more than 90 pages of risk factors. The company highlights risks, including potential political headwinds, its complex infrastructure needs as well as the difficulties that can come from balancing safety and commercial objectives.

"We consider it our personal responsibility to help the world understand these risks, to prevent or mitigate them when they arise from our AI models, and to work with governments and the rest of the industry to address them at a societal level," Anthropic's co-founders said in the filing.

## 7. AI-powered malware ‘highly likely’ to be used to hack UK infrastructure by 2028

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-02 21:01
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/03/ai-powered-malware-artificial-intelligence-hacking-critical-uk-infrastructure

AI-powered malicious software is “highly likely” to be used in hacking attacks against the UK’s power stations, airports and hospitals by 2028, according to the state cybersecurity body.

The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) said AI will probably enable a ransomware attack against critical national infrastructure [CNI] between now and 2028. The UK’s definition of CNI covers a range of facilities and services across sectors including energy, transport, defence, food, health and finance.

The warning from the NCSC, part of the GCHQ spy agency, was published in the organisation’s annual review, which also warned that Russia and China will start using AI “routinely” in cyber-attacks.

“It is … highly likely that between now and 2028, AI-powered malware will have been used in a ransomware attack on UK CNI,” said NCSC.

Ransomware has become a cornerstone of the criminal hacker’s repertoire and is a signature ploy of gangs that typically operate out of former Soviet Union states.

After gaining access to a victim’s IT systems, the attackers then encrypt files with bespoke malicious software – or malware – and then demand a payment in cryptocurrency to unlock the crippled systems and return any stolen data. UK ransomware victims include Royal Mail, outsourcing firm Capita and various public bodies, including councils.

AI is set to enhance the ransomware threat by helping attackers with tasks such as scouting targets, locating weaknesses in IT networks and writing malicious code, said the NCSC.

Last month, the government urged Britons to stock up to nine litres of bottled water and three days’ worth of food in order to ensure that households can survive a weather, cyber or other serious emergency. Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, said as the Be Ready campaign was launched that Britain needed to prepare for the threat of “state-sponsored terrorism” by Russia or other hostile powers targeting everyday infrastructure.

NCSC’s chief executive, Richard Horne, will warn the public in a speech accompanying the review on Tuesday that AI is going to unleash a wave of cyber-attacks imminently.

“In the coming months, we face a real prospect of an unprecedented rise in the number of cyber incidents,” he will say.

Horne will attribute the threat to the technology’s ability to uncover weaknesses in IT systems that have been “tolerated for far too long”.

An example of AI’s capacity to root out previously undetected vulnerabilities came last month, when the technology helped discover a critical weakness in PHP, a computer language used by almost 70% of the world’s websites.

Alan Woodward, a professor of cybersecurity at Surrey University, said NCSC’s warning underlined the need for nation states and companies to access cutting-edge AI technology.

“AI can help people find these flaws and patch them, but if we don’t get the access the bad guys will beat them to it,” he said. So far, leading AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have given some companies advanced access to their most powerful models in order to help them identify serious IT glitches.

Horne will also say in his speech that communications networks used by the British military are subjected to daily attacks that are “persistent” and “sustained”.

Horne will say Russia was one of the perpetrators, but the attacks have deliberately not crossed the line into warfare, with Moscow carrying out “notable campaigns” that “intentionally fall below the threshold of armed conflict as they escalate their attempts at hybrid warfare”.

Horne will add that recent cyber-attacks on supermarket fridges in Europe, which have seen fridge temperatures raised by hackers to more than 50C, are a warning to companies and organisations to invest strongly in cyber resilience.

“For decades we have collectively got away with tech on the cheap,” he will say.

## 8. Lumentum CEO touts 'enormous' opportunity in massive AI connectivity deal with Marvell

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology

- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/02/marvell-lumentum-ai-deal.html

Marvell Technology's deal for optical components maker Lumentum will create an "enormous" opportunity to address growing connectivity demands from the artificial intelligence infrastructure buildout, Lumentum CEO Michael Hurlston told CNBC on Monday.

"You need scale-up technology that needs semiconductors and optical working seamlessly together," Marvell CEO Matt Murphy told CNBC's "Squawk on the Street." "We are going to create a new category of company here."

Since the tie-up is a fixed-ratio deal with no cash, the value will depend on the price of Marvell shares. The company said the transaction has an implied enterprise value of $115 billion for Lumentum, in a release.

Marvell shares slumped 13% in trading Monday, while Lumentum's stock sank nearly 19%. But both stocks have been huge winners in the artificial intelligence boom, with Marvell more than doubling in the past year, prior to Monday's sell-off, and Lumentum quadrupling over that stretch.

Through the acquisition, Marvell said it can combine electrical and optical technologies to meet accelerating and shifting AI connectivity demands across regions and systems. Companies are increasingly relying on optical connections to piece together chips used to run and train large language models.

"We're actually going to create solutions around those that are highly sticky, and the reason that they're sold out is because the demand is exploding, and we don't see that changing anytime soon," Murphy said.

Lumentum shareholders will receive 4.5 Marvell shares for each Lumentum share under the agreement. The deal is expected to close during the first half of 2028.

Marvell's takeover marks one of the most significant bets on AI in 2026 and comes despite recent concerns over the long-term sustainability of infrastructure spending.

This isn't the company's first bet on AI growth. This year, the company closed a multibillion-dollar deal for Celestial AI, a startup focused on optical interconnectivity technology.

Marvell's Murphy also shot down concerns about Nvidia's circular dealmaking, given the leading AI chipmaker's recent multibillion-dollar investments in both companies.

In a bid to further support the data center infrastructure that houses its AI chips, Nvidia has positioned itself as a central cog in a network of related companies, investing in several technology companies that purchase its chips or neoclouds that rent out computing capacity. That's sparked criticism that companies like Nvidia are virtually funding themselves by taking stakes in customers.

"This is a double down on supporting Nvidia, who is the ecosystem leader in this area," Murphy told CNBC. "We're very committed to that partnership, and I think having two of the key suppliers come together, that support that ecosystem, is going to be a big benefit for them."

Marvell has seen its market value more than triple over the last three years, buoyed by unrelenting AI enthusiasm. Lumentum's stock has more than doubled in 2026 alone.

Here’s a question straight out of sci-fi: What if AI could predict what you were going to do before you decided to, even in a completely new situation?

Israeli startup 42AI says it is developing a “behavioral world model” that can do just that, and it has just come out of stealth with $62.5 million in seed funding. The company is initially running its predictive system at a handful of financial brokerages, where it says the model can help predict how individual traders will respond to volatile conditions.

“Behavior is not deterministic; it’s constantly probabilistic,” 42AI’s CEO and co-founder Amit Gal-Or told TechCrunch when I asked him to justify his company’s tall claim. He argues that people have underlying psychological tendencies that influence their decisions even when their circumstances change, and that’s what the startup is trying to measure and convert into data that the model can understand.

“A week from now, you might be a different person, but your tendencies will always play out in certain areas to a probability or distribution,” his co-founder and CTO Ehud Ezra added. And that, the company argues, is what the model does: show the probability of a person making a certain decision.

42AI says the model tries to figure out people’s internal motivations instead of extrapolating patterns based on their previous actions. To do that, 42AI examines how people react to seemingly unrelated events. For an investor or trader, for example, such events might include completing KYC processes, onboarding, support calls, money transfers, or trading stocks.

“When the person is talking to support or sales in the initial process […] we’re looking at the sentiment, we’re looking at the voice, we’re looking at a lot of interactions that they’re doing, how many times do they call, and so forth,” added Ezra. “And all of those data points are going into the model. Later on, you have the interactions: how you buy and sell, and when, and we’re looking at the market at that stage.”

“So we’re trying to look at how they’re looking at the world. In a world model, every individual is the world,” he added.

The company then converts those observations into mathematical representations of traits, which are then fed into a transformer-based world model that lays out probabilities for possible decisions under different circumstances. (The company uses a separate language model to generate explanations for its predictions.)

Ezra said the model is trained to preserve a person’s underlying tendencies during simulations. So if someone is usually risk-averse, but the model suddenly treats them as a risk-taker, “the target is off,” he said, explaining how the model’s training rewards are structured.

42AI spent three years developing this approach, combining psychological research with neuroscience and genetics, as well as advice from experts in psychology and PTSD, and Dan Shechtman, who was awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Gal-Or said the team first studied thousands of people via psych assessments and behavioral scenarios, before adding government and biobank datasets covering more than 2 million people. Its customer datasets have since expanded to cover almost 30 million people, he said.

In live deployments, the model analyzes customers’ existing records, including transactions and support calls. Gal-Or pointed out the system doesn’t need personally identifiable information, arguing that “the model never knows who the person is.” However, it seems the company still creates individual behavioral profiles from linked customer activity.

In predicting market trades, 42AI claims its model achieves 98% accuracy, but Gal-Or clarified that this is more a measure of whether trading flows fall within a predicted range, not if each trade was predicted correctly.

He also said the model consistently achieves an F1 score of 0.8 to 0.85 for predicting trading direction and volume. (F1 score is a metric in machine learning that measures how well a classification model can balance correct predictions against false alarms and missed events.)

And it’s not like the model can work everywhere, given it’s still a machine in a box. “There’s a natural ceiling because you really don’t have access to everything that a person experiences,” Gal-Or said. Ezra explained that the system’s accuracy also suffers when it misses important situational context, like if someone chooses to change their trading choices based on advice from a friend or a colleague.

Gal-Or said 42AI is working with 10 large brokerages, who are using it to manage liquidity, margin requirements, and hedging exposure. The company is onboarding more customers, he said, and is generating more than “seven figures” in revenue, though he wouldn’t specify a number.

Customers pay the startup a flat fee, or a percentage of the value the platform helps them gain. He claimed one brokerage generated approximately $30 million in additional value through dynamic hedging.

The $62.5 million seed funding came from the founders and Infinity Group over three years, and wasn’t entirely raised from external investors, Gal-Or said. Infinity was founded by his father, Amir Gal-Or, who is also 42AI’s chairman and a co-founder. 42AI now plans to raise more money.

Beyond finance, 42AI is now exploring applications for its model in gaming, fraud prevention, and cybersecurity, and one day wants to use it to help robots and AI agents understand people. But that may be a ways off yet: Ezra acknowledged that the model needs to be trained on rules and context for each new domain.

And like with all AI tools, there’s a potentially troubling side to 42AI’s technology: If a brokerage can anticipate what its customers may do, what’s to stop someone from using that knowledge to their own benefit? Ezra admitted that this was “a valid concern” when I raised it, and the founders pointed to regulations, monitoring, and an internal ethics committee, though they didn’t explain what, if anything, would stop their customers from using the technology to gain an advantage over the very people it’s profiling.

Amit Gal-Or, however, was confident that the technology could help address such risks. “We are very proud that actually we are part of the solution; we are the solution to this problem,” he said.

## 12. Trajectory raises $45M to make AI model training accessible to nontechnical experts

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-02 20:13
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.5
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/02/trajectory-raises-45m-to-make-ai-model-training-accessible-to-nontechnical-experts/

Whether developed by OpenAI or Anthropic or built on open source alternatives, current AI models share a critical limitation: They don’t learn from their mistakes without further training.

Businesses adapting these models for specialized fields, such as law and finance, typically get around this by fine-tuning general models. However, that often requires hiring internal research teams or relying on expensive consultants, commonly known as forward-deployed engineers (FDEs).

Trajectory, a startup founded about a year ago by three Stanford classmates with experience at Google DeepMind and Apple, wants to automate that process, allowing AI models to learn from feedback and continuously improve at specialized tasks.

The 18-person company just announced that it raised $45 million in a Series A round at a $300 million valuation, led by Sequoia, with participation from Nvidia, Bessemer Venture Partners, and Bain Capital Ventures.

Trajectory co-founder Ronak Malde compared foundational models to a very smart PhD student who’s always starting a new job. “But what the economy values is someone who worked on the job for 30 years,” he told TechCrunch.

The startup aims to solve this problem through an approach known as continual learning. Businesses using Trajectory can start with an open source AI model and connect the platform to their applications, allowing it to collect feedback on how the model performs. Trajectory then uses that information to retrain the model, helping it improve at specialized tasks over time.

With Trajectory, lawyers, doctors, and other specialists can improve the models near automatically themselves, Malde said. That capability could make deploying specialized AI faster and cheaper.

Companies like Harvey and Rogo are already using Trajectory’s platform to post-train open source models, helping them perform more like experienced lawyers or financial analysts by learning from previous tasks, such as reviewing a particular type of legal contract or valuing a company using a specific methodology. Trajectory’s other customers include Decagon, Mercor, and Nvidia. The startup is now making its post-training platform publicly available.

The people using Trajectory decide “what good looks like for their organization and can turn that into improvements for their model,” Malde said.

Trajectory’s approach to continual learning shares some similarities with recursive self-improvement (RSI), the idea that AI systems can improve their own capabilities. But co-founder Michael Elabd argues that keeping humans involved in the training process makes Trajectory’s approach safer, since autonomous self-improvement could lead to unintended or even dangerous outcomes.

Trajectory isn’t the only startup trying to automate post-training. Other companies going after this market include Thinking Machines Lab, founded by former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati and the company behind the fine-tuning platform Tinker, Applied Compute, which was valued at $3.25 billion last month, and Prime Intellect, a platform that helps companies build and train their own AI agents.

Malde believes the startup’s biggest advantage is its team. He was among the handful of researchers Google DeepMind acqui-hired from Windsurf. Elabd also worked at DeepMind, while Trajectory’s third co-founder, Arjun Karanam, previously worked on generative foundation models at Apple. The startup has also recruited researchers and product designers from OpenAI, Stripe, and Netflix.

Why does design matter so much to a company focused on AI training? Karanam says the startup has developed an interface that makes post-training more accessible to its nontechnical customers.

## 14. Omen AI adds liquid know-how in $16 million Aquatic Labs acquisition

U.S. data centers use 17 to 19 billion gallons of water to cool their hot-running chips each year — and that number is expected to triple in the next five years, according to The Water Foundation.

That’s an expensive and politically tricky proposition for investors throwing billions of dollars at the challenge of scaling up AI. One way to use less water is to do a better job monitoring the cooling fluids, a task that currently involves sending samples away to a laboratory for tests in a multi-day process.

Omen AI was founded in 2024 to solve that problem. The company is building a real-time sensor to determine whether valves were failing, or if bacteria growth in the system threatened to choke off water flow. After raising a $31 million Series A in June, Omen is now acquiring competitor Aquatic Labs in a $16 million cash-and-stock deal.

The deal came together after Omen founder Zach Laberge was introduced to Aquatic Labs’ CEO Allan Adams by an investor in the filtration space. Both startups were building real-time water sensors with complementary technology: Omen’s sensors looked for elements in the water, while the Aquatic Labs sensor tests its alkalinity.

Putting those two technologies in the same sensor creates a “much richer picture of the water and the fluid,” Adams told TechCrunch, while uniting their vision of automating what had previously been a slow, manual process.

The combination unites two classic species of founder: Laberge dropped out of high school to pursue his first company, which did similar fluid analysis for large construction machines that depend on hydraulics. Adams was an academic, trained as a physicist, who spent much of his career developing advanced ocean sensing technology as an MIT professor and Woods Hole Oceanographic Institute researcher before starting his company.

Now, with Adams as the company’s chief science officer, the combined teams are working to integrate both technologies into one sensor, which they expect to have on the market by early next year. Omen’s initial sensors will be monitoring chips pulling in 250 to 300 MW of power by the end of the year, worth about $5 to $7 billion, Laberge told TechCrunch, with the hope of being installed on the equivalent of 1 GW of chips by the end of 2027.

The added value is in uptime: If the fluid system gets out of whack, data center operators typically choose to dump the water rather than risk damage to their expensive equipment. That means shutting down operations until the fluid system is restored, which costs a lot of money. Monitoring lets data center operators use the same fluid for longer, and minimize the costs of a clean up.

Adams hopes to see their technologies have an impact outside of data centers as well, telling TechCrunch that “water goes into data centers, and then it comes out and gets dumped into municipal water, and you want that whole process to happen in a way that’s as safe and responsible as possible — and that means you got to measure stuff.”

AI companies once estimated as much as $4 trillion would be spent on AI infrastructure by 2030, but some investors have grown skeptical of the buildout, sending hyperscaler stocks plummeting in recent days. Omen’s team isn’t worried about shifting sentiment though, since their work comes after a data center has been built.

“We’re working customers on stuff they’re building today or have already built, and so it doesn’t really affect us because all of those projects were financed 12 months ago,” Laberge said. “Our view is, if there’s tightness, this is still going to be the biggest market in human history, even if you sliced it in half.”
