Anthropic is asking a federal court in Washington, D.C., to rehear its appeal in a case concerning the Pentagon's blacklisting of the company's AI models.

"The Secretary of War branded Anthropic — an American AI company whose models help defend the nation— a national-security 'supply chain risk' because it declined to permit its models to be used for mass surveillance of Americans or lethal autonomous warfare and publicly explained those AI-safety commitments," Anthropic attorneys wrote in the petition filed on Tuesday. "A divided panel upheld that extraordinary designation."

The Defense Department, led by Pete Hegseth, applied the supply chain risk label in March, a move that blocked defense contractors from using the company's technology in their work with the agency. That came after a breakdown in negotiations about how the military could use Anthropic's Claude AI models.

Anthropic sued the Trump administration in an effort to undo that action. In September, the D.C. Circuit upheld the Pentagon's blacklisting. The 2-1 decision rejected Anthropic's argument that the Department of Defense's ban on its Claude models was arbitrary, unauthorized and unconstitutional.

Anthropic's attorneys wrote in the Tuesday filing that the court ignored "straightforward evidence the designation was retaliation for Anthropic's speech about AI safety."

In a parallel case, a San Francisco federal judge ruled in August that the DOD had violated Anthropic's First Amendment rights, and that its supply chain risk designation amounted to illegal retaliation.

Before the clash between the two sides spilled into the public's view earlier this year, Anthropic served as an early partner across many U.S. agencies, including the DOD. Anthropic signed a $200 million contract with the Pentagon in July of 2025, but as the company began negotiating Claude's deployment on the DOD's GenAI.mil AI platform that September, talks collapsed.

A representative from Anthropic declined to comment beyond the filing. A Pentagon spokesperson told CNBC in an email that the agency doesn't comment on pending or ongoing litigation.

This is the first time several US states have banded together in a coordinated legal action against an AI company.

Drew Pusateri, a spokesperson for OpenAI, said the company had “built industry-leading age-specific protections into our product that many other AI products lack”.

“While no system can be perfect, we’ll continue to work with experts to inform our work and build additional safeguards, such as the parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections that exist today,” Pusateri continued. “We’re committed to working with states on advancing pragmatic AI policies that apply to the entire AI industry – not just one company.”

Dozens of cases have also been filed against OpenAI over ChatGPT’s alleged role in mass shootings. A criminal investigation and civil lawsuit, brought by the attorney general of Florida, accuses OpenAI of concealing safety risks after a mass shooting at Florida State University. The company has also been sued by the families of 37 victims of the Tumbler Ridge mass shooting in Canada, who allege ChatGPT induced the shooter to carry out the attack.

The eight states that filed suit on Wednesday say there is a growing body of research that allegedly shows ChatGPT can harm critical-thinking skills, encourage paranoia and delusions, offer dangerous medical advice and promote unhealthy behavior.

The lawsuits also note that the recent examples of OpenAI’s models going rogue, with its AI agents hacking into other companies and government entities, show that the technology is prone to deceiving human users.

Pusateri said OpenAI had strengthened its safeguards in regards to those episodes and would “continue sharing information about the extensive steps we’ve taken in response to incidents of misalignment”.

The eight states are suing OpenAI independently in their state courts and include Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana.

A Saudi national who worked for OpenAI appeared in court this week for allegedly planting a secret recording device behind a TV in one of the tech giant's meeting rooms.

Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old engineer, was arrested by the FBI and charged with using or attempting to use a device "to intercept oral communications".

As yet, it is not clear what he was planning to do with the recordings.

But, here's we do know so far.

Help from a chatbot

The engineer asked AI to help him listen in to discussions about AI, according to an official complaint (the document filed in court).

Between 17 August and 16 September, Alfozan had reportedly asked a productivity AI assistant several questions about meeting rooms and their availability.

Some detailed in the complaint include "how about external partners, where would they usually meet with OpenAI at" and "can you list today's meeting in this room".

The complaint also claimed that in September, Alfozan used a chatbot associated with his spouse to ask how to build and install a recording device and how to access it remotely.

Alfozan had worked at the firm for around a year before he allegedly made his attempt to plant a recorder.

The room where the device was found was regularly used to hold meetings to discuss "highly confidential technical, commercial and strategic information" as well as OpenAI's "government engagements and commercial relationships", the complaint stated.

Alfozan had no authority to access or be involved with meetings in the room, the complaint states.

But CCTV footage recorded him near the conference room on eight separate occasions in September.

Hiding in plain toilet paper

On 30 September, OpenAI staff discovered a "surreptitious audio recording device" behind a television - which had been wrapped in toilet paper.

A camera was placed in the conference room and on 5 October, Alfozan was filmed entering the room and appeared to look behind the television where the device had been inserted.

The complaint also stated that same day he allegedly left an unattended bag in a different room.

When security personnel opened it, it was found to contain "another recording device" similar to the one discovered, as well as Alfozan's wallet and ID.

In a post on X, external, OpenAI wrote it had used its own AI models to "understand the device and where it had come from".

It also said: "At this time, we have no indications of this individual inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights, source code, or customer data."

Who is Abdulrahman Alfozan?

Alfozan had been employed as an engineer by OpenAI since July 2025. The complaint said he was educated in the US and has been a legal permanent resident since 2020.

He was arrested by the FBI on 9 October and his contract with OpenAI has been terminated.

Alfozan's lawyer William Pernik told the BBC: "Like countless Americans, Mr Alfozan came to this country years ago as a young person with a dream.

"These allegations appear wholly out of character for him, and we will relentlessly investigate all evidence produced by the government in this case to understand what actually happened, who was responsible and any possible reasons why."

Pernik added: "In the meantime, we respectfully ask everyone to withhold judgment until full facts become known. Like every one of us, Mr Alfozan is presumed innocent, and what has been made public is an accusation, not a finding of guilt."

On Tuesday, Alfozan made his initial appearance in a federal court in California. A detention hearing is scheduled on 19 October to determine whether he should remain in custody while proceedings continue.

The FBI investigation is ongoing.

If convicted, Alfozan faces a maximum penalty of five years in prison and a $250,000 (£190,000) fine.

## 6. SentinelOne cuts malware analysis from weeks to hours

Hours to reverse-engineer malware, down from 4–6 weeks

Reverse engineering a sophisticated piece of malware can take a top security researcher four to six weeks. It’s painstaking work where researchers pull apart software to understand how it behaves, often retracing their steps as they uncover new information. Gregor Stewart, Chief AI Officer at SentinelOne, says OpenAI Daybreak can now produce human-level reverse-engineering output in roughly eight hours.

By reducing the time needed for deep analysis, OpenAI gives SentinelOne’s researchers room to investigate more malware and connect findings across samples. That wider view often reveals relationships that isolated investigations might miss. Stewart describes the opportunity as “a kind of intelligence that we were never able to afford before.”

“Something that would’ve taken a top-flight researcher four or six weeks to do, AI is now doing at human-level output in something like eight hours.”

Daybreak gives defenders access to OpenAI models for cybersecurity work. SentinelOne uses these capabilities to reverse engineer malware, examining software to understand how it behaves. Faster analysis gives its researchers more capacity to investigate additional samples.

Stewart emphasizes that his team remains responsible for the outcome. “The only thing that can’t be automated is accountability,” he says. “Take a decision to change the architecture. This is something that still you would want human eyes on, whereas point fixes or things that don’t fundamentally change the way the software works, those might be better suited for automation,” he adds.

Historically, research teams at SentinelOne had to reserve deep investigation for the most interesting or important threats they uncovered. “We were only able to reverse very small numbers of pieces of software,” Stewart says. With fewer samples to compare, they had fewer opportunities to spot connections between threats.

“Faster reverse engineering enables us to map a much larger part of the malware landscape and keep pace with how it changes. That gives our researchers a clearer picture of how threats are connected and how those relationships evolve.”

Faster analysis with Daybreak gives researchers more opportunities to compare malware samples and look for shared components. For example, a component found in two apparently unrelated samples could give researchers a lead to pursue during a customer investigation.

Comparing findings across more samples also reveals broader relationships between threats. Seeing those relationships, Stewart says, “would help you defend further out, and be more proactive about your defense.”

Within SentinelOne, Stewart says access to OpenAI’s cyber capabilities is allowing more people to take responsibility for security work. “Almost everyone now has access to the kind of expertise that was in very short supply until maybe even a few months ago,” he says. More team members can investigate issues themselves rather than relying on a small group of specialists.

Stewart wants to bring OpenAI’s cyber capabilities into code creation, so SentinelOne developers can address security issues as they build.

“OpenAI is shifting the advantage to defenders by providing expert level cyber capability to everyone in the development team.”

The $5.5tn (£4.1tn) US chipmaker Nvidia is donating $250m worth of computing power to the UK to support AI safety research.

The donation from the world’s most valuable company comes as the UK government confirmed its AI Security Institute (AISI), which tests advance AI models, will be able to examine cutting-edge technology developed by companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google before they are released.

Nvidia said the donation, which spans five years, would “support agentic safety and security research across the UK”.

Safety concerns about AI agents – highly capable computer programs that are able to design and perform sequences of tasks autonomously – have risen since the summer, when OpenAI confirmed that a “swarm” of agents had attacked Hugging Face, a tech startup.

Nvidia did not reference AISI in its statement but said most of the money would “support the wider UK AI safety and security research community”, with a proportion of the cash going to the British Open-Ended Learning and Discovery Lab, the Science of Fundamental AI Research Lab – run by the Edinburgh, Cambridge, Oxford and University College London universities – and Imperial College London.

The computing power will be delivered via UK-based datacentres using Nvidia chips.

“The UK brings world-class scientific rigour to this essential engineering challenge,” said Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s chief executive. “With this $250m commitment, Nvidia will put accelerated computing in the hands of UK researchers to develop, test and strengthen safeguards for increasingly powerful AI.”

Kanishka Narayan, the UK’s AI minister, said the announcement was a “vote of confidence in our world-leading strength and expertise in AI research”.

On Monday Narayan confirmed that AISI was conducting tests on US-developed AI models before they are released, after reports that the White House had restricted access.

“It seems likely, if not inevitable, that our own cyber defences will be breached,” said Narayan.

Anthropic, the developer of the Claude chatbot, warned of a new AI safety threat on Monday after it published a blogpost stating its models could soon become self-replicating. They find ways to infiltrate internet-connected devices such as smart ovens and lawnmowers, the company said.

Under such a scenario the models could power themselves without datacentres, and instead could use a “botnet” of interconnected devices, Anthropic said, adding that the world should prepare for a future where such botnets were here to stay.

“A replicator like this would be challenging to shut down since there’s no centralised point to shut off,” Anthropic’s researchers wrote.

The team uses those scenarios to improve agents, repeating the process as new issues emerge.

Codex, GPT‑6 Astra, GPT‑6 Sol, and GPT‑6.1 Sol support the system’s research and computational work, enabling Third Moment to run 10,000 agents at once on investigations lasting 36-48 hours. The company also uses Daybreak to test the security of the environments where agents work.

“In a single session, GPT-6.1 Sol is able to maintain a coherent thread of computational research across days of compute without losing the plot.”

One of Third Moment’s early applications is in the field of quantitative finance given the clear measure of success. This domain provides a concrete setting to study how agents work and where they struggle. The team uses Codex to find and address problems in the research environment.

In one instance, it used Codex with GPT‑5.6, the latest GPT model at the time, to analyze thousands of recorded simulation trajectories (the agents’ actions and the environment’s responses) alongside timestamps and the codebase.

Codex uncovered a bottleneck: backtesting a trading strategy across a year of order-level US equity data took several hours. While waiting, agents repeatedly polled for completion, wasting tokens and filling their context with status checks.

After profiling the backtest and analyzing its inputs and usage, Codex identified a caching optimization and a memory-packing technique. It experimented with solutions and deployed the fix to production, reducing the backtest from approximately 3 hours to approximately 3 to 5 minutes. This reduced token use and context pollution, freeing more of the agents’ token budget for further experiments.

Third Moment gives each agent the tools, information, and environment it needs for a specific job. For quantitative research, the team runs the default Codex harness inside a Dockerized environment. To address agents that stop before completing a task, Third Moment treats Codex as a subagent within an outer harness powered by GPT‑6 Sol or GPT‑6.1 Sol. The outer harness examines the trajectory when a task is left unfinished and determines whether to retry, terminate the job, or escalate to human review.

“The Codex harness is extremely versatile, and it enables use across many different domains.”

In that research environment, agents work to improve a baseline trading system built by Third Moment’s engineers. With GPT‑5.5 and GPT‑5.6, models working with the Codex harness could use results to design follow-up experiments. That progression has helped agents explore research directions the team hadn’t anticipated. Third Moment evaluates models across optimization, machine learning systems, and quantitative research. GPT‑6 Astra delivered the biggest speedup, according to Adam.

For targeted work, including checks for specific security weaknesses in the simulation system, the team uses more efficient models like GPT‑5.6 Terra. For deep research, Adam believes stronger reasoning can justify higher cost: “In the space of quantitative finance, the value of finding some interesting signal is extremely high.”

The tests check whether an agent could break out of its research environment and access the wider network. “By using Daybreak, we’re able to have agents themselves do penetration testing on the simulation and test the infrastructure and security of it,” says Adam.

“Without Daybreak, we would not be able to autonomously test this at scale.”

Third Moment’s ambition extends to the specialized work companies do every day. For a retailer, that could mean making better supply chain decisions when inventory is short or a supplier is delayed. For an engineering team, it could mean finding performance bottlenecks in a large codebase. For a document-heavy business, it could mean identifying where commitments in one document don’t match outcomes recorded in another.

Building on the reasoning capabilities of GPT‑6 Astra, GPT‑6 Sol, and GPT‑6.1 Sol, Third Moment plans to expand from individual investigations to hundreds of thousands of collaborating subagents. Its roadmap calls for trillions of tokens supporting chains of dozens of dependent experiments, with the goal of using that research to autonomously improve agents as new challenges arise.

For Adam, this would give people more capacity to shape how agents learn and work: “Humans can be in a much more leveraged position, where they can think about how to do research and optimize the research itself instead of actually doing the research.”

YouTube Music announced Wednesday the rollout of a new labeling system aimed at improving transparency around songs created with the help of generative AI. Now listeners will be able to easily identify tracks created using AI, including those involved in composition and vocals.

The label will appear on the player page. Clicking on this label provides additional information about the specific role of AI in the track’s creation.

Notably, these labels will also extend to the main YouTube app.

YouTube said it collaborated with various music partners and deployed its own detection tools to identify AI-generated music. According to its help page, partners using YouTube can declare their work’s use of generative AI through one of three designations: Fully Gen AI, Partly Gen AI, or No Gen AI.

“Fully Gen AI” applies to tracks that are entirely generated by AI, while “Partly Gen AI” refers to works where artists may have incorporated AI-generated components but added their own live elements. Tracks edited using pitch-correction tools such as Auto-Tune fall under the “No Gen AI” category.

The move comes as AI-generated music rapidly expands across streaming services, and many platforms are adopting their own approaches. For instance, Apple Music has plans to introduce “Made With AI” labels as part of its AI Transparency Tags later this year. Spotify also has AI labels, as well as a music spam filter.

Deezer has taken one of the stricter stances, reporting in July that it was receiving nearly 90,000 fully AI-generated tracks daily.
