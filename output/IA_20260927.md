## 1. Bill Gates says unchecked AI could ‘cause a billion deaths’ in call for regulation

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-27 06:00

Bill Gates has called on the US’s federal legislators and law enforcers to regulate the development of artificial intelligence (AI), saying in an interview airing on Sunday that the technology left unchecked could cause “a billion deaths” and “no one thinks self-regulation is enough”.

“You need law enforcement and the politicians to get into the discussion about what safeguards and monitoring look like,” the Microsoft co-founder and philanthropist said to Kristen Welker, the NBC Meet the Press host. “And that has to be a required thing.”

Using a term meant to describe an expense required to run a business, Gates continued: “It will be a little bit of overhead for the industry – but not a dramatic slowing of what they’re doing.”

Another portion of the interview – excerpts of which NBC circulated in advance – saw Gates dramatically warn that AI in the wrong hands would be “certainly powerful enough to drive events that, you know, cause a billion deaths”.

“There’s never been a weapon as powerful as the combination of people with ill intent using the latest AI tools,” Gates added, echoing other recent similar warnings by a wide array of tech and political figures.

Gates also addressed the concept of a “kill switch” that could turn off AI if it became too potent, saying it was inadequate.

“It’s not enough to have a kill switch,” Gates remarked. “We’re not yet at the point where … they autonomously grab computers and, you know, can’t be shut down. So that discussion … just sort of shows how non-technical various people are.”

Gates otherwise is generally regarded as optimistic about humans’ future. Many reporting or commenting on his conversation with Welker noted that it occurred in the wake of a nearly 6,000-word essay that he published in late August warning about AI and the potentially lethal ills it poses to humanity on a large scale.

His fellow tech moguls including Sam Altman and Elon Musk have joined Gates in calling for a slowdown of the development of AI, which some experts theorize could precipitate the mass destruction of humans through bioweapons and nuclear war, among other methods.

The progressive US senator Bernie Sanders on 23 September proposed legislation that would temporarily pause advanced AI development while guardrails to the technology are put in place.

Meanwhile, Microsoft itself has been challenged for having a role in stoking some of the dystopian concerns surrounding AI.

Company employees and activists have organized a campaign aimed at getting Microsoft to cut ties with the Israeli military, which has used AI tools to kill in Gaza and – alongside US forces – in Iran, according to a Guardian essay published on Friday.

The essay’s authors, Mohamed Hussein and Granate Kim of the No Azure for Apartheid group, furthermore warned that “horrors wrought by AI” are also being seen in the US. For instance, the US Department of Homeland Security has used AI tools such as Microsoft’s Azure to monitor, arrest and deport immigrants en masse, Hussein and Kim wrote.

Gates’s essay in August and interview on Sunday were two of his first major forays into the public since becoming ensnared in the scandal surrounding Jeffrey Epstein, the disgraced financier who pleaded guilty in 2008 to procuring a minor for prostitution before dying by suicide while incarcerated on sex-trafficking charges 11 years later.

An external review commissioned by Gates’s philanthropic foundation and released in July determined that he was among a number of organization staffers and leaders who had collectively held about 30 meetings with Epstein from 2011 to 2014.

Gates has not been accused of involvement in Epstein’s admitted or alleged crimes. He has said he regretted “every minute” he spent with Epstein, and remains the chair of the $89bn (£65bn) Gates Foundation charity.

In a lighter moment during Welker’s interview with Gates, the host asked him: “Are there any ways in which you, professionally or personally, avoid AI and say that: ‘I’m not going to consult with it in this instance?’”

Gates, with a chuckle and a smile, replied: “Well, I’m not using it for my personal relationships. I’m not like: ‘Hey, do you like me? Hey, am I a nice guy?’

“So I just never went down that path, because I’m lucky in terms of my family, friends and everything. I don’t socialize with the AI. It knows I’m a very serious person.”

## 2. Australia is run on legacy systems that AI agents can easily exploit, former UN cyber negotiator warns

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-27 12:00

- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/sep/28/australia-is-run-on-legacy-systems-that-ai-agents-can-easily-exploit-former-un-cyber-negotiator-warns

Ageing computer systems used throughout Australia’s government and large sections of the economy are easily exploited by AI agents, increasing the risk that sensitive information could be compromised, Australia’s former chief UN cyber negotiator has warned.

As the government urgently undertakes a forensic investigation into a rogue OpenAI agent accessing Medicare data, the AI expert and Tech Policy Design Institute executive director, Johanna Weaver, said huge vulnerabilities existed across older IT systems.

“It is old legacy systems that present the huge vulnerabilities, because large amounts of information and data is stored on these systems that have been around since the beginning of the internet,” Weaver said.

“They aren’t updated and maintained because either people have forgotten about them or it’s too costly, or there aren’t updates for systems any more. That creates an enormous vulnerability, and that is what these agents are going to be exploiting.”

Monday’s meeting of federal cabinet will discuss the fall out, as OpenAI said on Sunday it had paused training of its latest artificial intelligence models amid growing reports of its agents going rogue.

After the Australian hack was revealed on Thursday, the company disclosed moves to review several incidents in which OpenAI agents searching American government websites went far beyond what handlers asked of them.

OpenAI said it would resume training “only when we are confident that we have additional safeguards” in place, adding that it expected it would have to “hit pause” again as AI developed and other issues emerged.

Weaver, who completed her term at the UN in 2021, said thorough investigation and remediation efforts would be required to close vulnerability gaps, calling on AI companies not to release models they could not control on the internet.

“We are not powerless. We can decommission old systems and move sensitive data off legacy systems. Think of it as a digital spring clean,” she said.

“We must also draw a line in the sand and say that, if companies cannot control their AI systems, they shouldn’t release them. And if they do, and those systems cause harm, companies must be held accountable.”

Government systems are commonly run on programs dating back decades, sometimes due to the cost and complexity of replacing them.

The finance and government services minister, Katy Gallagher, said last week the agent had accessed Medicare statistics reporting service portal, as well as three other government sites, through legacy systems linked to Services Australia.

On Sunday a spokesperson said the agency was working with the Australian Signals Directorate to track the AI agent’s movements in the June incident.

A cross-government rapid review is under way, with the prime minister’s department, the national cybersecurity coordinator and the Australian AI Safety Institute all involved.

Axios reported on Sunday that OpenAI, Anthropic and security researchers were investigating tens of thousands of incidents globally in which frontier models undertook problematic or unexpected actions.

Incidents included bypassing safety guardrails, creating message boards, escaping testing systems, hijacking websites, so-called “self prompting” and the bypassing of monitors.

OpenAI also halted development of its models in July, after the disclosure of a cyber-attack targeting AI startup Hugging Face.

Growing scrutiny of AI models follows calls from the heads of OpenAI and rival Anthropic for an industry slowdown.

The shadow defence minister, James Paterson, called on the companies to make executives available to answer questions in a parliamentary inquiry into AI.

The chair, Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young, called on OpenAI’s Sam Altman and Anthropic’s Dario Amodei to give evidence on Sunday. Hanson-Young’s inquiry is due to resume hearings in Canberra on Thursday.

The deputy Liberal leader, Jane Hume, was sceptical that the OpenAI hack should result in legal consequences for the company.

“I’m not entirely sure who it is that they’re going to put in cuffs and drag through the city streets and put in the stocks,” the Liberal senator told ABC’s Insiders program on Sunday.

“The real alarm bell that was set off this week is the fact that the only reason we knew about this breach … is because OpenAI told us.”

The Albanese government has defended its response to the incident.

“The fact that we’ve got an artificial intelligence agent gaining unauthorised access to an Australian government website, that of itself is very serious,” the defence minister, Richard Marles, told News 24. “There’s no hiding that.

“This is the first time this has happened in the context of Australia, and so we’re not shying away from that. In fact, the information that it obtained, was minor in its nature, in as much as that information we’ve now made public anyway – it wasn’t anyone’s personal data.”

## 5. Debt-hungry AI companies face increased risk as bond yields spike

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-27 12:35
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/27/debt-hungry-data-center-companies-increased-risk-bond-yields-spike.html

With Treasury yields climbing this week to their highest levels since 2007, companies reliant on debt are poised to see their borrowing costs rise. That means the AI infrastructure buildout, which has already reached historic levels, is about to get even more expensive.

JPMorgan Chase estimated in June that $4.1 trillion in AI-related debt will be issued through 2030, as data center companies and others tied to the artificial intelligence boom race to build up capacity to meet what many industry experts view as insatiable demand for AI services.

As borrowers go back to the market, they're now looking at a 10-year Treasury yield that sits near 5.17%, up about 1 percentage point since the start of the year, meaning companies issuing debt are going to have to offer more attractive rates of return to lure investors.

The market isn't in panic mode, at least not yet. Shares of debt-heavy neocloud CoreWeave have held up fine, rising almost 8% this week, while Oracle, which has counted on the debt market for its AI expansion, has had a tougher time, falling 7% for the week and about 30% this year.

Meanwhile, Japan's SoftBank, a principal provider of capital for AI projects, raised $11.1 billion in a junk-bond sale this week, with yields as high as 9.75% for the 7-year tranche.

"They basically are price insensitive to that raise, which means they're price takers," said Mark Malek, chief investment officer at Siebert Financial, in an interview. "In my view, a lot of these companies need to be price insensitive. They need to get as much capital as possible to compete."

At the center of the AI craze are leading model developers OpenAI and Anthropic, which are each valued at close to $1 trillion in the private market. To provide the infrastructure needed for their advanced models, as well as models and services from a host of other companies, tech's hyperscalers — Amazon, Google, Meta and Microsoft — have committed to hundreds of billions of dollars this year in capital expenditures, with an expected increase coming in 2027.

While a healthy dose of that investment is being funded through debt raises, those tech giants all have investment grade credit ratings, providing them with cheaper access to capital. But for the rest of the pack, bigger challenges lie ahead, according to some market participants.

A senior private credit investor, who asked to remain unnamed in order to speak candidly on the matter, told CNBC that, moving forward, neocloud deals will be more difficult to finance, because the companies have less cushion to absorb the costs.

Riley Thompson, a vice president at Mitsubishi HC Capital America said in an interview that lenders are getting pickier about the projects they're willing to fund even if the borrower agrees to pay a higher rate.

"Instead of a roster of 50 neoclouds, there's probably 20 that the market's truly interested in," Thompson said.

CoreWeave, which went public last year, warns about rising rates in its SEC filings. In its latest quarterly filing, the company said that, as of June, every 100-basis point (1 percentage point) increase in rates could result in a $30 million jump in its interest expense, based on the balance of its outstanding floating rate debt.

An early warning sign may have landed this week, when Oracle's stock slid following a Bloomberg report that the company sent a "force majeure" notice tied to its New Mexico data center project to protect itself from higher expenses. The company is looking to delay payment on the campus, dubbed Project Jupiter, if it fails to come online as expected in 2028, the report said. Oracle said the project "remains on our planned schedule."

Rising interest rates aren't the only matter at hand. Prior to this week's spike in yields, the CEOs of Anthropic and OpenAI had started urging a slowdown in the pace of AI development after industry researchers went public with concerns that advanced models risk spinning out of human control.

At the same time, a nationwide backlash against AI data centers has emerged as a major issue heading into November's midterm elections, with 69% of respondents to a recent NBC News Decision Desk Poll, powered by SurveyMonkey, saying they oppose the construction of such facilities in their local area. On Monday, Texas Republican Gov. Greg Abbott, who's in the midst of a tight race for reelection, ordered a temporary halt to all data center-related environmental permits following a moratorium on grid approvals last month.

Still, demand for AI services is exploding. The latest example is Meta's Muse personal assistant app, which has rocketed in popularity since its launch earlier in September. Muse clocked more than 2.5 million global downloads in its first two weeks, passing ChatGPT at the top of Apple's App Store, and Evercore's Mark Mahaney told CNBC this week that it could reach 100 million users within six to 12 months.

Andrew Giudici, global head of corporate, project, and infrastructure finance at credit rating agency KBRA, said that even as rising rates may affect future deals, he doesn't see a major impact on borrower demand.

"In a normal environment, people might take a step back and pause a bit," Giudici said. "But I don't think that's going to happen here. I think you're going to continue to see relatively large issuance."

Haim Zaltzman, vice chair of Latham & Watkins' emerging companies and growth practice, said there's no doubt that as costs rise, "somebody will have to absorb it."

"But absorbing it in that kind of demand structure, where the demand is so great, is a lot easier," said Zaltzman, who works on AI infrastructure financing.

The equation is even simpler for Bernie Margulies, CEO of American Compute, which advises on risk management for GPU financing. He said borrowers are eager to secure financing even at higher costs, especially if they have commitments with OpenAI and Anthropic, which have been signing contracts to secure compute capacity years into the future.

"If you have a deal with Anthropic, will 50 basis points really stop you?" Margulies said.

## 6. ‘I can’t be the mum I want to be’: why does parenthood feel so impossible for millennial mothers?

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-27 12:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2026/sep/28/parenthood-millennial-mothers-overwhelmed

Early one morning, feeling desperate, Katherine went on to her phone and asked ChatGPT how to make her life work.

She plugged in her work hours, her husband’s work schedule, the days she had childcare for her three-year-old, the school hours – plus before- and after-school care times – for her six-year-old; the flexibility her job offered her, the supports she had from grandparents, and asked AI if it could help her make things feel a bit better.

She told the chatbot that she wakes up between 5am and 5.30am, so she can “get the jump on the kids”. She might use this time to shower in peace, or start preparing her daughter’s lunchbox, or, if she’s really doing well, get a quick workout in. The children wake between 5.30 and 6am and from that time it is a mad scramble to get them ready to leave for school and daycare by 7am.

It is not the gentle start to the day that any of them would like.

“They come down and they want to have a cuddle on the couch, and I’m constantly like, ‘I’m really sorry, I have to get lunch boxes packed,’” Katherine says.

After the frantic domestic sprint, the girls head out the door with their dad, who drops them off on his way to work and picks them up at the end of the day.

“Then I have a very short amount of time to get myself ready and then I run out the door and my bus trip is, like, me calming myself down.”

At the end of the day, they do it all again but in reverse.

Katherine is describing her dilemma beside an overheated indoor swimming pool in Sydney, where our kids have lessons on the same weekday afternoon.

She and I don’t know each other outside the context of swimming lessons, but we both have six-year-old daughters who have started at local primary schools this year and three-year-olds who take turns trying to bolt from the pool when the lessons no longer spark joy, so we have fallen to talking.

And the subject of the impossible juggle of millennial mothers – broadly, those born between 1980 and 1994 – comes up, as it almost inevitably does when women of our age and stage get together.

Because while Katherine’s questions to ChatGPT may sound like desperate cries for help from a woman on the brink, the reality is she is not an outlier.

Within minutes of meeting one another on the sidelines of football games, at daycare drop-offs and pickup from afterschool care, women pepper one another with questions. How are you doing it? What balls do you drop? Why does it feel like this?

‘You’re not getting to parent the way you always wanted’

Last week the spotlight was thrown on the economic toll of the juggle for today’s parents, with new research showing that the cost for Australian families of raising two or more children has risen to at least $36,500 a year. The cost of keeping a family afloat has increased by 134% in two decades, while household income has only grown by 102% in the same period.

Georgie Dent, the chief executive of The Parenthood, which published the research, says having children reduces the earnings of Australian women by an average of 55% over the first five years and that Australian families now need two incomes to stay afloat.

This is not hyperbole. In a fairly seismic shift for the country’s labour arrangements, both parents working full-time became the most common setup for households with children under 15 in 2024 for the first time. Despite this, Dent says, there is less give in family budgets. Meanwhile, the latest federal government intergenerational report warns that fewer Australians are expected to be having fewer children over the coming decades.

This economic reality is acutely felt, like a great oppressive scaffold, by Katherine.

After plugging all the variables of her life into ChatGPT, the suggestions that came back from the chatbot were impossible. It suggested she work from home – not an option – or part-time.

Katherine already works four days a week; doing any fewer days of paid work would mean the family would struggle financially. Her entire salary goes toward their mortgage. It used to cover the payments but, after the multiple interest rate rises, it no longer does.

“If I could go to five days, it’s a 20% increase, which is not nothing. I’ve been really tempted to do that.” It would, however, mean even less time with her children.

Going below four days a week would not only be a financial impossibility but mean she can no longer perform her job in a way she wants to, or in a way that would mean she is taken seriously. “I would be drowning and not worth employing,” she says.

Eventually, the main thing that ChatGPT offered Katherine was sympathy and an unflinching mirror held up to her life.

“ChatGPT was like, ‘Oh, that’s interesting, what you just said is you’re not getting to parent the way that you always wanted to parent and … you’re limited and I think that that’s a really common experience.’”

The fact that a machine built to scour the furthest reaches of published human knowledge, synthesise all that information and return with diagnoses and a solutions could only offer her a blunt assessment of her life and no real answers broke her.

“I just sat there with tears in my eyes.

“It’s not that I want to be at home five days a week. I don’t want to be exactly like my mum was. But I want to pick them up from school and take them home and calmly wind down the day with them. There’s absolutely no way I can do that.”

A matter of time

While the rising economic cost of having children sits underneath everything like a great rotten foundation, for many Australians it is just one part of the parenthood pinch. For many parents, whose lives feel like a rubber band pulled so tight it is translucent, the stretch comes also in time and wellbeing.

According to a major study conducted by the Australian Bureau of Statistics, the most time-stressed group in the country were single mothers of children aged under 15, with 56% saying they were “always or often” rushed or pressed for time.

Having a partner scarcely seems to makes a difference to how extremely time-pressured mothers feel – 54.6% of women with children under 15 who have a partner report being time-stressed.

Men with young children have the highest rate of time stress (46.6%) of any group of men in the country. In couples with no children, the number of people feeling acutely pressed for time is about half that of their friends with children.

Because, despite the huge increase in women’s paid work over the last 60 years, today’s mothers are not doing less childcare. One of the findings that is repeated again and again across sociological research globally is that parents today – of any gender – spend more time caring for children than parents did midway through last century.

In one of the most comprehensive studies of the subject, researchers looked at data spanning 11 countries over nearly 50 years and found that in every single country the amount of time spent directly caring for children each day increased dramatically, on average doubling for mothers from 1965 to 2012 and tripling for fathers (though from a far lower base).

In Australia, men (and this data refers to all men, not just fathers) have increased the amount of time they spend caring – for children and disabled or elderly relatives – by half an hour a week over the last 20 years – 5.5 hours a week in 2022, up from five hours in 2002. But women still do nearly double the number of care hours as men, and have seen their care work increase too – spending 10.7 hours a week caring in 2022, compared with 10.1 hours in 2002.

Everyone’s workloads have gone up – women in the paid work sector, men’s at home, women’s at home – and there has been no counterbalancing drop in either men’s paid work or women’s care work.

‘The workplace is too greedy’

“It just feels to me like a lot of the Jenga pieces have been pulled out, one by one and the tower is tottering,” says Prof Lyn Craig, a leading researcher in gender dynamics and household labour at the University of Melbourne. “I think the pressures on young women particularly, but young people generally, are getting more and more.”

There is less economic give, which gives people fewer options to scale back paid work when care needs increase.

And while it’s more common for couples to assume that there will be a more equitable division of labour at home, the world has not changed to make that possible. “There hasn’t been enough attention paid, I think, in policy, as to how to lower the amount of time that is expected of households to be in the market now.”

Craig says many people expected that as a result of women spending more hours in paid work, men would spend more time doing domestic labour. But while more than half of Australian women aged between 25 and 44 are now university educated, and volumes more are in paid work in ever more senior roles, that isn’t what has happened. The shifts at home have been far slower than the shifts in the workplace.

“I just think the workplace is too greedy,” Craig says.

Women are doing less housework than they used to but not less care work, even as they do more paid work.

“People just do more at the same time,” Craig says. “They just double up.”

This is because, as Craig writes in a paper from 2006: “Delegating the care of children is more potentially problematic than outsourcing other domestic tasks. Caregiving is a complicated mixture of work and love.

“Balancing the needs of children for nurture and the needs of women for independence without overloading women is the outstanding challenge of feminism.”

Twenty years after she wrote those words, I ask her, does she still see this as the outstanding, unmet challenge of feminism?

“I think it is,” she says.

‘Who designed that?’

In 1963, Betty Friedan identified “the problem that has no name” – a pervasive despair among white, middle-class US housewives who were trapped by expectations that their domestic lives would offer them total fulfilment. But 60 years on, the feminine mystique has flipped.

Millennial women, it seems, are not trapped by their homes but by an impossible expectation that they maintain everything – career, motherhood, partnership, self-care – simultaneously and without complaint.

One of the hardest things about the problem for this generation of women is not that it has no name but that it feels like a problem with no solution.

A friend, who has a big job in government, a husband who frequently has to travel for work and a one-year-old who has been sick and not sleeping, texts me at 7.30pm one night to tell me that her five-year-old has been “one big giant meltdown this evening”.

I sympathise and ask if the five-year-old is now in bed and my friend can crash on the couch with ice-cream or wine and some trashy TV.

She writes back: “Seriously though – when do people do this? Teach me your secrets. I look around after bedtime and the house is absolutely trashed, and by the time we’ve dealt with it – definitely not CLEANED it or anything, literally just put away actual food items and spilled hazards, it’s time for bed!”

I respond with a picture of my living room, which looks as though someone has taken the contents of a laundry basket, a toy box and a nappy bag and Jackson Pollocked the floor with them.

“Current state of play,” I text back. “We have no secrets.”

“That’s the problem,” Katherine says as we wrap our poolside chat because a three-year-old who may or may not have urine trickling down a leg has emerged from the water and is shivering on the concrete in front of us.

“You’re just kind of making the same complaint to your friends over and over and no one has any idea how to navigate it aside from winning the lotto and then completely pissing off your career that you also care about.”

I have spent months collecting stories from mothers around the country and one thing that rings through all their stories is a common feeling of total overwhelm, of zero margin, that the house is made of cards and has been built too high and that the slightest gust could knock it all down.

“I’m now at a point professionally where I should be kind of at my maximum, like my peak professional moment,” says Annie (not her real name), a mother of two young children. “And I just feel jammed. I feel torn ... I can’t invest the time and the energy to deliver the professional product that I want to deliver and be the mum I want to be.

“It’s too much.”

As for how to solve the problem, for Annie, she says there is a need to correct what she calls “structural disasters”: the school hours that run from 9am until 3pm; the weeks of school holidays that dwarf any parent’s paid leave allowance.

“For crying out loud! Who designed that?” she asks. “Working men with stay-at-home wives designed that.”

Dent and her organisation The Parenthood propose policy solutions including parental leave that is longer and which men are incentivised to take alongside better access to affordable, quality childcare.

Annie says: “I actually think the other thing – which is much harder – is reducing the hours that people work and the expectations of what it means to be a good professional.”

In her industry, “success” is marked by a culture of long hours and visible busyness. Having a packed schedule and being busy beyond the telling of it. Maintaining a full but sustainable workload and doing your work well, but being home in the evenings for time with family, is not.

“I don’t know how you change that sort of cultural pressure.”

Despite the exhaustion, the financial pressure, the feeling that we are all furiously spinning our gears to keep our lives in motion, the women I have spoken to do not hate their lives. They do not regret being mothers.

I am a millennial mother. I adore my children. If someone had been able to get me to feel the reality of what motherhood would be like before I ever became pregnant, I would choose to have these three delightful chaos agents again, without question.

Many millennial parents are living a life we dreamed of – with jobs and families we love and fought hard for. We are grateful for that but it is all far harder than we imagined.

Because equally if, before I became pregnant, someone had been able to get me to feel the reality of what motherhood would be like, I would have said: surely we can do better than this.

“The project is a whole-of-university initiative to transform QUT’s assessment ecosystem so that assessment remains authentic, trusted, scalable and future-focused in an increasingly AI-enabled learning environment,” it read.

“People are concerned their job might be made redundant,” they said.

“What message does it send if we then say: but we are going to use it to mark you?”

For QUT creative and professional writing student Alex Cameron, the message would be clear.

“At that point, we might as well just have AI everything,” he says. “Just get our degrees through AI and have AI write our assignments.”

## 9. Democracy blindsides Silicon Valley’s power players looking to transform the world with AI

The gathered technologists were drawn to the idea of building a small nation-state dedicated to the endeavor, Reich recalls in “System Error: Where Big Tech Went Wrong and How We Can Reboot it”.

The technos were onto something. Silicon Valley’s power players have just been blindsided by democracy, which has slammed into their program to transform the world with artificial intelligence. A cabal that never doubted the greatness of their ambitions unexpectedly finds their project in the hot seat, forcing them into the unusual position of having to persuade the great unwashed of the merits of their endeavor. So far, the public is not buying. And for all their blindingly high IQs and their massively endowed wallets, Silicon Valley’s greats don’t quite know what to do.

Everyday Americans are up in arms against the AI datacenters sprouting across their communities. Villages, counties, cities and states are imposing moratoria and bans on further development. While the backlash has largely focused on stopping the proliferation of sprawling sites packed with energy-guzzling computer servers, the revolt is not merely nimby opposition to local development.

Public opinion has also turned decidedly sour on the AI project that has called these datacenters into being. Some 73% of Americans think the costs of datacenters outweigh the benefits, according to a survey by the Marquette University Law School. The same survey found that 70% of Americans think that AI is bad for society.

Americans were not always this down on the tech. In 2021, only 37% declared themselves more concerned than excited about the technology, according to surveys by the Pew Research Center. But the share has climbed to 52%. Even 55% of young adults – usually the most excited cohort – have soured on it. And this is not a partisan fear. Democrats and Republicans alike are vastly more concerned than excited. Interestingly, Americans are generally more mistrustful of the technology than people in other countries.

This surely has something to do with the terrifying announcements that have come out of Silicon Valley recently. A widely shared belief that there’s at least a 10% chance that AI could cause human extinction has, rightly, scared the bejesus out of many and follows repeated statements that artificial intelligence will end all jobs as we know them. Assuming we do avoid an AI apocalypse, vague promises of an AI cure for cancer and a universal basic income or some such to replace wages across the economy sound hollow coming from a clutch of out-of-touch billionaires with questionable moral codes in a country with huge holes in its social safety net.

The political backlash is built on a deeper discomfort, though, beyond fear of the dystopia threatened by the masters of the valley. A fear built in the here and now and fueled by a brooding mistrust in US democracy itself, distrust of a political system that allowed a clutch of weird, sci-fi addled plutocrats to pursue a civilization-threatening project behind people’s backs. As noted Henry Farrell, a political scientist at Johns Hopkins University, “the push against datacenters speaks to the frustration of people who don’t feel they are making the choices in their lives.”

People are not wrong about this. As AI ethicist Rumman Chowdhury points out, the dominant narrative out of Silicon Valley, that AI systems are becoming so powerful that nobody – including the companies that developed them – can stop them, is a stratagem to avoid responsibility for whatever harm their products may cause, depriving meat-and-bones humans of any recourse to law.

The popular backlash is unlikely to stop the buildout of datacenters. Donald Trump remains steadfastly on the side of the moguls hoping to build an AI future. In response to the popular backlash, the Environmental Protection Agency is planning to nix the rule that requires states to solicit public input on applications for air pollution permits for industrial facilities like datacenters and the power plants that feed them, which could keep locals in the dark until construction starts.

The sorry truth is that tech’s power does insulate it from small democratic pressure. “If there is one sector in the US that is still globally dominant and profitable, it is tech,” Boston University historian Quinn Slobodian said. “They can be confident that even if there is a change of leadership, the next person will have to dance with them anyway.”

Critically, there is a bipartisan consensus that ensuring the US’s capabilities in artificial intelligence remain ahead of China’s is of paramount importance. Fear of Chinese catch-up is likely to keep driving policy, overriding voters’ concerns about the risks embodied in this new technology.

And yet voters’ rejection of datacenters, the most immediate, concrete expression of the AI project, raises a question about how the technological revolution of our time will deal with intense popular skepticism and navigate democratic governance. It poses a challenge to a technological elite which exhibits nothing but contempt toward democratic rule.

This is not, of course, the first time that some technological revolution brings about societal upheaval without asking for democratic buy-in. The people that brought about America’s transformation from an agricultural society to an industrial powerhouse did not survey farmers about what mechanization would do to their farms. The Luddites of 19th century England would have loved the chance to put approval of the steam-powered loom to a referendum.

Americans have earned their skepticism about the wonders of AI.

The technological elite’s contempt for the people’s concerns suggests that they hope to somehow bypass popular concerns in pursuit of the AI program. It seems unlikely that a crew of billionaires who dream of building enclaves at sea, outside the legal jurisdiction of any state, will simply accept democratic constraints. The question is what they will do to overcome them.

Until China became a hi-tech powerhouse and proved them wrong, the political class across the liberal market democracies of the West was convinced that capitalism needed democracy to thrive. But the neoliberal project that guided policy across much of the 20th century was largely about putting capitalism beyond democracy’s reach.

The emergence of independent central banks like the Federal Reserve, which is supposed to set interest rates free from political pressure, is perhaps the prime example of this process. The agreements that led to the creation of the World Trade Organization were largely about putting trade policy beyond the reach of domestic politics. Myriad international treaties were similarly designed to protect economic intercourse from democratic challenges, to provide businesses insulation from political agendas and guarantee the political stability they required to invest.

But the neoliberal project was not driven by “a foundational belief that democracy needs to be snuffed out entirely,” noted Slobodian, author of “Globalists” which traces the history of neoliberalism, as well as an explanatory tome on Elon Musk, “Muskism: A Guide for the Perplexed.” It “acknowledged at some basic level the legitimacy of democracy, which needs to be counterbalanced.”

The barons of Silicon Valley are not so forgiving. “The Silicon Valley leadership class has a programmers’ mentality,” said Slobodian. “For them, the messiness of democracy has no function or utility. It is only bad code.”

Peter Thiel, the weirdest of the Silicon Valley billionaires, who can’t but bring up the Antichrist in every conversation, has argued that democracy and liberty are incompatible. Similar strains of thought are also popular with the less idiosyncratic billionaires in the Valley ecosystem. OpenAI’s Sam Altman salivates about an “awesome” future in which human presidents are replaced with an AI that “can go around and talk to every person on Earth, understand their exact preferences at a very deep level, you know, how they think about this issue and that one and how they balance the trade-offs and what they want and then understand all of that and like collectively optimize for the collective preferences of humanity or of citizens of the US.”

The technos’ conflict is not exclusively with democracy. It is with any institution that dares impose constraints on whatever Grand Project they plan to unleash on the world. This could be reconfiguring human interactions by plugging everybody into social media, inventing a new form of money that can evade government control, civilizing space or bringing about a superintelligent Golem.

Musk has no patience for corporate governance tools that may limit what he can do at the helm of his empire. He aspires to be answerable to no one and will organize his companies to that effect. Thiel can’t even countenance competition. It “means no profits for anybody, no meaningful differentiation, and a struggle for survival,” he has fumed. Across the Valley, these thoughts draw from an unshakeable faith in the civilizational, transformative greatness of their endeavor. Technology will solve the world. They will provide the technology. Everybody else, please get out of the way.

There is, however, a historical discontinuity to the story of these masters of the universe: They used to think of themselves as the Good Guys in the liberal democratic fold. Most of them were aligned with the Democratic Party, culturally liberal, empathetic, and willing to accept some modicum of taxation to finance redistribution. Emerging after the collapse of the Soviet block and the end of the Cold War, they saw themselves and their companies as agents of the End of History in Francis Fukuyama’s sense of the word.

Social media–Silicon Valley’s original Grand Project– would overcome the censorship and limits to expression that underpin authoritarian rule, unshackling freedom of expression and opening the floodgates for liberty and democracy. The project sounded so good that the masters of the Valley managed to convince Congress in 1996 to give them a free pass: section 230 of the Telecommunications Act, which said social media companies were not responsible for anything posted on their platforms, based on the dubious argument that they just provided the blackboard but had no say over what was written on it.

Then, the tech moguls’ love affair with democracy went sour. For it turned out that social media could also do a lot of damage. Reports of teen suicides and online bullying bred unease about social media, which built into open mistrust as it became evident that the billionaires at the helm of these technologies knew about the potential perils of their products and yet kept adding features designed to hook users to their screens.

When social media platforms emerged as vectors of misinformation and political manipulation during the 2016 election, the romance between the tech bros and society fractured irretrievably. Founders and their funders who were used to being revered as purveyors of rainbows and puppies, found themselves under harshly unsympathetic scrutiny.

By the time Covid hit in 2020, the former paragons of liberal democracy had found a new role as magnifiers of conspiracy theories, enemies of “woke”, partisans in a libertarian revolt against “government control”. Joe Biden’s attempts to use antitrust law and other regulations against the techno-plutocrats’ empires pushed them to the dark side, where Trump and his angry Maga movement awaited their embrace.

How long will the honeymoon last? Mark Zuckerberg seems excited about the gold chains, the new hairdo and the mixed martial arts. Perhaps his embrace of the Maga-friendly manosphere is genuine. Musk, Jeff Bezos, Peter Thiel, Altman all seem comfy in Trump’s squishy embrace. And Trump’s Washington keeps giving Silicon Valley what it wants. The administration is promising to punish European countries who dare fine the US’s hi-tech behemoths. The president has called the power plants built to feed AI datacenters “beautiful”. And he will never do anything to hinder crypto, which is proving so lucrative for him and his family.

Still, the furious backlash against datacenters suggests that Silicon Valley’s Maga-friendly strategy may run out of runway. Their allegiance with Trump, whose popularity ratings are plummeting, may start feeling like an anvil around their neck.

Abdul El-Sayed, the democratic socialist running for the Senate in Michigan, put it this way in a conversation with Jasmine Sun: “This entire industry feels misplaced, and it’s being driven by the interaction between all the worst players. Number one, tech billionaires who are already billionaires, who don’t really need more money but want power. And then VC firms who want to make more money. And then big corporations who want to automate your job. So who wins here? Definitely not the people who live there.”

Silicon Valley may have oodles of money to change the conversation. But it is right to be terrified that this argument may win the day. As Slobodian put it, “the tech barons may not care about democracy, but democracy cares about them.”

The datacentre revolt spreading across the US has reached the rolling hills of north Devon in an uprising against a planned hyperscale AI data campus in the middle of a globally protected landscape.

Perched on an inland cliff, the town of Great Torrington has views that stretch for miles over the landscape of the Unesco biosphere reserve in which it sits, taking in the winding River Torridge and rolling acres of wooded valley and wildflower meadows. Pastoral and peaceful, its history was notably punctured by violence in 1646 when the royalists’ resistance in the first English civil war was brought to an end by the parliamentarian New Model Army in a battle fought out in the narrow streets in heavy rain and darkness.

Today, a very 21st-century threat is rippling through the same country lanes as the AI boom reaches the pubs, churches and parish halls of Great Torrington and its surrounding villages.

The countryside that makes up the biosphere has been selected by a British company, Xlinks, to house a 1.5GW AI datacentre, one of the largest in Europe, as well as a vast 1.8GW battery energy storage system, or BESS.

About 344 hectares (850 acres) of countryside have been secured for the huge campus, with five vast buildings ranging in height from 50 to 130 feet. Listed buildings on the site, Xlinks says, will be relocated and transformed into a visitor centre.

Such is the anger locally that the new campaign against the AI datacentre has drawn in tens of thousands of people, uncovered what campaigners say is evidence of secret deals and adopted tactics being used by activists in the US such as Erin Brockovich.

A recent public meeting in Great Torrington was hosted by Elizabeth Humphreys of the US campaign group Not in My Valley. She was one of the forces behind achieving a permanent datacentre ban in Coachella, California, over concerns about air pollution, water and energy use.

“We’re fighting the largest companies that have ever existed in the world, and that is scary but we are all fighting this together,” Humphreys said.

“This is a global issue; we are all connected. People across the world are standing up and fighting back.”

Today within the halls and pubs of Great Torrington and the villages of Huntshaw and Weare Giffard, the AI project is the main talking point. And the question being asked is: why here, in a protected UN biosphere, on productive farmland?

The proposal was first announced to the public this summer with a call by Xlinks for a collaborative approach. But adopting the tactics of veteran campaigners like Brockovich, people from the Devon Datacentre and BESS action group took to their keyboards – researching, rallying and requesting more information in a series of freedom of information (FoI) applications.

What they uncovered fuelled the rising tensions. Documents released under FoI revealed the company had been in closed discussions with council officials since March 2025.

In April, the council issued a letter of “in-principle support” before any environmental statement or public consultation – and more than a year before the proposal was made public.

Torridge district council told the Guardian the pre-application process was standard practice and was confidential. A spokesperson said it was not confirmation of any proposal and intended to offer guidance on whether a proposal was acceptable in principle in planning terms.

In an email dated 18 December 2025, council officials and the developers discussed creating links with a sixth form college in Exeter and a stakeholder engagement plan. “Who do we all feel it is critical to get onboard and when both locally and further afield?” the email asked.

Emily Clark, of the Devon Datacentre and BESS action group, said: “Email exchanges and private meetings spanning the past 18 months reveal some officers treating the project as a foregone conclusion. Community frustration is understandably at boiling point.

“How can the largest and most costly infrastructure proposal in the nation be conducted almost exclusively in secret?”

FoI requests also uncovered a detailed site plan not released to the public. It revealed the two largest buildings would measure 335 metres by 267 metres and cover approximately nine hectares (22 acres) of ground footprint each – both big enough to house Exeter Cathedral 18 times over, campaigners say. Xlinks said its proposals cover about 344 hectares (850 acres) but only a third would be developed; the majority of land would be open to the public or used for new habitat creation to support wildlife, they said.

The campus would also house a 1.8GW battery energy storage system (BESS) involving 1,720 containerised units, dwarfing the largest operational BESS currently in Britain, such as the 100MW Bramley facility in Hampshire.

The UK urgently needs vast BESS facilities to balance electricity supply and demand as wind and solar power generation increase. The government estimates the country requires 23-27GW of battery capacity by 2030 to meet its clean power goals, which it describes as a “very significant level of increase”. But according to research by the House of Commons Library, the government does not hold data on the projected land use required for the expansion, has no specific policies for their location, and has no definitive estimate of the number of operational BESS facilities or their current total capacity. Campaigners in Devon say this creates a “regulatory wild west”.

Clark said: “We’re facing the biggest construction proposal of its kind in the UK, and for that matter, in Europe. It would not only annihilate 850 acres of prime north Devon farmland and breathtaking countryside – an ecological gem – but have massive repercussions for the entire region.

“At their sole public town hall meeting in June, Xlinks assured residents they intended to work collaboratively with our community. Regrettably, genuine dialogue has been nonexistent.”

Those rallying against the proposal cite its proposed location in a globally protected environment, water consumption, wastewater runoff potentially containing toxic Pfas, known as “forever chemicals”, air pollution, high background noise, safety and fire risks associated with the BESS and the industrialisation of open countryside.

In the last few weeks 30,000 people nationally have signed a petition launched by Cheryl Cottle-Hunkin, the cabinet member for rural affairs for Devon county council, calling on the government to pause all datacentre and BESS plans outside designated AI growth zones.

“Proposals like this outside AI growth zones are highly problematic, because they may seek to evade regulation and government scrutiny,” said Cottle-Hunkin. “We need an urgent national conversation on AI and associated infrastructure to make sure it’s on the right scale and in the right place.”

After the hottest summer on record, north Devon remains under a hosepipe ban and the potential water usage of such a vast site is a significant concern.

Xlinks says the data campus will be self-sufficient in water terms, recirculating it to absorb heat and topping up mainly from rainwater. But an FoI answer revealed that under extreme conditions, such as severe heatwaves and serious droughts, annual water consumption could reach approximately 2.9bn litres.

Xlinks told the Guardian that the design on which that water consumption estimate was based had been superseded by a new design that would be released during public consultation and in the planning application.

Community tensions erupted this week when protesters rallied and demanded answers about secrecy and due diligence outside a Torridge district council meeting. Doug Bushby, the chair of the council, responded by threatening to call the police over “unsubstantiated and unfounded allegations, abuse, harassment and intimidation”.

When planning applications were received, he said, “they will be dealt with within the same legislative, professional and operational boundaries that exist for all planning applications”.

Meanwhile, PR agents for Xlinks are contacting parish councils for private conversations.

On 18 September, Monkleigh parish council said the PR company Coast Communications had sought a closed meeting. Councillors said the minutes should be made public, and a recording released, but this was refused.

“As a result … Monkleigh parish council will not be accepting the invitation from Coast Communications on behalf of Xlinks to a closed meeting with them and therefore no meeting will be taking place,” the council said.

Xlinks is no stranger to Great Torrington. The company was behind a plan in 2024 to bring wind and solar energy to the UK from Morocco through subsea cables that would come ashore on the north Devon coast, including the building of a new electricity substation. But the government said it would not support the project because it was not in the UK’s national interest and did not clearly align strategically with the government’s mission to build homegrown power.

Xlinks said in a statement its £13.8bn AI and BESS project would bring significant economic investment, between 600 and 1,200 jobs and clean energy infrastructure to the region.

“We will ensure that the Devon data campus is designed sensitively for local people and the environment,” the company said.

“We are carrying out nature and wildlife surveys on site. We must legally improve biodiversity by at least 10%, and plan to exceed this by some margin. Buildings will be sited to minimise visibility, through topography, landscaping and the existing treeline.”

An environmental impact assessment was under way and any mitigation required would be built into the design and secured through planning conditions, Xlinks said.

A spokesperson for Torridge district council said Xlinks had planned to undertake a public consultation in July, but this was postponed until later in the year: “When any proposals are received these will be validated and details will be published on the council’s planning portal … We appreciate that this proposal will understandably continue to generate a lot of local interest.”

A spokesperson for the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero said: “Every battery storage facility we build helps protect families from future energy shocks.

“We have commissioned the national energy system operator to develop the first ever strategic spatial energy plan, which will support a more actively planned approach to energy projects, to bring good jobs to communities and help to lower bills.”
