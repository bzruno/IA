The llama.cpp × AMD Benchmark dashboard is available on Hugging Face. It lets you track prompt processing and text generation performance across llama.cpp builds, models, and quantizations, so you can explore how local inference performs on AMD Ryzen and Radeon hardware.

Local inference performance changes as models, kernels, and builds evolve. We want those changes to be easy to inspect for any AMD developer: pick a hardware, a model and a build, compare quantizations, and follow performance over time. The benchmark workflow and underlying results are public, so you can also inspect how we benchmark inference performance.

You can choose Hardware, Model, and llama.cpp build in the Configuration sidebar. The current hardware choices are Radeon 8060S (gfx1151) and Radeon AI Pro R9700S (gfx1201).

- In the Overview tab, compare quantizations for one hardware, model, and build. The two charts show PP512 (512 input tokens, no generation) and TG128 (128 generated tokens), both starting at context depth zero.

- In the Build history tab, track PP512 and TG128 across all recorded builds for the selected hardware, model, and quantization.

- In the Context scaling tab, see how token-generation throughput changes as the existing context grows, for the selected build, hardware, model, and quantization.

The dashboard shows two separate tests, both measured in tokens per second:

Both tests start at context depth zero (n_depth=0). PP512 measures processing 512 input tokens without generation; TG128 measures generating 128 tokens without a pre-existing context. The plotted value is the mean throughput (avg_ts). These are useful reference workloads, while the Context scaling tab lets you explore token generation throughput as the context grows.

We benchmark the prebuilt binaries distributed through llama-install.sh, the build and installation project behind llama.app. The public gfx1151 and gfx1201 workflows are scheduled weekly and run the standard benchmarks using the llama-bench tool with Flash Attention on and off, with 10 repetitions per configuration.

The model set evolves and currently includes Inkling 2 Mini, Qwen, Gemma, gpt-oss, and LFM models, covering both dense and mixture-of-experts architectures. Available GGUF formats include Q4_K_M, Q8_0, other quantizations, and higher-precision formats such as BF16 and F16.

The explorer reads directly from the public ggml-org/install.sh-benches-data Storage Bucket on Hugging Face. The data source link beside the chart title opens the folder for the selected build, hardware, and model.

Standard results use <build>/<hardware>/<model>/<quant>.jsonl. Workload sweeps use the same folder <quant>-tg.jsonl filename.

Use them to investigate a result, make your own charts, or compare with a local run.

Thanks to the AMD team for providing test infrastructure and helping validate the setup! Try the dashboard, inspect the data, and let us know which models and workloads you would like to see next.

## 5. Riemannsche Vermutung: Claude kürzt KI-Beweis um mehr als 99 Prozent

- **Fonte:** Heise
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-26 09:50
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.heise.de/news/Riemannsche-Vermutung-Claude-kuerzt-KI-Beweis-um-mehr-als-99-Prozent-11539145.html?wt_mc=rss.red.ho.ho.atom.beitrag.beitrag

Innerhalb weniger Tage ist es Mitarbeitern von Anthropic gelungen, den mithilfe des KI-Modells Claude entwickelten Beweis der Riemannschen Vermutung auf einen Bruchteil seines ursprünglichen Umfangs zu reduzieren. Bei der Überarbeitung kam ebenfalls Claude zum Einsatz.

Am Freitag hatte der bei Anthropic tätige Mathematiker Levent Alpöge den Beweis der Riemannschen Vermutung veröffentlicht. Der Beweis umfasste rund 17,4 Millionen Zeilen in Lean, einer formalen Sprache, mit der sich mathematische Beweise maschinell überprüfen lassen. Bereits einen Tag später bestätigten Vertreter der Lean-Gemeinschaft die formale Korrektheit des Beweises.

Anfang dieser Woche räumte Alpöge auf X ein, dass der Lean-Code viel Überflüssiges enthalte und sich der Beweis erheblich vereinfachen lasse. Wie groß das Kürzungspotenzial tatsächlich ist, zeigt sich wenige Tage später.

Claude überarbeitet Claudes Beweis

Am Mittwoch veröffentlichte Anthropic-Mitarbeiter Luke Sernau auf GitHub zwei kürzere Fassungen des Beweises, die er innerhalb weniger Tage mit Kollegen und Claude erarbeitet hatte. Einer unabhängigen Auswertung zufolge umfasst die kompaktere Fassung einschließlich Kommentaren und Leerzeilen rund 113.000 Zeilen, über 99 Prozent weniger als das Original. Eine auf Lesbarkeit optimierte Fassung kommt auf rund 477.000 Zeilen.

Laut Sernaus Dokumentation verfasste Claude sowohl den Lean-Code als auch die begleitenden Erläuterungen. Die Überarbeitung ging dabei über eine bloße Codebereinigung hinaus. Wie Sernau im Forum der Lean-Gemeinschaft erläutert, wurden für zahlreiche mathematische Zwischenresultate einfachere Beweise gefunden. Viele Argumente seien daher neu.

Beide Fassungen wurden inzwischen vom MIT-Mathematiker Andrew Sutherland unabhängig maschinell überprüft. Zu demselben Ergebnis gelangte der Mathematiker Michael Rubinstein von der University of Waterloo bei einer Prüfung der kompakten Fassung. Die Prüfungen bestätigen, dass die Neufassungen dieselben mathematischen Aussagen beweisen wie das Original.

Vom Beweisen zum Verstehen

Trotz der drastischen Vereinfachung steht die mathematische Fachwelt weiterhin vor der Aufgabe, die Beweisführung nachzuvollziehen. Die Lean-Gemeinschaft, die bereits mit der Bereinigung des ursprünglichen Codes begonnen hatte, nutzt mittlerweile Sernaus lesbarere Fassung als Grundlage für ihre Arbeit.

Über die Aufbereitung des Lean-Codes hinaus geht ein gemeinschaftliches Forschungsprojekt, das drei Mathematiker am Dienstag auf dem Blog des Fields-Medaillenträgers Terence Tao ankündigten. Es soll einen für Menschen verständlichen Beweis hervorbringen und die darin enthaltenen mathematischen Neuerungen für die weitere Forschung erschließen.

Dass KI auch bei dieser Aufgabe helfen kann, zeigt Rubinsteins Arbeit: Mit Claude, Codex und Gemini hat der Mathematiker auf Grundlage des ursprünglichen Beweises eine 265-seitige, noch unvollständige Ausarbeitung zur Riemannschen Vermutung in herkömmlicher mathematischer Fachsprache erstellt. Der polnische Mathematiker Bartosz Naskrecki veröffentlichte am Donnerstag einen ebenfalls mithilfe von KI entstandenen Arbeitsentwurf, der bereits die gesamte kompakte Fassung auf diese Weise aufbereitet. Eine fachliche Begutachtung durch Menschen steht noch aus.

Naskrecki sieht in der KI-gestützten Beweisfindung und der raschen Vereinfachung einen Wendepunkt für die mathematische Forschung. Auf X schreibt er: „Der Engpass liegt nicht mehr beim formalen Beweis, sondern darin, dass Menschen ihn lesen müssen.“

エージェント、開発性能を示す5つの利用例

Googleがまず強調するのは、Neonのエージェント性能だ。Geminiのモデル名を元素で表現した実験台の3Dシーンの生成デモでは、Neonが「Google Antigravity」の「/swarm」機能を使って複数のエージェントを統制し、3DCGソフト「Blender」を操作して制作した。

## 7. Taking Trainium3 to the Track: Generating Videos with LTX-2.5

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-26 21:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/video-trainium3

Thanks to our partnership with AWS, we were lucky enough to get access to a Trainium3 machine (trn3.48xlarge, 64 NeuronCores) for a month. We spent most of the month testing Hugging Face models on it, and getting more of them to work.

With five days left, I allowed myself a little fun. When you have a Ferrari, every now and then you want to take it out on the track. So I asked myself: what if I tried to generate videos?

I picked LTX-2.5 from Lightricks, which generates video and sound together. By the end, once the models were loaded, a 10-second clip with sound took 22 to 23 s to generate, on half of the machine. Here is the result.

I had two goals. The first was to see how easy it is to generate videos with diffusers on Trainium3. Not the simple clips from the examples: I wanted to make short films from a script, closer to a real use case, like drafting a movie. The second was performance. I wanted to get close to what you get with CUDA, or with LTX's own API.

For CUDA, I found no real figure to compare against. The only one is LTX's claim of 6.8 s for a 10-second 720p clip on four B200 GPUs, with no detail on how it was measured. So I set CUDA aside and compared with LTX's online API instead, which LTX says makes a 10-second 1080p clip in 23.7 s. In the end, the setup matches it, and even beats it by about a second: 22 to 23 s for a 10-second clip at 2048×1024, once the models are loaded, on half of the machine. Not bad, considering how little time I had to optimize the pipelines.

The input is a film: a JSON list of shots, each with a name and a prompt, much like a storyboard. LTX-2.5 does better with long prompts, so each prompt is a paragraph on the style, the subject, the motion, the camera, the light and the sound. Here is the seal film's file, with the prompts shortened:

[

{"name": "dream", "prompt": "Style: cinematic, realistic, shallow depth of field. A plump seal ... is fast asleep on warm golden sand under palm trees ...",

"seed": 3},

{"name": "nap", "prompt": "Style: cinematic, realistic, wide-angle lens. ... a plump grey seal is fast asleep on a small ice floe ...",

"seed": 1, "to": {"shot": "iceberg", "frames": 9}},

{"name": "iceberg", "prompt": "Style: cinematic, realistic, wide-angle lens. ... the whole iceberg slowly rolls over ... and washes over the seal. ..."},

{"name": "shake", "prompt": "Style: cinematic, realistic, wide-angle lens. ... It slowly shakes its head and body ... and flops down on its belly on the floe, ...",

"from": {"shot": "iceberg", "frames": 25}}

]

Each shot has a name and a prompt, and can have a seed, to keep the take I picked among several. Shots are chained with two keys. This chaining is the point, since prompts alone do not keep a subject from one shot to the next:

- from: the shot starts from the last frames of an earlier shot. Starting from the earlier shot's last frame keeps a subject's look across a cut; starting from its last 25 frames (1 s) also continues the motion.

- to: the shot ends on the first frames of a later shot, so that it leads into it.

A shot can also be trimmed, or have a caption drawn over it.

Making a film from shots works like animation, which has two ways to go. Straight ahead, each shot continues the one before. Pose to pose, you make the key shot first, until you get one you like; then the shots before it end on its first frames (to), and the shots after it start from its last frames (from). When a film builds up to a payoff (the moment everything was leading to), the payoff is the key shot. I found this order by trial and error, and made the seal film pose to pose, starting from iceberg.

The payoff is iceberg: the iceberg rolls over, and its wave drenches the seal. nap, the sleeping seal, ends on the first 9 frames of iceberg (to), so that the seal, the floe and the iceberg stay the same into the payoff. shake starts from the last 25 frames of iceberg (from): the dripping seal shakes off the water and flops down on the floe. The dream is made on its own, and a cut joins it to the rest.

On the diffusers side, three pipelines share the models of LTX-2.5. LTX2Pipeline makes the shots from their prompt alone, and LTX2ConditionPipeline makes the shots that start or end on frames of another shot. These two follow the two-stage distilled recipe of the model card: 8 steps, then the third pipeline, LTX2LatentUpsamplePipeline, upscales the latents ×2 before 3 final steps.

The diffusers pipelines do all of the generation. The film part is about 160 lines of Python on top: read the film file, order the shots so each keyframe exists before the shot that needs it, and cut the frames to hand over; then trim, caption, even out the sound and assemble the film. The code is in dacorvo/video-generation. The films I kept are in the dacorvo/video-generation bucket, with their JSON files: the seal, the fox, and the goat, which trims two of its shots and draws a caption over its payoff.

Out of the box, the model does not run on the NeuronCores: the transformer does not fit on one, and diffusers has no plan to split it. The first attempt that ran (the model card's pipeline, with the transformer split over 4 cores and a few patches) took about 5 min 20 s for a 10-second clip, and the NeuronCores were actually computing for only about 45 s of it. What's more, the target resolution was out of reach.

Five days later:

Getting there took three kinds of work: making the model run on the NeuronCores (requires tensor parallelism), splitting it over more of them (sequence parallelism), and making the host keep up with them.

Most of the fixes below are on the device, and that is where most of the effort went. The biggest end-to-end savings came from the host: the low-hanging fruit, since that is obviously where the waste was.

Three issues were never explained: the transformer compiled as one graph returned NaN at one size, the first shot that ends on keyframes was slow in each new process (even with every graph in the cache), and the latent upsampler fails to load next to the transformer, so it stays on the CPU.

Some of this is being fixed upstream. diffusers#15420 (open) fixes the LTX-2 pipelines, which would make the second workaround above unnecessary. diffusers#14725 (still open) brings tensor and context parallelism into one ParallelConfig, and a _tp_plan for LTX-2.5 is planned.

- Both sides fell hard: the host and the device. Once running, a clip's host time fell from about 280 s to about 8 s (~35×), and its time on the NeuronCores from 45 s to 15 s (3×, at four times the pixels, and with a second stage the first attempt could not even load).

- Sequence parallelism is how one clip uses the machine. Tensor parallelism is imposed by memory (the transformer does not fit on one core) and capped by the 32 attention heads. Splitting the sequence over more cores is what puts 32 of the 64 NeuronCores on a single clip, and what made 2048×1024 possible at all, by shrinking each core's graph until it loaded.

- On Trainium, plan for the size of the graphs. The shapes decide whether the attention takes the fast kernel, and graphs that are too large fail to load.

- Check against the CPU first. Each split of the models was checked on CPU before it ran on the device, and the comparison with CPU found the GELU issue.

- Keep the traced code stable. In the build I used, torch-neuronx's cache key included the file and line of each traced op, so an edit could trigger a recompile of up to 30 minutes per graph. I had already reported it (aws-neuron/torch-neuronx#1027), and the issue is now closed.

- A film needs more than prompts. Prompts alone do not keep a character, or a mountain, from one shot to the next, so shots start or end on frames of other shots. Each shot also stays in one place: when a shot in an early version of the fox film took the fox from the forest to the lake, the model made a cross-dissolve to another fox.

- Make several takes of each shot. The model often gets a detail wrong: the same prompt gave the goat funny horns in 3 takes out of 4. I made 4 takes of each new shot and picked one.

On the performance side, once the models are loaded, a 10-second 2048×1024 clip with sound takes 22 to 23 s. That rivals LTX's paid API, on half of the instance. A service would keep its processes up, as they take about 3 minutes to load the models, and 80 s for their first clip.

On the feature side, diffusers has what it takes to draft a movie. The shots come from prompts, and keep their subject from one shot to the next by starting or ending on frames of other shots. Takes differ by their seed, and the film file keeps the one I picked. Re-run with the same code and film file, and you get the same film back, byte for byte. What keeps it at drafts:

- The model still gets details wrong, so each new shot needs several takes.

- Each shot makes its own sound. The film evens out the background level, but the sound does not carry over from one shot to the next.

- A shot lasts at most 20 s, the model's limit.

## 13. Building on Nemotron 3.5 Parse: Open Data, Fine-Tuning, and Faster Inference

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face

We recently released NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Parse, a compact document model that turns page images into structured data for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and agents.

Now we’re building on that release with synthetic training datasets, a fine-tuning recipe, and a DFlash2 speculator for faster inference. We’re also sharing how we trained Parse 3.5: first teaching document understanding through supervised fine-tuning, then using reinforcement learning to improve accuracy, consistency, and reliable completion.

Parse extracts content from pages of documents into reading order, assigns content classes such as headings, paragraphs, tables, and charts, and returns bounding boxes that locate the content. Tables and charts become structured records that can power downstream document processing applications.

Figure 1. Actual model output from a synthetic operations brief, showing the connection between the page and its structured representation.

This latest version of Parse significantly extends its ability to read charts and tables, especially in more difficult and ambiguous cases. For example, bar charts or other plots that do not have any marked numeric values. The model is able to infer values by looking at the axes and height of the bar to derive these. Tables can be especially tricky because the structure is so meaningful, a shifted row or column can totally change the interpretation and meaning. These advancements have been largely powered by synthetic data generation, rendering variations of charts and tables with known ground truth and then training our model to reverse this process.

We are open-sourcing three datasets used in the Parse 3.5 training:

- Synthetic Charts V1 — 9.98M examples: rendered charts and structured annotations for learning categories, series, and numerical values.

- Synthetic Tables V1 — 1M examples: rendered HTML tables with targets that preserve rows, columns, headers, and cell relationships.

- OCR Synthetic Multilingual v2 — 7.9M examples: synthetic document pages with Parse-format targets across varied fonts and layouts, for multilingual OCR and structured page reading.

Each release includes images, annotations, generation documentation, and the training format. Use these pairs to fine-tune document models or create new variations for a target document type or language.

Figure 2. Parse recovers 18, 27, 39, and 54 from the chart geometry

We trained Parse 3.5 with two phases of training. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) spanning text, layout, tables, charts, and multilingual documents. Learning to predict structured targets established the general capability of reading a page and representing its contents.

We then continued with reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Rewards check the quality of sampled extractions. For example, a chart extraction can earn a better reward for recovering the correct series and values, producing a usable structure, and finishing without repeatedly generating the same content.

A difficult page can have several valid representations. These checks give the model flexibility to find a correct answer without prescribing one exact token sequence, while discouraging looping and unfinished output.

Figure 3. A controlled comparison of SFT and GRPO with matched evaluation settings. These are development checkpoints, separate from the final released model. Loop counts use a repetition check. Methods.

The matched comparison shows the benefit: loop-flagged outputs fall from 126 to 29 pages—about 77% fewer—and outputs reaching the 9,000-token limit fall from 185 to 35. Overall score improves from 66.24 to 71.38, including +5.18 points in tables and +4.15 in charts.

A parser must generate substantial text and structure for every page. Alongside the training work, we optimized how Parse 3.5 processes images and decodes those outputs.

NaViT preprocessing adapts the input shape to the page. A visual adapter compresses the image sequence, and the text decoder consumes it as a prefix, replacing encoder–decoder cross-attention. Grouped-query attention with eight key/value heads (GQA-8) reduces the decoder’s attention-cache memory.

The built-in multi-token prediction (MTP) head supplies one-token lookahead. Our DFlash2 speculator goes further: a small draft model proposes up to 32 tokens ahead, and Parse verifies the proposals together. In the measured 32-token configuration, it averages about 14 accepted draft tokens, or roughly 15 output tokens of progress per verification step including the target token.

Figure 4. Native single-pass throughput on one H200, BF16, using the same 2,078 pages. Parse 2.0 uses one trial per point; Parse 3.5 shows three-trial medians. Arrows show the gain from Parse 3.5 base to DFlash2. Measurement details.

In the native single-pass benchmark on one H200 in BF16, DFlash2 increased throughput 7.62× at concurrency 1 and 1.52× at concurrency 512, compared with Parse 3.5 with speculative decoding disabled. At 512, Parse 3.5 reaches 13.48 pages/s without speculation and 20.44 pages/s with DFlash2, versus 2.98 for Parse 2.0. Those are 4.53× and 6.86× the Parse 2.0 throughput, respectively.

Fast parsing gives us room to revisit difficult regions. Start with a full-page prediction, then add focused reprocessing where the document needs it:

- Single pass - Parse the full page once for text, layout, tables, and charts.

- Double pass - use for dense tables and small chart details. Crop the table and chart regions found by the parser, re-parse them at higher detail, and merge the refinements into the page output.

- Triple pass - Instead of only using the models full page parsing bounding boxes, use a second pass of the model in detection mode in order to get verification that all boxes have been accurately found and text has not been missed and re-extract any found charts or tables in a third pass

Figure 5. Additional stages focus computation on difficult regions. Multiple crops can require multiple requests.

In the published reference evaluation, double and triple pass add 2.18 and 2.46 ParseBench points over single pass. The additional work lets applications trade some throughput for better extraction in difficult regions. Reference results and serving measurements.

We are sharing a Parse fine-tuning recipe that takes developers through paired data generation, NeMo Automodel SFT, and NeMo RL GRPO, using vLLM to generate candidate answers.

The worked example uses a synthetic dental form with an odontogram: a chart showing teeth, planned procedures, and affected tooth surfaces. Before adaptation, Parse reads the printed tooth numbers but misses the intended procedure table. SFT teaches it to associate each marked tooth with a procedure and its placement on the tooth.

Figure 6. A recorded recipe example: after SFT, all six chart rows match the target. Full image and native training label.

RLVR then reinforces accuracy and consistent completion. In the dental reference run, the estimated first-attempt success rate (sampled pass@1) rose from 0.00% for the base model to 95.85% after SFT and 96.58% after RLVR.

Figure 7. Matched evaluation on 256 held-out forms from the same synthetic generator, with eight samples per page. Success requires natural completion, exact chart content, and error-free normalized page text. Evaluation protocol.

To adapt the recipe, supply your own image/label pairs and a verifier: checks that determine whether an extraction is correct. The included document reward components cover text fidelity, bounding boxes, tables, chart facts, and completion. Combine the components relevant to your task, add schema checks, and use completion and repetition checks to discourage looping or unfinished output.

We are excited that the community has extended and improved Parse for Thai, Persian/Farsi, and Arabic already and we hope that these additional datasets and fine-tuning recipes will help encourage these efforts and future models.

"The main focus now will be trying to determine how many further increases the Fed considers necessary in early 2027 and whether AI shares can withstand that rate path while investors continue to question the returns on heavy spending."

U.S. inflation data on Wednesday reinforced expectations of further monetary tightening by the Federal Reserve. The personal consumption expenditures price index rose 3.8% year on year in October, accelerating from 3.7% in September, while core inflation came in slightly above expectations at 3.3%.

Mark Haefele, chief investment officer at UBS Global Wealth Management, said in a note on Thursday morning that his team believes investors should maintain strategic allocations to equities.

But he advised market participants to "review current allocations and use market moves as an opportunity to rebalance, refocus, and strengthen portfolio resilience."
