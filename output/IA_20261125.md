## 1. Saudi national in OpenAI bugging case is out on bond after DOJ tries to block release

Abdulrahman Alfozan, the Saudi national accused of bugging an executive conference room at OpenAI's headquarters, has been released from custody, his attorney told CNBC on Wednesday.

The confirmation of Alfozan's release came a day after a federal judge in San Francisco said the defendant would be allowed out of jail on a $1 million bond, despite prosecutors' warnings that he posed a serious flight risk.

Alfozan, a former OpenAI engineer, is expected to be fitted with an ankle monitor, his attorney, David Rizk, previously told CNBC.

Alfozan was scheduled to be released from detention Wednesday morning at 8 a.m. PT. Shortly before that deadline, U.S. Attorney Craig Missakian of the Northern District of California urged the court to maintain Alfozan's detention pending trial, "given the significant risk that the defendant will flee to Saudi Arabia if he is released from custody."

In an order posted on the case docket later Wednesday, Judge Vince Chhabria said the court only learned of the government's last-minute bid to block Alfozan's release after he had already been let out.

The government's motion "is therefore denied as moot," Chhabria's order stated. The judge set a hearing for Dec. 3 on the bid to revoke the magistrate judge's order releasing Alfozan.

Pointing to Alfozan's "ample resources, significant family and professional ties to Saudi Arabia, and few remaining ties to the United States," Missakian had argued in a court filing that the release conditions imposed by Magistrate Judge Alex Tse "are insufficient to safeguard the defendant's appearance for future proceedings in this case."

Alfozan "presents a serious risk of flight that no set of release conditions can reasonably mitigate," Missakian wrote.

His office sought to pause the magistrate judge's release order until a ruling on a motion to revoke it. The Department of Justice did not immediately respond to CNBC's request for comment on Alfozan's release.

Tse in a court hearing Tuesday ruled in favor of Rizk's motion for his client's release, which said prosecutors have admitted finding "no new evidence to support the theory that Mr. Alfozan was stealing trade secrets, or to support the speculation that he was acting as an unregistered foreign agent for Saudi Arabia."

In a text message to CNBC after that hearing, Rizk said, "We are simply grateful that the Court made the right decision to release Mr. Alfozan and now we look forward to defending the case."

But Missakian's filing Wednesday morning pushed back, saying "the government's investigation of this seven-week-old case is ongoing."

Prosecutors have so far found "hundreds of audio files" on Alfozan's personal computer, "including dozens of recordings of meetings or conversations that occurred at OpenAI throughout September 2026, at least one of which involved representatives of a foreign government and many of which involved discussions of what may be trade secrets," according to the filing.

"To be sure, what the defendant did or intended to do with those audio files remains under investigation, but the absence of that evidence at this point has no bearing" on whether Alfozan is a flight risk, Missakian argued.

Alfozan was arrested Oct. 9 and is accused of concealing a recording device in the artificial intelligence company's conference room to secretly record discussions. The device was found in a USB port in a television in the room on Sept. 30, prosecutors have said.

Jury selection for Alfozan's trial is scheduled to begin Jan. 20.

BEIJING — Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia as its CEO underscores how the U.S.-China artificial intelligence race is not only about semiconductors, but tech talent.

"We have to protect our geniuses, and he's clearly a genius," Donald Trump told CNBC about his intervention to bring Huang back. If not, Trump warned, the founder of the AI chip giant could hypothetically have taken a job in China.

The concern reflects how Chinese AI companies have been narrowing the gap with their U.S. counterparts, not just in models, but now in engineering and research talent. China has surpassed the U.S. by share of global AI talent in just three years, according to Carnegie Endowment research.

Nvidia "was shaken without a single chip or model changing," said Winston Ma, an AI investor and adjunct professor of law at New York University. "Whoever builds and keeps that human layer will hold the rest of the stack."

Huang resigned Nov. 12 after reports of an affair with a woman U.S. intelligence reportedly believes has links to Chinese intelligence. He returned on Nov. 23, news which sent Nvidia shares surging.

More than 30 years ago, Huang founded Nvidia as a gaming graphics chip company. The semiconductors gradually became used to mine cryptocurrencies, and now, training cutting-edge AI models.

Huang has more advanced tech coming, Trump told CNBC's Eamon Javers on Monday.

"He's got other chips in his brain that nobody else knows about, including me, that nobody else understands," Trump said.

"Jensen could take the next plane to China, and I guarantee you, they would give him whatever he wanted, and then and then they automatically have, you know, all of the things," Trump said. He acknowledged there was "never even a suggestion" that the Nvidia founder was recruited by China.

Trump returned to the U.S. over the weekend from Shenzhen, China, where he met Chinese President Xi Jinping. Earlier in the month, turmoil around Huang's future at Nvidia had gripped global attention.

For Huang's supporters, talent was a critical part of the argument, said Damien Ma, director and Maurice R. Greenberg Director's Chair of Carnegie China.

As the incident has increased scrutiny on the people involved in the U.S.-China tech race, Ma said he's closely watching whether either country restricts AI engineers from leaving. While he expects limited policy changes, other countries such as Singapore and South Korea could benefit from an inflow of AI talent, he added.

Already, many companies are using a mixed-market approach, given engineers in the U.S. make two or three times as much as their Chinese counterparts due in part to foreign exchange rates and AI company valuations.

In the U.S., "you need to have a lot of money to recruit them," said Alex Lu, founder of LSY Consulting — and that can get expensive.

Lu cited the founder of a U.S. consumer research firm, who opted to hire engineers based in China after visiting the country.

It's an anecdote that's frequently repeated by analysts, sometimes with U.S.-trained Chinese staff based in Singapore.

Chinese companies are "very, very aggressive in getting good talent from the U.S.," said Tidalwave Solutions Partner Rachel Wu, who recruits for large Chinese tech companies and startups.

She also said a Meta engineer in Canada took an offer from a Chinese AI startup for a salary of 1.4 million yuan ($210,000), in addition to stock options.

"There is some truth to Trump's comments given Jensen's knowledge of the AI industry and supply chain," said Lian Jye Su, a chief analyst of Omdia. "It would be unwise for the U.S. to let this saga continue to affect its AI ambition, especially when that ambition includes slowing down Chinese progress in AI."

However, Rui Ma, founder of advisory Tech Buzz China, said Huang's background of being a founder makes it more likely that he would have started his own company, rather than taking a managerial role at one.

She also noted Huang's "deep belief in America" and laws that would block him from using his access to TSMC and other suppliers to help a Chinese company.

The U.S. has restricted China's access to advanced Nvidia semiconductors over the last four years. But in early 2025, DeepSeek upended assumptions that chip restrictions could keep Chinese AI capabilities well behind that of the U.S.

A few years ago, only the U.S. and Singapore had the kind of AI talent that Chinese companies were looking for, Wu said. "Today we see the gap has been filled," she said, adding the supply of local talent has increased in the last two to three years.

"The demand is huge," Wu said, saying one ex-Alibaba AI engineering candidate with seven years of experience had more than five offers.

Switching between companies can result in a 25% to 30% pay bump, Wu said. She expects the trend to persist for another two years, although the increase in compensation may not be as high.

Still, restricted access to advanced AI chips plays a role in where the best researchers choose to work.

It's a question of "where I can do my further research and do I have enough resources to do my research," said Lu.

He said industry research suggests "China is becoming a really important pool for talent, while the U.S. is paying 80% of the money."

## 8. Where scientists draw the line on AI agents

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-25 15:52
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/where-scientists-draw-the-line-on-ai-agents

Delegating research tasks to AI does not remove scientists’ responsibility for how the work is accomplished. For example, a researcher using AI to review the literature needs to check whether the system overlooks conflicting evidence or makes claims the cited papers don’t support. Someone handing off data analysis to an agentic system needs to understand which methods it used, which files it accessed, and whether the results can be successfully reproduced. A researcher automating part of a lab protocol needs confidence that the system respects physical safety constraints, such as temperature limits or material toxicity, while a researcher working with human-participant data needs assurance that a system protects participants' privacy and limits who can access their information. Researchers also need clear institutional guidelines for verifying and disclosing AI-assisted work.

These ethical and safety questions are central to our work on AI for science. Responsible science is about more than accuracy—scientific fields have ethical research norms because of past actions and choices by scientists and scientific communities, and those norms also cover how research is conducted and what impact it has. We want AI tools to expand what scientists can accomplish without compromising their ability to scrutinize evidence, verify methods, and – perhaps most importantly – stand behind their findings.

To better understand what that requires, we interviewed 17 scientists working in the natural, applied, and social sciences about the responsible development of AI agents. These systems can use tools and carry out research tasks with varying degrees of autonomy. The interviews drew on a network of researchers established through earlier survey work—we wanted to understand how scientists would steer AI’s development to reflect the responsibilities of their fields. We asked where researchers would welcome agentic assistance, what could go wrong, and what safeguards they would need to trust agents with their own studies.

Our interest in these questions grew out of work to evaluate the safety of models developed at Ai2. As we expand our focus on AI for science, we’ve found that much of the safety work on scientific applications emphasizes catastrophic risks in areas such as biology and nuclear physics. We also need ways to assess whether agents follow the standards of responsible conduct of research in the fields where they’re used.

One of our main findings concerns trust. Across the interviews, we identified five dimensions of scientific trust: validation, privacy, objectivity, accountability, and transparency. Together, they reflect concerns that go beyond whether an AI system produces a correct answer. Researchers also need to understand how an answer was reached, protect sensitive information, recognize bias, and determine who is charged with verifying and standing behind AI-assisted findings. These are matters of responsible conduct of research.

Another main finding suggests that scientists should help decide which research tasks agents take on, how those agents operate, and how their performance is evaluated. Even a technically correct result may be unsuitable for scientific use if researchers cannot inspect how it was produced, or if an agent accesses confidential data or files beyond what the researcher authorized.

A third key finding pertains to the future of science. Interviewees were divided between excitement about AI-driven progress and existential concern about the role of human scientists. They proposed training in AI use and named developing and enforcing guidelines as a critical, timely challenge.

For many participants in our interviews, the appeal of AI agents began with time-consuming research tasks—processing large datasets, writing code, documenting experimental procedures, and handling repetitive administrative work. Automating these activities could free researchers to spend more time on fieldwork, mentoring, or developing new ideas. It could also make it possible to analyze volumes of data that would be difficult to examine manually.

But delegating research tasks creates a corresponding need to verify the work an AI system performs.

Several participants described checking AI-generated answers against original publications rather than accepting summaries at face value. They worried about confident responses containing subtle inaccuracies, as well as tools that reinforce assumptions by retrieving only studies that support a favored hypothesis.

But checking the final answer from an AI system is only part of the challenge. Researchers may also need to know which sources an agent omitted, whether it followed the requested methodology, and what information it accessed along the way.

Our interviews suggest how evaluations of AI agents for scientific research could better reflect these concerns. A literature-review benchmark, for example, could test whether an agent overlooks contradictory evidence or draws conclusions unsupported by its sources. A coding benchmark could assess not only whether the generated code works, but whether the agent accesses files outside its authorized scope. Evaluations of data-processing agents could compare their methods and results with established human workflows.

These examples point toward a longer-term goal—a participatory benchmark in which scientists help define the research practices and safety requirements that agents should be tested against. We want to evaluate whether systems developed at Ai2 or elsewhere follow the standards of the fields where they’re used, as well as whether they complete assigned tasks.

These evaluations remain proposals to develop and test with scientists, rather than established solutions. But they offer a direction for our work at Ai2: building open scientific tools means making their behavior and limitations easier to investigate and giving the research community a role in defining what trustworthy performance looks like.

Verification is only one consideration in using AI tools for science. Respondents also raised a more fundamental question—what happens to scientific expertise when AI takes over parts of the research process?

An early-career biologist described deliberately limiting AI use while acquiring skills they hoped to teach future students. Another researcher questioned whether producing a correct result was sufficient if the researcher learned nothing in the process: even with perfect outputs, “did the person who obtained the perfect outputs get anything out of this?”

Some participants were concerned that AI-assisted research could diminish learning, though the value of automation depends in part on the researcher and the task. An experienced scientist may reasonably delegate a familiar procedure to save time, while someone still learning that procedure may benefit from doing the work themselves.

Participants also drew different boundaries around activities involving intellectual contribution. One biologist refused to use AI to generate research ideas, considering that a responsibility to retain personally. Others were more open to AI-assisted ideation or writing. An anthropologist, for example, argued that some sections of scientific papers, particularly routine descriptions of methods and results, are formulaic enough to benefit from automation.

Even researchers who tried AI for writing sometimes reconsidered. One political scientist described using Claude to help revise a manuscript, then deciding to rewrite the entire paper because the boundaries of their own intellectual contribution had become unclear. “I don’t know what was AI and what was me,” the researcher said.

Context mattered, too. Having AI critique a manuscript before submission was viewed favorably by some participants, while using it to review another researcher's unpublished work raised concerns. The distinction involves more than technical capability; peer review carries responsibilities around confidentiality, attribution, and the fair evaluation of others' research.

These disagreements suggest that responsible automation cannot be defined by a universal list of acceptable and unacceptable tasks. Scientific fields have different norms, and individual researchers may have different goals even when performing similar work.

The same tension applies to safeguards built into AI systems. One political scientist described how content restrictions that prevent AI tools from discussing certain topics could interfere with legitimate research on political violence or human rights violations. Meanwhile, participants working with sensitive human-subject data wanted stronger guarantees about privacy, access, and how information would be used.

Both are valid concerns. An agent that acts without sufficient safeguards could expose confidential data or produce harmful results, while one that’s constrained too broadly could prevent scientists from pursuing legitimate lines of inquiry.

This is why scientific communities need a meaningful role in shaping the boundaries of AI assistance. Developers need to understand not only which activities an agent can perform, but which decisions researchers want to retain and where safeguards are necessary—as well as where those safeguards might obstruct valuable work.

Our findings point toward an approach to scientific AI development that starts with the people who will use the technology.

Researchers should be able to examine an agent's contributions, determine which work to assign, and preserve opportunities to develop their own expertise. Achieving that will require technical features such as transparent records of agent actions and granular data controls, alongside evaluations informed by the standards and practices of different scientific communities.

It will also require continued collaboration. Our study is exploratory; the findings come from interviews with 17 US-based scientists, not a representative survey of scientific opinion or an empirical evaluation of specific AI agents. Participants discussed both the generative AI tools they use today and what they anticipate from more autonomous systems. Their perspectives identify important concerns and design priorities, rather than establishing a consensus about how AI should be used in science.

Still, they offer a path forward. At Ai2, we believe AI can accelerate scientific discovery while preserving the openness, reproducibility, and independent scrutiny that make research trustworthy. Through our broader efforts to develop and evaluate scientific agents, we aim to put those principles into practice. That means working with scientists to identify worthwhile uses for agents and translating their concerns into concrete evaluations, in addition to making it easier to understand both what these systems accomplish and how they accomplish it.

The promise of scientific AI isn't simply that researchers can pass off more work. It's that they can pursue questions that might otherwise be out of reach while retaining the knowledge, judgment, and agency needed to make discoveries that are their own.

## 10. An Indic Voice Agent with CUGA

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-25 07:22
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-research/an-indic-voice-agent-with-cuga

Most useful knowledge is written and digitized in English. Most of the people who need it speak, and think, in their own regional languages like Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Gujarati, Marathi and many others. Many would rather talk than type.

We built a small voice agent to close that gap, using CUGA as the agent harness. This post explains the use case and what we learned. You can try it yourself in CUGA Apps.

A farmer asks a question aloud, in their own language: how do I tell good seed from bad? The agent:

- Listens and turns the speech into text.

- Searches a 154-page agricultural handbook, which is written in English.

- Answers in two to four spoken sentences, in the language the question was asked in, using only what the handbook says.

- Speaks the answer back and shows the source pages, so the answer can be checked.

If someone asks about cricket or coding, it politely steers them back to farming instead of making something up.

CUGA (ConfigUrable Generalist Agent) is IBM Research's open-source agent harness. It handles the model wiring, tool management, state and guardrails, so you write tools and a prompt instead of the agent loop. Here we use it as an embedded SDK inside a small web app, for three things:

- One agent, any model. Switch between watsonx and OpenAI with one setting.

- The knowledge engine. Parse a PDF, chunk it, embed it, and search it locally with agent.knowledge.search().

- Conversation threads. Each question gets a fresh thread, so one person's language or context never leaks into another's.

The key design idea is simple: CUGA is a text-in, text-out brain. Voice is two thin layers around it.

audio ──▶ speech-to-text ──▶ knowledge.search ──▶ CUGA (grounded answer) ──▶ text-to-speech ──▶ audio

Because the agent only ever sees text, you can build and test it with plain text first, then add the ears and the mouth. You can also swap speech providers without touching the agent. We support two: Sarvam AI's cloud speech APIs, called directly from the user's browser, and open-source models (SraVaani for speech-to-text, Svara for text-to-speech) running on your own server.

Adding speech is the easy part. The hard part is cross-lingual retrieval: a Hindi question has to find an English paragraph.

Search works by turning text into vectors and finding the nearest ones. CUGA's default embedding model is English-only, so it turns Hindi into vectors that carry almost no meaning. With it, a Hindi or Tamil question still retrieves five passages, but they are the wrong ones. The model, correctly told to answer only from what it was given, quietly says "the handbook doesn't cover that". Every layer behaves correctly, so there is no error and no warning.

The fix is to use a multilingual embedding model (we use intfloat/multilingual-e5-large), so the same meaning lands in the same place whatever the language. Conceptually simple, though wiring it into CUGA took a small workaround (shown in the tutorial). After that, one English document serves every language, and you do not need translated copies of anything.

Our advice: before you tune a single prompt, ask your agent one question in a non-English language and check that it finds the right pages.

A few small decisions mattered more than we expected:

- Write for the ear. Markdown, bullet points, citation tags and bracketed translations such as "millet (बाजरा)" all sound wrong when read aloud. The prompt asks the model not to produce them, and a light formatter strips any that slip through before speech.

- Keep guardrails cheap. Simple input checks catch obvious off-topic questions. If one gets through, the model starts its reply with OFF_TOPIC, and the app swaps in a polite redirect. No second LLM call, so the voice path stays fast.

- Ground every answer. The model sees only the retrieved passages, is told not to use outside knowledge, and the page sources are shown to the user.

- Keep keys out of the server. With the Sarvam option, the browser talks to Sarvam directly. The server receives only text and never sees the user's key.

El organismo que otorga un premio a quien logre solucionar el problema declaró que, aparentemente, el caso ya estaba resuelto, pero que su revisión llevaría tiempo.

Pero horas antes del anuncio, Tristan Buckmaster, matemático de la Universidad de Nueva York, hizo públicas sus preocupaciones. Formó un equipo independiente con Levent Alpöge, matemático que trabajaba para la empresa rival Anthropic.

Ambos habían estado trabajando para lograr el mismo objetivo y guardaban sus borradores en Codex, la herramienta de codificación de OpenAI.

Buckmaster comentó que su compañero de investigación había recibido información sobre sus avances, aunque no especificó cómo. Preguntó si su investigación, almacenada en Codex, podría haber servido de base para el trabajo de OpenAI, aunque recalcó que no estaba acusando a nadie de nada.

OpenAI afirmó que nadie en la empresa, ni seres humanos ni IA, vio el trabajo de los otros equipos antes de su publicación.

Inicialmente, declaró que, si bien era improbable, no podía descartar que los datos anonimizados derivados del uso de sus productos hubieran contribuido a mejorar sus modelos. Días después, afirmó que las indicaciones del Codex de Buckmaster de los dos meses previos al anuncio "no pudieron haber influido en el sistema de ninguna manera".

Es una historia compleja; aún no sabemos con certeza qué sucedió. Pero pone de relieve un problema creciente sobre el uso de la IA en la ciencia.

Por lo general, estas palabras nos hacen imaginar robots creando terribles virus modificados genéticamente o armas químicas para aniquilar a la humanidad. Sin embargo, existe un problema más sutil que no ha acaparado la misma atención mediática: que la IA podría socavar la confianza en el proceso científico, planteando dudas sobre la novedad y la precisión de la investigación.

Y existe otra preocupación. Es bien sabido que los chatbots de IA como Claude o Gemini a veces pueden generar alucinaciones que simplemente no son ciertas. Te hablan de una película que nunca existió o de una cita que nunca se pronunció. Ahora hay indicios de que esto ocurre cada vez con más frecuencia en la ciencia.

Se teme que, con el tiempo, será difícil distinguir entre los verdaderos descubrimientos científicos y la ficción generada por la IA.

Y todo esto podría amenazar con erosionar, si no con hacer estallar, el pilar sobre el que se asienta la investigación científica: la confianza.

Un "momento decisivo"

Una de las amenazas recae sobre las revistas científicas, guardianas de la integridad científica, que parecen estar fracasando en su intento de hacer frente a un tsunami de distorsiones generadas por la IA.

"La IA generativa ha trastocado prácticamente todo el proceso científico", declara Dalmeet Singh Chawla, periodista científico que ha dedicado una década a informar sobre malas prácticas en la investigación.

En conversación con la BBC, explica que el personal de control de integridad de las editoriales —las personas que revisan los artículos para verificar su exactitud y fiabilidad— tendrá que "aumentar drásticamente [en número y esfuerzo] si quiere tener alguna posibilidad de éxito". Añade: "Ahora mismo, parece que no la tienen".

A primera vista, la IA parece la herramienta ideal para impulsar la investigación científica. Los científicos manejan enormes cantidades de información. Los telescopios y aceleradores de partículas, por ejemplo, recopilan miles de millones de datos, una cantidad inabarcable para el cerebro humano. La IA puede extraer los hallazgos científicos más relevantes.

El ejemplo paradigmático de la IA científica es la empresa británica Google DeepMind, que en 2020 resolvió uno de los mayores problemas de la biología: cómo se pliegan las proteínas de los seres vivos para adoptar sus formas precisas.

Puede sonar aburrido, pero las proteínas son las diminutas máquinas que hacen posible la vida, y las formas en que se pliegan determinan lo que pueden hacer; desde construir células y combatir infecciones hasta ayudar a formar los tejidos y órganos del cuerpo.

Antes de la IA, los científicos tenían que realizar experimentos laboriosos en el laboratorio. Google DeepMind desarrolló un modelo de IA llamado AlphaFold que predijo con precisión 200 millones de pliegues de proteínas en tan solo un año.

El trabajo experimental equivalente habría requerido cientos de millones de años en laboratorios. La empresa cedió los resultados gratuitamente a los investigadores. Tanto el director de DeepMind como el científico principal del proyecto recibieron el Premio Nobel de Química.

Hubo muchos abrazos por todas partes.

El Dr. Pushmeet Kohli, quien dirige el trabajo científico de DeepMind, me dijo que AlphaFold podría considerarse un "momento decisivo".

Dudas sobre el trabajo de la IA

Pero en otros ámbitos, el uso de la IA en la ciencia ha venido acompañado de acusaciones de exageración. Algunos modelos informáticos son acusados de sobrestimar la importancia de sus hallazgos, y los investigadores humanos que los supervisan terminan creyendo algunas de las afirmaciones sin el suficiente escepticismo.

Tras el éxito obtenido con AlphaFold, por ejemplo, DeepMind centró su atención en predecir la estructura de nuevos materiales. Este trabajo podría conducir al desarrollo de mejores baterías, células solares más económicas y componentes electrónicos que no dependan de elementos de tierras raras. (En teoría, nadie tendría que volver a discutir sobre derechos mineros y Groenlandia estaría a salvo de invasiones).

Un modelo de DeepMind generó, según la empresa, más de dos millones de nuevas estructuras cristalinas, anunciadas como "el equivalente a casi 800 años de conocimiento" publicado de una sola vez.

De esas, unas 380.000 se consideraron lo suficientemente prometedoras como para ser sintetizadas en un laboratorio.

Pero entonces llegó una señal de alerta de dos químicos de la Universidad de California.

Anthony Cheetham y Ram Seshadri analizaron una muestra aleatoria de los supuestos nuevos cristales. En un artículo publicado en la revista Chemistry of Materials, concluyeron que aún no habían encontrado ningún compuesto realmente novedoso, argumentando que muchos eran simples adaptaciones de materiales conocidos.

Su veredicto no fue que el método fuera erróneo, sino que los resultados necesitaban un análisis más exhaustivo y la validación de químicos.

DeepMind rechaza la acusación, afirmando que analizó cada compuesto para detectar su novedad. La empresa argumenta que descartar una nueva estructura por parecer familiar no tiene sentido: muchos de los materiales más útiles de la actualidad, desde componentes de teléfonos hasta protectores solares, se obtuvieron mediante el intercambio de un solo átomo.

"Basura" de investigación

En esos casos, al menos, la ciencia era en gran parte válida y el problema radicaba simplemente en que la IA podría estar exagerando su importancia.

Podría decirse que lo más preocupante es el creciente riesgo de que las revistas científicas se contaminen con lo que se denomina "basura" generada por IA, donde es una computadora, y no un científico humano, quien escribe la investigación, lo que da lugar a citas falsas y artículos fantasma.

En 2024, el científico informático Prof. Dmitry Kobak, de la Universidad de Gante, buscó en más de un millón de artículos de investigación biomédica palabras clave preferidas por los sistemas de gestión de la vida (SVL) que generan textos mediante IA.

Su equipo descubrió que, en diciembre del año pasado, casi el 90% presentaba indicios de haber sido generados por IA.

La tecnología de detección de IA no es perfecta; algunas versiones del software han etiquetado la Constitución de Estados Unidos, escrita en la década de 1780, como "generada por IA".

Aun así, Kobak afirmó estar sorprendido por la magnitud del aparente uso de la IA. Existe el riesgo, según le dijo a la revista Nature, de que "cualquier sesgo que pueda tener el SVL se extienda repentinamente por la literatura científica".

Kobak es uno de los muchos investigadores a quienes les preocupa que la IA esté acabando poco a poco con las revistas científicas.

Y es importante: los artículos de investigación son la base del progreso científico. Cuando se publica un descubrimiento, otro equipo en otra parte del mundo puede obtener financiación para desarrollar aún más los resultados.

El proceso de revisión por pares, en el que los artículos de investigación son revisados por otros científicos antes de su publicación, también se está viendo comprometido.

Un equipo liderado por Weixin Liang y James Zou en la Universidad de Stanford analizó los informes de los árbitros presentados a cuatro importantes conferencias de informática y descubrió que hasta el 17% del texto había sido modificado sustancialmente por un modelo de lenguaje.

Un aspecto fundamental de la integridad de un artículo científico es que los científicos principales citen investigaciones previas para demostrar que su trabajo es realmente novedoso.

Un equipo independiente de la Universidad de Cornell descubrió que los investigadores estaban utilizando inteligencia artificial para recopilar citas y que esta estaba generando investigaciones falsas, con alrededor de 147.000 estudios ficticios citados el año pasado.

Si la IA continúa distorsionando la integridad del proceso académico de esta manera, los investigadores se verán abocados a callejones sin salida, se malgastará el dinero público y los frutos de una investigación válida, como nuevos fármacos o tecnologías verdes, se retrasarán o no se materializarán en absoluto.

IA a la altura

Algunas editoriales de revistas científicas, como Elsevier, están tomando medidas para evitar esta creciente crisis de confianza.

El equipo de integridad de la investigación de Elsevier desarrolló unas 100 señales que indican que un artículo requiere una revisión humana más exhaustiva. Las revistas de Nature prohíben las imágenes generadas por IA y le dicen a los revisores que nunca suban un manuscrito a un sistema de IA. El sitio web arXiv, donde los científicos comparten sus resultados preliminares, suspende durante un año a los investigadores que presenten referencias inventadas.

La profesora Sarah Main, vicepresidenta de Elsevier, es una de las personas de la empresa que intenta determinar cómo utilizar la IA de forma responsable en la investigación.

"Lo que llamamos IA de grado de investigación se centraría en garantizar que el investigador tenga el control", aclara.

A medida que los agentes de IA procesan la enorme cantidad de datos que surgen de los aceleradores de partículas, el cosmos y el mundo natural, ¿cómo sabemos que lo están haciendo bien?

Y surge otra pregunta preocupante: ¿cómo sabemos que los agentes de IA no descartan un resultado importante como una anomalía?

Los descubrimientos de la penicilina, la radiactividad y las extrañas estrellas llamadas púlsares surgieron de resultados inusuales que fácilmente podrían haberse descartado. Las intuiciones humanas los convirtieron en algunos de los momentos científicos más importantes del siglo pasado.

¿Puede la IA replicar realmente esa sensación de serendipia e ingenio humano?

Kohli, de DeepMind, no cree que la solución sea darle la espalda a la IA. Eso sería "como decir que deberíamos calcular sin usar calculadoras", me comentó. Pero reconoce que debe haber controles humanos: "Los sistemas actuales son imperfectos. Alguien tiene que asumir la responsabilidad".

"Hay que entender lo que ha hecho el agente y asumir la responsabilidad. No basta con especificar el problema y dejar que la máquina redacte el informe y lo presente como cierto".

La profesora Andrea Pauli, del Centro de Biociencias de Viena (Austria), ha seguido de cerca esa prueba. Dedicó casi diez años a una pregunta que nadie había podido responder: cómo un espermatozoide penetra en un óvulo. El uso responsable de la IA la ayudó a encontrar una respuesta.

Su laboratorio descubrió una proteína en la superficie de los óvulos de pez cebra que apuntaba a un mecanismo de llave-cerradura que permite la entrada del espermatozoide.

Su caso es un motivo de esperanza, en medio de un mar de pseudociencia sobre la IA. Demuestra que la IA puede ayudar.

Pero esta es también una historia sobre seres humanos. Poco después de la creación de Basecamp —el laboratorio que visité en una antigua fábrica de relojes de Londres—, su cofundador, Oliver Vince, enfermó de leucemia en el hospital (de la que se recuperó). Esto lo motivó a desarrollar un sistema de IA con mayores probabilidades de curar el cáncer. Puede que no funcione, dice, pero vale la pena intentarlo.

"Tenemos una vía técnicamente viable para curar el cáncer", afirma.

No es una promesa de Vince, sino una hoja de ruta. La gran pregunta es si la IA impulsará el progreso científico o si, silenciosamente, erosionará los cimientos sobre los que se asienta.
