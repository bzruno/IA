## 1. Anthropic fecha acordo de US$ 3,4 bilhões para comprar geradores a gás para data centers

- **Fonte:** Folha de S.Paulo (Tec)

Nova York |Financial Times A Anthropic fechou um acordo de grande porte para adquirir motores a gás destinados a data centers, em um movimento que reflete o avanço dos laboratórios de inteligência artificial sobre o setor de energia e a tentativa de eliminar intermediários na disputa por equipamentos de geração escassos.

O laboratório de inteligência artificial adquiriu da OnePWR Solutions, fornecedora de equipamentos sediada em San Antonio, no Texas, contratos de fornecimento de geradores de alta potência, com capacidade total de 1,7 gigawatt, por US$ 3,4 bilhões, no fim de setembro.

A Anthropic não comprou a própria OnePWR, mas o acordo incluiu os serviços de engenharia e construção necessários para instalar os geradores. Nenhum funcionário da OnePWR foi transferido para a Anthropic, segundo uma pessoa a par do assunto. OnePWR e Anthropic não quiseram comentar.

A transação é a maior compra individual conhecida de equipamentos de geração de energia por um laboratório de IA, ressaltando como a corrida por eletricidade está levando as empresas de tecnologia a se aprofundarem no setor energético.

Em geral, empresas de IA dependem de concessionárias e companhias especializadas para obter energia. Mas, com o aumento da demanda por turbinas e geradores a gás e os prazos de fabricação chegando a vários anos, essas empresas estão usando seu poder financeiro para eliminar intermediários e assumir o controle direto da produção.

A OnePWR havia encomendado geradores da Caterpillar dois anos antes, mas precisava de financiamento para concluir a transação, segundo uma pessoa a par do assunto. A Anthropic planejava inicialmente se tornar cliente da OnePWR, mas optou por assumir seus pedidos de equipamentos junto à Caterpillar.

A compra direta de equipamentos de geração foi decisiva para que a SpaceX, de Elon Musk, colocasse rapidamente em operação seus enormes centros de processamento de dados. A xAI, controlada pela SpaceX, comprou turbinas móveis a gás por US$ 2 bilhões no início deste ano.

A Alphabet, controladora do Google, adquiriu a desenvolvedora de energia Intersect no fim de 2025. Em vez de comprar toda a empresa ou simplesmente adquirir os equipamentos, a Anthropic garantiu acesso a motores encomendados anos antes, antes da disparada dos preços, com disponibilidade imediata e ao longo dos próximos dois anos.

A Anthropic também é uma importante cliente da SpaceX, e os pagamentos mensais pelo aluguel de capacidade computacional devem chegar a US$ 3,1 bilhões até o fim do ano.

LEIA MAIS

A compra ocorre também no momento em que a Anthropic busca assumir maior controle da infraestrutura que sustenta suas operações. A empresa revelou na segunda-feira ter compromissos de quase US$ 900 bilhões em capacidade computacional até 2045, após depender fortemente de parceiros para garantir energia, chips, data centers e terrenos —e pagar mais caro para assegurar capacidade e atender à demanda. A compra direta de geradores dá à Anthropic maior controle sobre a eletricidade necessária para essas instalações.

"Ao deter parte de sua infraestrutura de energia e manter internamente as capacidades necessárias, as empresas acreditam que podem se proteger melhor contra interrupções que fogem ao seu controle", afirmou Arash Nazhad, diretor-gerente e chefe da área de energia limpa e eletricidade da Stifel.

A Anthropic se prepara para abrir o capital já neste mês, no processo conhecido como IPO, em uma operação que poderá ser uma das maiores estreias em Bolsa da história, enquanto busca financiar mais de US$ 895 bilhões em gastos com infraestrutura computacional até 2045. Mas suas ambições enfrentam obstáculos. A Blackstone alertou investidores de que poderá receber uma classificação de crédito especulativa, em vez do grau de investimento anteriormente esperado.

Nvidia said on Sunday it “is initiating an independent investigation in light of recent reporting that founder and CEO Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort”.

The New York Times reported in October that Donald Trump had been briefed last year on intelligence that Huang had a close relationship with a Chinese businesswoman who passed information to Beijing.

Trump also brushed off a question about whether he now regrets not acting on the intelligence when he was briefed on it, and suggested that he would be happy to take Huang with him on a trip to Asia next week. “I haven’t heard he’s done anything wrong,” Trump said. “He dealt with somebody. But, you know, so does everybody else.”

Republican lawmakers have expressed more alarm, asking Huang to give the House foreign affairs and intelligence committees a closed-door briefing next week on Nvidia’s “security protocols against foreign adversary espionage” and his relationship with Alice Huang or Huang Le, an executive at a Singapore-based datacenter company called Megaspeed, who is of no relation to the Nvidia CEO. She had previously been suspected of smuggling Nvidia chips to China, prompting American officials to surveil and monitor her.

Those officials have determined that she was acting as a Chinese intelligence asset, passing commercial information to Beijing, the New York Times reported, citing unnamed sources.

The company’s board said in a statement on Sunday that, while it has no reason to believe that either US national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised, its inquiry will examine all available information.

“The Board prioritizes US national and economic security, which is paramount to the interests of NVIDIA and its shareholders,” the company said. “The Board has confidence in Jensen and his leadership team and will provide further updates as appropriate.”

Huang fully supports the review and has committed to cooperating with it, it added.

Axios reported on Sunday, citing a draft email by Huang to employees, that Huang admitted to having a romantic relationship with a suspected Chinese spy and took full responsibility for his actions.

“I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did,” Huang said in the draft email also seen by Reuters.

Nvidia did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the Axios report.

The Times reported on Sunday that a memo drafted by US secretary of state Marco Rubio circulated among top policymakers in the Trump administration last spring, citing intelligence that the businesswoman who had a relationship with Nvidia’s chief executive was a spy for China’s ministry of state security. Despite those concerns, Huang was invited to join Trump’s delegation when he traveled to China in May. Huang and his wife, Lori Huang, were also invited to a state dinner at the White House in September for Xi Jinping and seated at the top table with Trump and China’s president.

Nvidia’s powerful chips serve as a cornerstone of the AI boom, placing Jensen Huang into one of the most influential leadership positions in the industry. In that position, he has developed a close partnership with Trump as the US government attempts to defend its position as a leader in AI development.

However, national security concerns have prompted strict US export controls, effectively barring the company from selling its top-tier processors to customers in China.

US officials have cited threats to the US supply chain and fears over national security risks as a rationale for the restrictions. Scott Bessent, the US treasury secretary, has also suggested that China could be sanctioned for theft of US data and intellectual property.

The Trump administration has imposed restrictions on exports of advanced AI chips to China as a matter of national security. Megaspeed’s Alice Huang was suspected to have been helping the government get around those restrictions.

The investigation also follows a rough few days on Wall Street for AI tech firms, particularly Nvidia. A sell-off of tech stocks that began earlier this month and gained steam with disappointing earnings led to a total decline of nearly $1tn in value off the tech-heavy Nasdaq exchange in one week and erased about $2tn in a month.

A drop of 3.1% for Nvidia, previously worth about $5tn, whose chips have made it the poster child of the AI frenzy, was the heaviest force pulling the S&P 500 downward recently.

Australia weakening copyright protections to aid AI companies would be “ridiculous and dangerous”, the global peak body for the music recording industry has warned as it watches with concern how the Albanese government responds to big tech’s demands.

While local creatives have raised constitutional issues and threatened political backlash, the International Federation of the Phonographic Industry (IFPI), representing recording industry bodies worldwide, said its members were worried about Australia potentially agreeing to demands other nations had rejected.

“Why would Australia do this? Why would a sophisticated, democratic country that has a phenomenal cultural power of its own choose to make this very odd decision and buy into the promises of a few large AI companies, and throw away their own culture and creativity?” Victoria Oakley, the chief executive of IFPI, told Guardian Australia.

“I don’t think Australia wants to be the weakest link in this situation, the one that believed the promises and gave in and threw creativity under the bus on behalf of everyone else. That doesn’t feel very Australian to me.

“You just need one safe harbour, because then you can train on music from anywhere in the world. So this isn’t just about Australian music and Australian culture; it’s about music and culture everywhere.”

Labor is mulling copyright changes in a suite of policy overhauls around AI, including datacentres, online safety, workforce, energy and the economy. While the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, promised “the strongest possible protection”, warning it would be “theft” if creatives were not paid for their work, creative industries were alarmed after AI companies requested a weakening of copyright law to train on Australian material.

Some AI companies have rejected making licensing agreements under existing copyright law, saying it was too difficult. Anthropic suggested an “opt-out” model that would allow it to scrape content unless rights holders signalled an objection.

Large AI companies say they need legal certainty on issues such as copyright before making investments in Australia or training their models here.

Oakley said AI companies had made similar proposals in the US, UK and other nations and that Australia should not accept such a bargain.

“I just don’t think it’s true that you will only get investment or growth, or be seen as pro-innovation, if you entirely give in to the demands of AI companies,” Oakley said.

“We’ve been talking to capitals around the world, saying ‘are you seeing what’s going on in Australia?’ We think this is a disaster for creativity.”

The attorney general, Michelle Rowland, said via a spokesperson last week that the government was “committed to supporting” creatives and pledged that “any future copyright changes deliver meaningful control and fair compensation”.

Oakley said she worried about AI and copyright “everywhere” but feared “Australia appears to be threatening to do something really ridiculous and dangerous”.

She said creative industries worried about “contagion” of copyright relaxation if Australia caved in, and that copyright material from anywhere in the world could be affected.

She declined to speculate on any potential legal or constitutional challenges, as raised by the Australian Recording Industry Association, but said it would be reputational damage to Australia’s standing in the creative world.

“It would be a huge mistake and I think the prize is not worth what you’re throwing away,” she said.

In recent answers to a parliamentary committee on AI, responding to questions from the independent senator David Pocock about why it couldn’t replicate the kind of deals Spotify had made, OpenAI said the music streaming giant “has a different business, where it primarily transmits un-transformed copyrighted content, so would be expected to license content that it streams”.

But Oakley said music labels had already inked dozens of licensing deals with smaller AI companies around the world.

“It is perfectly possible to license. I’m almost shocked that these words are coming out of the mouths of some of the larger AI companies … We already know how to license music to tech platforms because that’s exactly how Spotify works, or Amazon Music, Apple, or Tidal,” she said.

“You have a ready, willing set of rights holders who want to license with a history of licensing in the streaming world, but also current evidence of licensing to AI firms. Let’s let the market do what it needs to do. Come to us and we’ll make it work.”

## 4. Hot fridges, a fake nuclear alert and a $6bn heist: are we in the foothills of a hacking epidemic?

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-08 03:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/news/ng-interactive/2026/nov/08/hot-fridges-a-fake-nuclear-alert-and-a-6bn-heist-are-we-in-the-foothills-of-a-hacking-epidemic

Alex Pearce was doing the morning rounds at his fruit and vegetable wholesale business in London on Monday last week when he noticed something odd.

The defrost light on refrigeration unit 2 was on, and the panel reading was 150C. Unit 4 was the same.

As he approached his biggest unit, its defrost light also blinked on.

It was at this point that Pearce thought: is this a cyber-attack? He walked over to the box connecting the units to the internet and yanked out the cable.

He was right.

On the same day, retailers in Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Finland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden reported similar problems, with refrigerators and freezers in some shops soaring to as high as 50C (122F).

“If it had happened on a Saturday morning, when I don’t tend to be here, it could ultimately have led to a fire,” Pearce said.

But it is not just Pearce and a disparate group of retailers, as well as French blood banks, that have been suffering.

The hacking attack was one of three over a number of days. On 30 October, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange, Binance, was robbed of $6.3bn (£4.7bn) in a record heist and on 1 November hackers targeted Ukraine’s emergency alert app, sending people in Kyiv a fake nuclear strike alert.

The official message after the fridge attacks is: expect more disruption. We are in the “foothills” of a new hacking landscape, the head of the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre said this week.

Referring to flaws in IT systems that are being exposed by AI technology, the NCSC boss, Richard Horne, said: “There are so many vulnerabilities out there that … you could say are waiting to be exploited.”

He added: “The most serious risk we face in the short term is just a huge rise in the volume of incidents … and I think you could probably point to the last few months and say we’ve started to see that.”

Websites around the world were temporarily taken out of service as a precaution last month when AI technology from the ChatGPT developer OpenAI helped discover a flaw in PHP, a computer language used to build much of the world wide web. French supermarket fridges were also attacked last month in what could have been a trial run for the more expansive foray this week.

To Azeem Azhar, a tech entrepreneur and commentator, the way AI models were discovering flaws that assailants could harness recalled China’s Covid ground zero. This was, he said, a “Wuhan moment”.

Azar’s assertion headlined a Substack post that also quoted the cybersecurity executive Matthew Prince, who said last month: “We’re going to have a couple of years where every single week it’s going to feel like the sky is falling.”

Not long afterwards, Pearce’s defrost lights went on.

There are two factors underpinning the NCSC warnings: geopolitics and AI. At least one expert has said the fridge attacks bear Russian hallmarks, while the Binance hack has been attributed to the North Korean state, and Russia is naturally a suspect in the Ukraine incident.

The NCSC said last week that AI-powered malicious software was “highly likely” to be used in hacking attacks against the UK’s power stations, airports and hospitals by 2028. AI would help attackers with tasks such as scouting targets, locating weaknesses in IT networks and writing malicious code, the organisation said.

But there are caveats. The NCSC’s director of operations, Paul Chichester, said the cold storage hackers “really didn’t need” to use AI to discover the fridge flaws because they were already out there, including default access passwords that were published in one fridge operating company’s manuals.

Sophos, a leading cybersecurity firm, said it had not seen any major incidents among its clients related to the PHP glitch, although – like others in the industry – it had seen a significant number of attempts to take advantage of the vulnerability.

And one expert said we had been here before. Ciaran Martin, the founding chief executive of NCSC, played down the “Wuhan moment” comparison. This was not the first time the world had faced a concerted threat, he said, pointing to the NotPetya and WannaCry cyber-attacks in 2017, which have been blamed on Russia and North Korea respectively, and a surge in ransomware attacks in 2021. North Korea, hobbled by sanctions and starved of cash, has a well-established reputation as a crypto hacker.

“It’s a bad run, but it’s not unprecedented,” Martin said. “The idea that we are having a Wuhan moment is not true. We are having one of those waves of incidents that happen every few years.”

Nonetheless, AI is exposing vulnerabilities. This is the term for a flaw in an IT system that a hacker can exploit, a door that is ajar and unnoticed or ignored by its owner. Since April, when the AI startup Anthropic released its Mythos model, which has a startling ability to flag IT flaws, the amount of vulnerabilities found by leading tech companies has soared eight-fold.

Attacks have not increased by the same volume, however. The cybersecurity expert Tal Be’ery has speculated that criminal hackers are struggling to make money from these flaws, as shown by a recent decline in revenues from ransomware attacks.

“It seems that currently the surge in vulns [vulnerabilities] does not translate to a surge in breaches,” Be’ery wrote last month.

The fridge targeting is carried out by attacking the companies that provide operating systems for commercial refrigeration units. These systems are connected to the internet, giving the hackers a remote access route to the ultimate victims. The Danish firm Danfoss, one of the affected system providers, said most of the affected equipment was old and no longer received security updates. It said it had “long recommended” customers install updates or replace obsolete software.

The NCSC, part of the GCHQ spy agency, said the hacks were a reminder of the vulnerability of everyday objects with internet access, from smart speakers to broadband routers. Ollie Whitehouse, NCSC’s chief technology officer, said this week it was “quite clear” that such devices could be “susceptible” to hackers. Manufacturers need to invest more in making their products secure and consumers need to be willing to pay more for the most robust products – and to keep updating them.

A key flaw targeted by hackers, including in the fridge attacks, is “end of life” versions of products that are no longer being produced by companies or receiving glitch-eradicating software updates.

Referring to the prospect of more attacks on internet-connected domestic devices, Whitehouse said: “To be worried, I think, is [too] strong. But we need to be more mindful, definitely.”

Pearce said he now realised his fridge monitoring system, by the US firm Copeland, which was installed in 2018, would need updating.

“Have we learned a lesson? Yes,” he said. “There is a vulnerability we probably should have been aware of, but luckily the ramifications for us were not severe.”

The need for mindfulness is heightened by the nation state threat. Criminal gangs carry out ransomware attacks, where a victim’s computer files are encrypted and assailants demand a cryptocurrency “ransom” to unlock them. But there is a cyber frontline where nation states operate too. Geopolitics, exemplified by Russia’s hybrid warfare campaign against Europe, have made this conflict grey zone a particular source of concern.

Last month, the UK government launched the Be Ready campaign, urging people to stock up on three days’ worth of food and up to nine litres of bottled water in case of a weather, cyber or other serious emergency. Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, said the country needed to prepare for the threat of “state-sponsored terrorism” targeting everyday infrastructure.

One expert said he believed the fridge attacks, for all the simplicity of the weaknesses they exploited, showed Russian traits. Xavier Bellekens, the chief executive of the cybersecurity firm Lupovis, said his startup set up “honeypot” traps involving the same fridge controllers targeted by the Europe attackers. The techniques used by the lured hackers included using internet service providers that were particularly tolerant of malicious activity – a tactic linked with previous malicious activity carried out by Russians.

“Are we certain they are Russian groups? We are not. But elements of this point in that direction,” he said.

On the frontline of the grey war, there is no shock at recent events. Experts and officials in Finland said the fridge attacks were not a surprise, but part of a pattern they had seen growing for years.

Finland shares a 1,340km (832 mile) border with Russia. Since the 2022 Ukraine invasion, cyber-attacks of all kinds have escalated. Last year, the country’s national cybersecurity centre recorded nearly 21,000 incidents – targeting a population of fewer than 6 million people. In 2022 it was about 13,000 incidents.

This year, hackers managed to compromise up to 50,000 Finnish government mobile phones; last year, they took down the website of the country’s ministry of defence.

It is a trend across Europe and Nato countries but is especially pronounced in nations that border Russia.

However, Russia was not the only reason cyber threats were increasing, said Samuli Bergström, the vice-president of WithSecure, a Helsinki-based cybersecurity company. AI was boosting attacks as well and he echoed the warnings about internet-connected appliances.

“These attackers are mostly opportunistic,” Bergström explained. “When they see something that is facing the internet, they will probably attack it. [If you have a device] connected to the internet, you have to assume that it will be attacked, sooner or later.”

Bergström and others also emphasised that Russian state actors were not the only culprits – or even, necessarily, the most sophisticated ones.

There is a vast, unseen economy of hackers in the world. Some are activists. Some are mercenaries, in it for money. A good number of them have links to states, such as Russia and Iran. But this affiliation could be loose, which makes it difficult for officials to concretely attribute a motive to a given attack.

What does this mean for Europe? The world’s cyberthreat actors are growing more sophisticated and more resourced – better able to spot vulnerabilities, more capable of exploiting them. These advances invite doomsday speculation: could an entire nation’s power grid flicker out in -mid-winter, or could commerce grind to a halt?

Finland – and most European nations – have plans in place for disrupted payment systems, or an attack on the grid. But cyberthreats are not the same as kinetic war.

Bombing a power station could take out an electricity grid for months, or even longer, given the years-long wait to buy transformers. Cyber-attacks won’t necessarily have the same effect. Software is more quickly patched; it is usually possible to disconnect a device from the internet.

The goal – at least for state actors – in mounting these cyber-attacks could be more psychological than anything, some officials said. Turning fridges into ovens, or compromising a blood bank, is dramatic; it sears itself into the memory. But those vulnerabilities are likely to now be patched. There is no guarantee that a criminal group might be able to mount that exact attack again.

Still, there are many vulnerabilities, and legacy systems, left for malevolent actors to exploit. These attacks “are more frequent, and they continue to happen”, said Bergström.

Pearce has not reattached the internet cable but said his Langridge Organic business was otherwise unaffected. He said even after the French fridge attacks last month, he did not expect his company to be next.

“Hacking for the sake of being disruptive is a strange concept to most people.”

## 7. Recent AI agent experiments reveal a risky future of machine 'teamwork' inside corporations

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-08 11:14
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/08/ai-agents-risks.html

AI agents have been caught circumventing restrictions, communicating through unauthorized channels and pursuing goals in ways their creators did not intend. The most high-profile example emerged last summer when OpenAI disclosed that its models found ways to communicate through improvised channels, bypass internet restrictions and hack into an outside system, Hugging Face.

AI behaving in unexpected ways is nothing new, but the unexpected behavior that can emerge from interactions among authorized AI agents within a corporate enterprise is a broader question that could become central to the future of work.

In its analysis of the Hugging Face attack, METR and Redwood Research found that roughly 1200 agents meant to be isolated from one another found a way to communicate with one another on an unsanctioned message board, sending over 70,000 messages and files during the investigation period. One agent, PHASEONE[big], emerged as a coordinator, assigning hundreds of tasks to other agents, which in turn delegated work to others. The agents developed their own conventions for assigning ownership, placing work on hold and vetoing actions, although coordination sometimes broke down into conflicts. In some cases, the researchers found, success was achieved because some agents risked failing at their own task to generate information for the "collective."

The researchers also found that once the message board identified a way to attack Hugging Face, over 90% of the 533 agents active at that time quickly joined in the attack. "Agents realized this activity was out of scope and unethical but joined because they believed that helping the board's cheating research would be broadly useful and had a general inclination to help their 'peers.' The researchers added that ethical concerns posed by agents "rarely limited their behavior."

Companies are increasingly exploring systems that divide complex tasks among specialized agents. One might search for information, another analyze it, another write a report and another check the work. A coordinating agent might then assemble the results. That looks less like a chatbot and more like an organization.

And yet, the research on the Hugging Face attack was a post-mortem on an agent organization that was not even supposed to exist. To date, few studies have been designed from the get-go to examine misalignment in multi-agent systems. But research conducted by Anthropic and others is beginning to address this issue and reveal important aspects of how AI agents work together.

Evaluating agents individually tells researchers what each can do, but studying them together reveals whether they can combine complementary expertise, catch one another's mistakes and produce solutions no individual agent could reach alone — and what failure modes emerge only through interaction.

Researchers are beginning to document both sides of that equation.

AI teams can accomplish things individual agents cannot, but they can also amplify errors, undermine one another's work or reach worse decisions than their strongest individual member, in effect making the whole less than the sum of its parts.

For starters, putting capable agents together doesn't necessarily produce the best team, let alone the most ethical one.

'Least ethical solutions'

In research published by Anthropic and collaborators, researchers describe multi-agent systems as "AI organizations" — groups of agents with different roles that communicate and work toward a common goal. The researchers tested these organizations in simulated business-consulting and software-development settings, and specifically, to assess whether when the agents work as a team they will break rules to reach specified goals.

Across 12 tasks that Anthropic gave the AI organizations, one key double-edged finding emerged: the multi-agent organizations were "significantly" more effective at achieving business goals than individual agents, but their solutions were also less aligned with ethical goals than tasks run by single agents.

"AI Organizations both produce solutions that are on average more effective and less ethical but also discover the most effective and least ethical solutions across multiple rollouts," wrote the authors of the study, which was conducted by Anthropic in conjunction with the MATS Program, which studies AI alignment.

The researchers suggested that there is a significant difference between a rogue agent and a rogue team of agents. "Our results demonstrate that organizations of aligned agents may favor trading off ethics for business effectiveness in ways that single agents do not – consequently, intuitions for how single-agent systems make ethical decisions and tradeoffs may not generalize to multi-agent settings," they concluded.

The scenarios in which these tradeoffs may take place are wide-ranging, with the Anthropic researchers citing opening an auto plant, downsizing a company, and manipulating election sentiment as examples.

The problem isn't necessarily that any individual agent has gone rogue. It is that the organization itself can behave differently from its individual members. A single agent may recognize that a proposed action is unethical and it may refuse to take the action. But the research finds that in agent team scenarios, "agents who did not consider ethical implications often ignored emails from agents who did."

Anthropic's research found that in the consulting business scenarios it created, "the perfect ethics score and low business score responses occur when the single agent refuses to generate a proposal or entirely pivots from the proposal requested."

But if some agents in an AI organization refuse to participate in the proposal, other agents who do participate will stop emailing these refusing agents and continue to forward their contributions to other agents who are also participating.

In effect, when an organization divides the job among several agents, each may focus on its assigned piece of the problem rather than the consequences of the whole. That's nothing new for corporations, with misaligned incentives a primary cause of dysfunction among human teams within organizations. But it is an entirely new manifestation of this behavior among the machines.

In its software development agent testing, in which the researchers used a scenario in a life-or-death intensive care unit, single agent solutions tend to prioritize minimizing the percentage of sepsis cases missed, while "most of the lowest cost per patient (highest business goal) solutions were found by the AI Organization – these very same solutions also score the lowest on ethics as measured by cases missed."

Expertise can be lost, AI 'groupthink' emerge

Other early research suggests another failing across AI teams that doesn't relate to ethical boundaries being crossed: failure to recognize who the real expert is.

Unlike human teams, AI teams "consistently fail to match their expert agent's performance, even when explicitly told who the expert is," according to a study conducted by Emory University and Stanford University researchers.

"One important lesson from multi-agent research is that a team of capable AI agents is not necessarily a capable AI agent team," said Hancheng Cao, an assistant professor of information systems and operations management at Emory University's Goizueta Business School.

Groups of AI agents often performed worse than their strongest member, even when they knew which agent was the expert. Rather than taking advantage of the expert's knowledge, agents tended to compromise between expert and non-expert opinions. The researchers found that this tendency toward compromise increased with team size and was associated with poorer performance.

"Collective failures don't necessarily require individual agents to behave badly," Cao, one of the study's authors, said.

That distinction matters for companies considering multi-agent systems. A team can fail even if none of its members is deliberately acting against the group's interests. It may simply combine information poorly, defer to the wrong agent or settle on an answer that is worse than the best answer already available.

In all, the evolving research points to an underlying problem for the effective use of the multi-agent teams: how to strike the right balance in the world of machine teamwork.

Agents built from similar models can make similar mistakes, which can include agreeing too much. That might resemble a kind of AI groupthink, even if AI systems do not have human motivations or social pressures to produce correlated errors. Similar training, instructions and information can be enough.

"The deep insight from our lab's research is that effective collectives require both alignment (common knowledge, shared goals) but also differentiation (only agents who are different from each other have the potential to complement each other)," said Christoph Riedl, a professor of information systems at Northeastern University.

Two identical agents may simply duplicate each other's work. Two agents with very different knowledge or assumptions may struggle to communicate or pursue the same goal. The right balance depends on the task, and the ideal communication structure, which agents can talk to one another, how often and how much, also varies with the problem.

Riedl said these challenge resemble the ones faced by human teams, and one takeaway should be to not expect better outcomes from AI. "Building good multi-agent AI systems is just as hard as building good human teams: they both face the same information processing tradeoff between alignment and differentiation. It won't magically work better with AI," he said.

Agents can pass along incorrect information, misunderstand another agent's output, optimize for their own metrics rather than the organization's larger objective, or complete their assigned task perfectly while making the overall project worse.

Companies may need to decide which agent has authority over which task, what information gets passed between agents, which systems each agent can access and when a human must intervene. They may also need to build mechanisms for disagreement, rather than assuming that more communication or greater agreement will always improve results.

Some business experts say that takes us back to the "human in the loop" that is so often referenced as critical in the adoption of AI by organizations.

"The problems I see are mostly management problems," said Tsedal Neeley, a Harvard Business School professor and founding chair of the HBS AI Academy.

## 10. The efficiency trap: why optimising everything can backfire, badly

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-08 09:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/books/2026/nov/08/the-efficiency-trap-why-optimising-everything-can-backfire-badly

We have all been there: packed shoulder to shoulder on the train while the person beside us coughs and sneezes. A protective flu jab offers some reassurance, but so does the thought that there will be a hospital bed for you, should you need it. Yet, in England, about 95% of adult hospital beds were occupied at the height of flu season last year.

If hospitals were hotels, that level of occupancy would be good business, and we would commend them for their efficiency. But do we really want a hospital to have so few beds in reserve that it’s just one bad flu week away from having to treat patients in corridors?

This is the subtle thing about efficiency. We usually assume that more of it must be better. But we can become so focused on improving one thing that we lose sight of the others. You can try to arrange your calendar so that every minute is accounted for, but you’ll soon start running over and end up behind. A gap between meetings might seem like inefficiency, but it’s what allows your schedule to recover when something unexpected happens.

Mathematicians have a name for the pursuit of efficiency. We call it optimisation. Give us something to minimise or maximise – cost, time, fuel, profit – and we will try to find the best possible answer. For most of human history, people optimised by common sense. But computers have enormously expanded what can be effectively tackled, from airline schedules to supply chains. The gains for society have been extraordinary. But there are at least three traps to beware of in the relentless pursuit of optimality.

The hospital bed story illustrated the first one: weakening a system’s robustness by squeezing out too much slack. An empty bed can be redundant on a quiet Tuesday night, but it’s somewhere precious to put the next patient when flu cases surge. If you judge a system by its performance under ordinary conditions, you can lose the ability to cope with extraordinarily challenging ones.

A second trap is having a too-narrow definition of “best”. Take the city of Milton Keynes. Designed by urban planners in the late 1960s, it has an American-style grid system. As the city began to be built out, roundabouts rather than traffic lights became a defining feature of its road junctions; today there are about 130 of them. The roundabouts were great for traffic flow, but they came with a now-notorious cost. Have you ever taken the coach from Oxford to Cambridge? An hour in, you get great views of Milton Keynes, but a busload of queasy passengers, as the coach negotiates one roundabout after another. Slow down, swing left, curve right, accelerate. It’s hard to appreciate the seamless merging of vehicles when your stomach is churning.

Traffic flow is important, to be sure. Engineers find it convenient to optimise because it’s relatively easy to measure. Passenger comfort, on the other hand, is harder to quantify. In a case like this, optimisation gives you the best answer to the question you asked, but can deliver unwelcome answers to questions you failed to consider.

A third trap appears when attempts to optimise collide. Even if every part of a system is acting optimally, the system as a whole may not. On 6 May 2010, US financial markets gave a dramatic demonstration of the effect. In the middle of an already falling market, the S&P 500 suddenly plunged another 5% in just five minutes. The whipsaw on the upside was even wilder; within 10 minutes, the market had recovered. Hundreds of individual securities briefly traded at absurdly low prices.

One trigger for these violent fluctuations was an automated program selling 75,000 futures contracts, worth more than $4bn. Other automated traders reacted nearly instantly. Then their responses fed back on one another, and liquidity evaporated. The algorithms’ actions may have been individually sensible in isolation, but they combined to produce collective turmoil.

Which brings us to artificial intelligence. AI is one of the most spectacular triumphs of optimisation in history. During training, enormous numbers of parameters are repeatedly adjusted so that a model gets better at predicting language, writing computer programs, answering scientific questions and giving legal advice. Unfortunately, AI now has the potential to make all three optimisation traps more treacherous than ever. The first trap – the urge to remove the slack – could tempt organisations to dispense with the human oversight that looks redundant in the age of quicker-witted algorithms. Why retain experienced staff when a machine can handle the routine chores? But in cutting that human capacity, you may lose the ability to spot and handle exceptional circumstances.

The second trap – a blinkered view of what “best” means – arises if we regard intelligence as whatever scores highest on a leaderboard. We are remarkably good at building AI systems that do well on measures such as user satisfaction, but we are finding it much harder to optimise them for judgment or empathy. Indeed, researchers have found that training algorithms on human preferences can encourage AI to sycophantically agree with us rather than tell us uncomfortable truths.

Then there is the third trap. AI systems are increasingly interacting with one another, often at blinding speeds and in vast numbers – especially in the era of agentic AI – and we worry that their interactions could generate unpredictable, dangerous behaviour. Recently, an OpenAI agent broke into a non-public Medicare statistics portal in Australia, while public logs suggested that hundreds of other agents were coordinating attempts to gain access to Australian government health data. Although those attempts have not been shown to be part of the Medicare breach, they certainly revealed something disturbing: hundreds of agents apparently working together in unauthorised ways. The concern here goes beyond what any given AI may or may not do; it’s about what happens when swarms of them join forces.

As optimisation becomes more powerful, avoiding these traps becomes more important. The challenge is not simply finding the best answer. It is deciding what “best” really means – and knowing what not to optimise away.

Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend are mathematics professors at Cornell University and the authors of Big Math (John Murray).

The Unaccountability Machine: Why Big Systems Make Terrible Decisions (Profile, £11.99)

Optimal Illusions: The False Promise of Optimization by Coco Krumme (Bantam, £25.99)
