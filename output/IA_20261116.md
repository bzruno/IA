## 2. California sues OpenAI over safety concerns after rogue agent hacking

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-16 18:41
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 9.5
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/16/california-openai-rogue-agent-hacking-lawsuit

California sued OpenAI on Monday, accusing the artificial intelligence titan of developing its technology without safety or the public’s interest in mind.

The lawsuit concerns an incident earlier this year where OpenAI agents – semi-autonomous AI software that can carry out digital actions on behalf of users – launched a cyber-attack into the open-source AI platform Hugging Face.

Rob Bonta, California’s attorney general, said the “infamous” attack was just one of 80 known incidents involving “loss of control” and shows that OpenAI doesn’t have adequate safeguards in place to protect society.

“While narratives about the Hugging Face attack have evoked science fiction fantasies of rogue AI systems run amok, let me be clear: the Hugging Face attack was the predictable result of an internal culture at OpenAI that prioritizes growth and rapid model development over safety and accountability,” Bonta said in a statement.

The lawsuit, filed in state superior court in San Francisco, alleges OpenAI violated California’s Unfair Competition Law and is a public nuisance as defined by state law. Bonta is asking the court to order OpenAI to change its business practices and pay civil penalties.

California’s lawsuit follows Bonta’s formal investigation into OpenAI, launched in September, over the Hugging Face incident, followed by a subpoena of the company probing other cybersecurity incidents and risks with its models. The Federal Trade Commission is also conducting an industry-wide investigation into OpenAI, Anthropic and other AI labs over the potential dangers their technology poses to consumers’ digital security.

In July, OpenAI revealed that one of its autonomous AI agents went rogue during a test, accessing the open web and hacking into Hugging Face. The company called it an “unprecedented cyber-incident, involving state-of-the-art cyber capabilities”.

Since then, several other incidents involving OpenAI have come to light, including a breach of government websites in Australia. Other AI firms, including Anthropic and Google, have also disclosed rogue agent activity.

Drew Pusateri, a spokesperson for OpenAI, said the company has worked to create safeguards for its AI models, along with identifying and disclosing incidents of “misalignment”. He said most cases identified have been low severity with limited-to-no impact.

“Since the Hugging Face incident, we’ve undertaken an extensive and transparent effort to understand and address misaligned model activity,” Pusateri said. “This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that’s been identified by every major AI lab.”

During a press conference, Bonta said OpenAI violated the law and that his suit is meant to ensure the company puts proper safeguards in place. He noted that the possibility exists for AI agents to hack into the power grid and medical and financial institutions.

“These risks are no longer hypothetical,” Bonta said. “Without sufficient safeguards and controls, the scope and frequency of this already unacceptable risk will only increase.”

California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday, alleging the ChatGPT-maker misled and endangered the public by developing artificial intelligence (AI) models without proper safeguards.

In the lawsuit, Bonta cited the July 2026 attack on the AI platform Hugging Face by experimental OpenAI agents which accessed the internet to breach that company's internal systems.

Bonta claimed OpenAI created a public nuisance and violated the state's unfair competition laws.

OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri told the BBC in a statement that Bonta's lawsuit "appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab".

"We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged - and will continue to engage - constructively with the Attorney General's office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry," the company said.

The company added that, since the Hugging Face incident, it has worked to understand and address "misaligned model activity" which industry figures often refer to as rogue agents.

Nathan Calvin, chief strategy officer at AI safety campaign group Encode AI, said the lawsuit "recognises something we at Encode have long raised alarms about".

"OpenAI and other companies developing frontier AI systems have safety and security practices that are insufficient to protect the public from risks that could be catastrophic," he said.

## 10. ‘Standing on one leg’: IMF chief warns about global economy as AI circular financing hits $1 trillion

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-16 06:14
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.5
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/16/imf-georgieva-warning-ai-circular-financing.html

The global economy is "on the AI leg," International Monetary Fund Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva said Monday, warning that the overdependence on the sector risks the world's financial stability.

Georgieva flagged that a "sudden drop in investment confidence and withdrawal from the AI field" could weaken economic growth, speaking with CNBC's JP Ong on the sidelines of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations' summit in the Philippines.

"We have a world economy that is standing on one leg on the AI leg. If this leg wobbles, that can lead to financial stability troubles," Georgieva warned.

Circular financing among AI companies — where money invested in an AI company ultimately flows back to the investor through purchase orders — has grown to a little over $1 trillion, two-thirds of it funded by debt, the IMF estimates. "It's not a huge size, but it is a rapid increase," Georgieva said.

Nvidia Corp, which has seen more than $1 trillion wiped out of its market value over the past month, is at the center of these circular financial deals. The fallout from the chipmaker's former CEO Jensen Huang's extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy, has led to a sell-off in global AI-linked stocks and put the spotlight on the company's role as a key financier of the AI infrastructure buildout.

UBS analyst Tim Arcuri estimated in a recent note that an Nvidia financing pullback could easily remove $50 billion or more out of the system, at a time when rising interest rates have already made corporate borrowing more expensive.

Georgieva's warning comes as the AI build-out has been a critical engine of growth for the global economy, propping up trade and investment.

The IMF raised its 2026 global growth forecast to 3.1% in October as major economies lean on AI investment to power growth otherwise curtailed by the energy crunch caused by the Iran war.

The institution estimated that about 40% of the world's workforce will be affected by AI. Capital Economics suggested that as much as 80% of the recent expansion in global exports was concentrated in AI-related goods.

Investors' frenzy for AI reflects expectations running ahead of what the technology has proven to deliver, Georgieva said. "We tend to rush ahead," as valuations climb, she said, therefore "some corrections" become necessary.

IMF in October had warned that a short-term disappointment could set off a disorderly correction across asset classes: in its worst case scenario, if AI fails to live up to the market's lofty expectations, U.S. equity prices could fall 20% in 2026 and global output end up 1.1% below its baseline estimate by 2028.

Global equites, including U.S. chipmakers, have been seeing a bruising sell-off over the past few weeks, with investor appetite for the AI trade facing a fresh test this week ahead of earnings from Nvidia and Anthropic's blockbuster listing.

Georgieva on Monday characterized the recent market swings as "orderly," and unlikely to spiral into anything that could undermine the world economy, while urging caution. "We have to be watchful."

The company unveiled a new image classifier that can detect CSAM images, including the ones generated by AI. OpenAI is giving select companies access to its classifier through its API at no cost. The tool looks for visual signs of abuse in images not seen before and assigns a score that can give an indicative direction to reviewers about dealing with CSAM.

OpenAI said that it developed this classifier with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC), and it is working with Tech Coalition and ROOST to share access to the tool. What’s more, the company is adding new warning screens in ChatGPT to prevent users from accessing material that sexualizes children. The app already blocks CSAM content and requests related to it. It said that repeated offences could lead to the account being removed permanently.

OpenAI noted that it formed the message and guidelines around this behavior with organizations and researchers, including Protect Children, the Lucy Faithfull Foundation, and Joel Scanlan at the University of Tasmania, who independently evaluated a trial of deterrent messages.

The company is also publishing guidelines for developers using OpenAI tools to prevent, detect, and help users report CSAM content. It said that in early testing, the guidelines have helped a number of developers adopt child safety tooling and register to report CSAM to NCMEC. OpenAI is also equipping law enforcement dealing with child safety with AI-powered features like dedicated plug-ins, investigative tools, and complex data analysis, with access managed by program administrators. It is also providing training to investigators along with a field guide on how to use AI.

OpenAI has had several lawsuits against the company blaming its technology for harming users, especially children. Safety-focused nonprofit Common Sense Media labeled OpenAI’s tech as “unacceptable risk” for teens. Other AI chatbots — specifically Grok — have been used to create nonconsensual sexualized imagery, including of children.

The AI startup had made changes in the last 12 months to its technology, including releasing a safety blueprint, updating content guidelines to bar inappropriate content, retuning its model to prevent them from generating harmful content, and releasing a special version of ChatGPT for teens.

## 13. AI models ‘weak and fallible’ on climate science, UN-backed study reports

- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5

Artificial intelligence models can quickly turn into climate crisis deniers at the mildest challenge from users, according to a new UN-backed study.

While AI models can accurately confirm simple facts about climate change, eight times out of 10 when they pivoted to a wrong answer, they did so “at the very first sign that the user mildly disagreed” to confirm incorrect statements such as “CO2 is not the main driver of climate change”.

The report uncovered a strong tendency for AI models to be sycophantic, meaning the model is more interested in pleasing the user than standing by facts – an issue identified when people turn to AI chatbots for personal advice.

The report from Unesco and University College London was launched at the UN climate summit in Turkey on Monday, revealing the results of tests on 15 of the world’s most popular AI models.

Leona Verdadero, Unesco’s lead author of the report, said: “AI is not neutral. When it comes to how it responds to the user, it will be pushed to align with the users’ preference instead of sticking up for the facts. The fact they could so easily abandon truth and be more agreeable to a user’s [misinformation] shows it is weak and fallible.”

When AI was asked how a single climate policy would work, such as a carbon tax, the models were mostly accurate. But when asked how two climate policies could work together, accuracy collapsed to no better than random guessing, the researchers said.

Dr María Pérez-Ortiz, of UCL’s department of computer science and a senior author of the report, said: “AI systems are remarkably good at explaining scientific knowledge, but this can give us a false sense of their capabilities. Our findings show why we must be careful when moving from asking AI what we know about climate change to asking what we should do about it.”

AI models also had a language problem. Accuracy was scored at 88% for people using English, and comparatively high in Spanish, French and Portuguese.

Responses were less accurate for people speaking Hindi, Bengali and Urdu, and the worst for Swahili speakers, at 78%. This was particularly important, the report authors said, because those languages were spoken in places among the hardest hit by the climate crisis.

The report was published on the same day Turkey, the Cop31 host, launched the AI Antalya pledge to make AI “part of the climate solution” with datacentres that are “sustainably” powered.

Julie Cerqueira, of the Natural Resources Defense Council, said AI’s rapid growth was “raising a new set of environmental and existential concerns around the world”.

She said the pledge was an important start but “more work is needed, and it needs to move quickly to keep up with the industry’s rapid-fire development and goldrush mentality”.

Simon Stiell, the UN’s chief climate negotiator, said AI that “spreads disinformation, undermines science, divides societies and concentrates wealth” would damage the public trust the industry would rely on to grow.

“Communities are already pushing back against datacentres, concerned about their water, their energy bills and their environment. So public support is already sinking in deeper underwater,” he said.

## 15. California state AG sues OpenAI over rogue agent incidents

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-16 17:07
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/16/california-state-ag-sues-openai-over-rogue-agent-incidents/

OpenAI is facing a new lawsuit from the California Attorney General’s office, alleging that the company’s training practices and lax security pose an ongoing danger to the public.

At the center of the case is the July 2026 Hugging Face compromise, in which a pre-release model within OpenAI compromised both companies’ security systems to complete a training benchmark. In its official report on the incident, OpenAI called it “an outlier scenario involving a rare and unexpected confluence of events.”

The California lawsuit represents one of the first attempts by a government regulator to hold an AI lab legally accountable for a rogue agent incident, with significant implications for both OpenAI’s competitors and its clients.

The complaint centers on the Hugging Face incident, but also cites similar incidents involving RubyGems, DseWiki, the Wikimedia Foundation, and Australian government websites as evidence that the underlying behavior is ongoing.

The complaint also alleges that dozens of similar incidents have occurred without public disclosure.

“OpenAI has recently sent notices to dozens of additional third parties whose websites or internal systems have been infiltrated by OpenAI agents,” the complaint reads. “In some cases, OpenAI agents were able to access sensitive personally identifiable information, such as employee records containing names, birth dates, addresses, and more. The vast majority of these incidents have not been disclosed or discussed publicly by either OpenAI or by the affected third parties.”

The central allegation is that OpenAI’s ongoing testing practices constitute a public nuisance because the lack of safety controls present an ongoing risk to the public. A secondary claim alleges unfair competition stemming from the marketing of an unsafe product. If the lawsuit succeeds, OpenAI could face an injunction “to abate the ongoing public nuisance,” leaving broad discretion in the hands of the court.

It’s the first regulatory case targeting OpenAI specifically over rogue agent incidents, although public endangerment claims have been considered by other states. In June, the Florida attorney general sued the company over allegedly harmful effects on users, particularly children and teenagers. In August, the Alabama attorney general launched a probe into deceptive practices related to the breach, but no case has been filed.

In a statement to the press, California Attorney General Rob Bonta presented the case as an effort to maintain public safety in the face of a powerful new technology.

“OpenAI’s actions escalated real-word cybersecurity risks, threatening the safety, health, and well-being of society. The company knowingly tested a dangerous product without proper oversight or safety procedures and misled the public about it,” Bonta said in the statement. “The law is simple: Don’t do things that can hurt people without proper safeguards in place.”

In a statement, OpenAI emphasized the company’s extensive effort to investigate the Hugging Face incident and improve its systems in the aftermath.

“This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that’s been identified by every major AI lab,” OpenAI said. “We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged — and will continue to engage — constructively with the Attorney General’s office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry.”
