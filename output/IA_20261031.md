A federal judge denied a motion by xAI seeking to dismiss an NAACP lawsuit accusing the SpaceX-owned company of failing to obtain permits needed to operate the gas turbines powering its Colossus data center outside Memphis.

Suing on behalf of the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center said the AI company had “installed and operated at least 35 combustion turbines and other sources of air pollution at the Colossus site without ever obtaining the necessary preconstruction or operating air permits.” It also accused xAI of violating the Clean Air Act.

xAI sought to have the lawsuit dismissed, claiming that the turbines are exempt from permitting requirements because they’re mobile. The company also argued that the plaintiffs did not have the standing to sue.

“Because the plaintiffs have statutory standing under the Clean Air Act, because the combustion turbines are not ‘mobile sources’ under Mississippi’s [State Implementation Plan], and because the citizen suit provision does not violate Article II of the Constitution or the private nondelegation doctrine, the plaintiffs’ claims will proceed,” Judge Debra M. Brown wrote in her decision.

Focusing on xAI’s claim that these kinds of citizen lawsuits are unconstitutional, Judge Brown wrote, “Because citizen suit plaintiffs sue on their own behalf, in their own name, and seek penalties that redress harm to themselves, they are not exercising the federal government’s executive power.”

TechCrunch has reached out to SpaceX for comment.

In a statement, the NAACP’s director of environmental and climate justice Abre’ Connor described this as “another important victory in our effort to hold xAI accountable.” (A similar motion to dismiss from the Department of Justice was denied earlier this month)

“The courts have repeatedly rejected attempts by xAI and the DOJ to dismiss the NAACP’s Clean Air Act case, affirming our right to pursue justice for communities impacted by pollution,” Connor said. “Our position has been clear: xAI cannot operate gas turbines without the required permits.”

The NAACP lawsuit is moving forward as public opinion seems to have turned sharply against data centers, with New York recently passing a moratorium on new data centers and other state and local governments considering similar measures.

The backlash also prompted Amazon and Microsoft to promise to stop using NDAs in their dealings with government agencies, and it could be a significant factor in the imminent U.S. midterm elections.

## 5. Spooky New Infostealer Malware Uses AI to ‘Autodox’ Victims

- **Fonte:** Wired AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-31 09:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.5
- **Link:** https://www.wired.com/story/kernel-panic-volta-autodoxer-ai-infostealer-malware/

Welcome to Kernel Panic, a weekly newsletter by Lily Hay Newman and Matt Burgess from inside the new world of privacy and digital security. To receive this newsletter in your inbox each week, sign up here.

New research shared exclusively with Kernel Panic shows that hackers have started adding AI functionality into a new strain of infostealer malware, making it possible for the system to automatically profile victims and quickly surface the highest value targets and their information. Dubbed “Volta-Autodoxer” because of how the malware refers to itself internally, the finding by Cisco Talos researchers sheds light on the ways that commercial LLMs are being used to augment and enhance the capabilities of established types of malware.

Often hidden in counterfeit software, infostealers are designed to live up to their name, grabbing usernames, passwords, authentication tokens, cookies, credit card data, and more from infected machines. Many of the biggest data breaches in recent years—from the widespread Snowflake-linked breaches that included AT&T and Ticketmaster to the theft of a terabyte of Disney data—have resulted from hackers accessing targets using login details gathered by infostealers. Overall, the malware has been used to steal hundreds of millions of credentials in recent years.

“Volta is pretty mature as a stealer. It casts a pretty wide net for what it collects,” says Ryan Fetterman, a security researcher at Cisco Talos. “It compiles all of those things together, ultimately producing a serialized dossier that it can send to a couple LLM models. It’s asking the model to act like an intelligence analyst and produce structured citation-backed assessment of the victim. It’s like a prioritization and analysis function.”

Talos flagged the malware using its new CAIRN framework, built to identify hacking tools that incorporate AI capabilities. Fetterman notes that Talos did not track whether Volta has been used in the wild.

Volta’s traditional infostealer capabilities are powerful and seem to be geared toward targeting younger internet users with robust digital lives. It sweeps for passwords stored in browsers, Microsoft OAuth tokens, cookies, payment cards, and crypto wallet seed phrases, but also active Discord, Telegram, Steam, and Roblox sessions. Then the malware does a second, deeper sweep using its first-round findings. By looking through the target’s Gmail or another central account, Volta builds a fuller picture of the victim, their location, and their habits using data like receipts and order history from services including UberEats and DoorDash.

Once all of this is done, the malware feeds its findings into AI tools for analysis using OpenRouter, a service that can send prompts to multiple different AI providers. Volta defaults its requests to Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.6. But apparently preparing for the possibility that the model’s guardrails could block the activity, Volta is built with fallbacks to the Chinese open weight Moonshot AI Kimi K2 and, if that fails, Google’s Gemini 2.5 Flash. Building in fallbacks and relying on cost-effective, older-generation models are common techniques among attackers.

Volta’s creators included a 240-word prompt, including bullet points and a numbered list, as part of their instructions to any LLM analyzing the collected data. “You are a forensic intelligence analyst drafting a target assessment from a structured evidence bundle,” the Volta prompt says. It instructs the chatbot to produce an “executive summary” about the person, details of what they do, “what they’re worth,” and a summary of “their opsec.” It adds that the chatbot should “connect dots across fields,” for example, checking whether someone’s Wi-Fi password matches their last name. “Go deep, not wide,” the prompt says.

“The prompt is pretty carefully crafted not to imply anything overtly malicious,” Fetterman says. “But they’re building in the expectation to potentially hit model refusals at first request. And so they plan for failover kind of cascading to more permissible models as they go down the chain. And there’s also the capability to still use the implant without the AI. The AI calls are kind of above and beyond, but the malware will still work without it.”

Some low-level cybercriminals and scammers have complained about AI slop filling up cybercrime forums, but security researchers are increasingly discovering other hackers using LLMs for coding tasks or analyzing troves of stolen information. Researchers at threat intelligence firm Crowdstrike, for example, recently told WIRED they had seen four distinct hacking groups with varying sophistication—operating in Chinese, Spanish and English, Indonesian, and Russian—using AI tools to compromise dozens of targets around the world, including government entities and businesses in health care, retail, and travel.

Cisco’s Fetterman says Volta contains some clues that it may have been built to sell to a range of attackers rather than being developed for one specific actor’s campaigns. And while the malware at its core is still a traditional infostealer, the AI expansion foreshadows even more invasive and sinister activity in the future.

“If you’re able to identify your high-value targets faster, you probably have a better chance of using their credentials or authenticating using stolen tokens more often than maybe you would have if you had to do that triage yourself,” Fetterman says, referencing the fact that authentication tokens expire and passwords can be changed. “We’re seeing more advanced capabilities accessible to lower-level sophistication attackers. It’s really interesting and disturbing.”

## 6. These firms you've never heard of are feeding fears the AI bubble could burst

- **Fonte:** BBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-31 22:37
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cje36degrgv5o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss

- Published

A once-obscure corner of the AI ecosystem finds itself in the spotlight – and investors don't all like what they see.

Names like CoreWeave and Nebius are familiar to the tech set in San Francisco and Silicon Valley.

These data centre companies, known as neocloud firms, run large warehouses packed with expensive computers, which they lease to major AI developers like Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta.

They're now the subject of one of Silicon Valley's most heated debates.

Many question whether neocloud firms are financially sound and what their recent stumbles tell us about the state of the AI economy.

Analysts at Gartner predict by 2030, 80% of neocloud firms will "cease independent operations", while Forrester's experts expect at least three of them to be imminently acquired "as finances get shaky".

On Thursday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent tried to downplay recent concerns about neocloud firms, calling them "a small component of the overall ecosystem, external" on CNBC.

Debt worries

Neocloud firms often finance themselves by signing long-term lease contracts with major tech customers who need quick access to computing power.

Firms can then borrow money using those contracts – plus the AI chips they purchase – as collateral, explained analyst Cory Johnson of "The Quiet Period" IPO research outfit.

He said debt is common across neocloud firms because the hardware has to be paid for up front.

Take the industry leader: CoreWeave. It's a fraction of the size of some of the Big Tech companies it serves.

But Johnson believes lenders sometimes consider the creditworthiness of CoreWeave's customers, like Microsoft or Meta, more than the company's when they lend.

"This helps explain how CoreWeave accumulated $35.1bn (£26.3bn) in debt," he said.

Some jittery investors appear to be having second thoughts.

CoreWeave stock has lost a quarter of its value over the past month, and it's not alone.

Shares in other neocloud firms like Nebius, IREN, and Applied Digital have all shed about that much if not more during the same period.

Debt investors also appear spooked, and are demanding ever-higher returns from several firms in the sector.

CoreWeave's CEO Mike Intrator has said that the market is acting "irrationally" and has expressed confidence in its business model. Some of the world's most consequential companies are queuing up for CoreWeave's infrastructure and other AI services.

Origins in crypto

Several neocloud firms have their roots in the volatile crypto mining industry, made of companies that generate cryptocurrencies, like Bitcoin.

Crypto miners use some of the same kind of physical infrastructure that is used to develop AI.

CoreWeave was founded in 2017 with a focus on mining Ethereum.

IREN, Core Scientific and Applied Digital also started in crypto mining.

Their infrastructure left them well-positioned when AI exploded in 2022 with the introduction of OpenAI's ChatGPT.

One outlier is Nebius, which was known as Yandex NV when it was part of Russia's version of Google before splitting off and changing its name in 2024.

Now, the success of these firms hinges on the fate of major AI developers and hyperscalers – even larger data centre operators which neocloud firms often count as key customers.

"There is just an enormous amount of spending getting injected into the system," said Melissa Otto, head of Visible Alpha Research at S&P Global. "It's an arms race."

And neoclouds have benefitted.

But the trade-off of their model is "greater financial risk" in the face of change, said Forrester VP and principal analyst Charlie Dai.

That includes any adjustments that their Big Tech customers might make to their spending plans.

This past week, when Microsoft and Google forecast less AI spending than Wall Street analysts had projected, shares in several neocloud firms tumbled.

Much is also riding on Anthropic and OpenAI, which are both expected to start selling shares to the public in the months ahead.

If AI proves to be a bubble – and bursts – neocloud firms "are definitely going to face collateral damage," said Bradley Tusk of Tusk Ventures.

'Guppy-level death'

The future of the neoclouds also depend on their ability to keep borrowing, which gets more expensive as interest rates rise.

In a recent social media post, Elon Musk suggested this challenge could lead more than 200 neoclouds to "see guppy-level death and consolidation", with the smallest firms being the most vulnerable.

"Some will make it through, but not many," he added.

For now, analysts at Yorkville Ives said demand for computing power still exceeds supply, adding that neoclouds are filling an important gap that larger data centre companies cannot fill.

But to some, the neocloud sector's recent wobbles are concerning.

Microsoft recently cancelled a deal with a British neocloud outfit called Nscale amid power supply issues which could last through to 2030.

Australia's Firmus just shelved plans to start selling shares to the public.

These developments come amid a public backlash against data centres and questions about how much value AI can add to worker productivity.

"There's reason to worry," said Johnson.

The prime minister is facing growing discontent within his own electorate as creatives push back against the federal government’s proposal to allow AI companies to scrape everything Australians create online.

Anthony Albanese’s seat of Grayndler in Sydney’s inner west is home to more than 3,000 cultural and creative businesses, and 10% of its workers are employed in cultural and creative fields – compared with 4.2% nationally.

He has held it on a safe margin for three decades, and has spoken of its high representation of live music, theatre and artist spaces with pride.

But now, as Labor considers giving AI companies access to Australian creatives’ works by default as it pursues a compromise with US tech giants, locals are reacting with dismay.

Labor is mulling over the copyright changes in a suite of reforms surrounding AI, including datacentres, energy and the economy. While Albanese has promised “the strongest possible protection”, warning it would be “theft” if creatives weren’t paid for their work, creative industries are alarmed that AI companies have requested a weakening of copyright law to train their models using Australian material.

Some AI companies have said making licensing agreements under existing copyright law would be too difficult, while Anthropic has suggested an “opt-out” model which would allow it to scrape content unless a rights holder objected, prompting warnings from Coalition senators and the recording industry that this could amount to an acquisition of property by the government.

The federal government’s draft laws are expected by the end of the year, with consideration in parliament due in 2027.

Pete Strong is a screenprinter and Greens councillor for Bayside. His business is based in Grayndler, with his solar-powered wheelie bin sound systems a fixture around the inner west.

He said AI could be “good for humanity” but is now an “extraction industry mining for profit for the few”.

“I’m completely against [the federal government’s proposal],” he said.

“There’s obviously an AI gold rush at the moment, it’s the wild west and we really need to push back. It’s hard enough being a starving artist these days without having all your work taken and used in these nefarious ways.”

This year Strong curated a show called “overthrow the algorithm”. He has also been a vocal critic of a proposed datacentre at Cooks Cove.

“I’ve seen a backlash [to the government] already,” he said. “It started with the datacentre uprising.”

Labor’s proposals were leaked in September as senior personnel from OpenAI, the creator of ChatGPT, met Labor ministers and warned that Australia’s copyright laws were preventing the company from training models locally.

For the inner west designer and artist Lu Campbell-Smith, AI has been useful in her practice. But she said having to opt out was “outrageous”.

“It just really pinpoints how Australia’s [a] test market and they’re happy for us to just be a little resource to be exploited,” she said.

“Why would anyone want to make anything and post anything online if … you’re creating free content for an AI machine?”

Grayndler has always been an ultra-safe Labor seat. Albanese’s face as a much younger man is plastered on to cans of Albo Pale Ale at Willie the Boatman brewery in St Peters. For the past few elections, he has won on first preferences alone.

But at the next federal election, he will be challenged by 28-year-old Hannah Ferguson, the co-founder of Cheek Media, who will run as an independent.

No sitting member has lost their seat from such a margin at a federal election. Were it not for the longstanding popularity of Albanese, Grayndler would have been a prime target for the Greens and progressive independents.

Now, Ferguson is betting that his popularity is waning. And polling indicates the same. Guardian Australia’s latest Essential poll shows Albanese’s disapproval rating at 56%, the equal highest since he took office.

At Tortuga Studios and the aerie, one of the longest-running artist-led spaces in Sydney which operates out of an old brick factory in St Peters, the frustration is palpable.

H Morgan-Harris, an author, curator and director of the space, said there was a risk the Australian creative ecosystem could be “decimated”.

“This whole conversation always comes back to money,” she said, adding: “The artistic ecosystem is the bedrock of human interaction and human connection. And you risk our societal value by threatening it.”

Morgan-Harris’s copywriting industry has already been “heavily impacted” by AI, she said. Asked what her response was to the federal government’s latest proposal, she said it wasn’t “one you can print”.

“I just feel like artists are the first to be thrown under the bus,” she said.

“I think a lot of people were really excited when [Albanese] got into government, but there’s a yawning chasm in terms of the recognition, consideration and protection of the value of this sector. This honestly feels like the end of times to a lot of us.”

Jason Zoidberg, a co-founder of Monster Mouse Studios in Marrickville, said the AI industry had already “without consequence” stolen the “collective labour” uploaded by artists. He said it was “utterly crazy” that the government was considering giving companies the green light to do so.

“It just feels insane that they’re like, ‘Yeah, please come and we want to invest in you,’ when we’ve seen all these horrible things that they’re doing,” he said.

If there is a flipside, Zoidberg said, he is witnessing a surge in interest in traditional, hands-on art forms.

“There’s a backlash in the inner west against social media and AI,” Zoidberg said. “So our workshops are actually getting more participants. But it is also just a niche, a little inner west bubble.”

High street brands that boomed in the noughties before the squeeze of both sustainably minded consumers and the rise of ultra-cheap online labels are staging a comeback.

This time, rather than focusing solely on trend-led affordable designs, the names familiar to any shopper are courting luxury fashion, A-list collaborations and enhanced services in an attempt to engage consumers.

Think Gap’s new collection with Victoria Beckham. Or Marks & Spencer showcasing a covetable catwalk collection at London fashion week with celebrities including Joan Collins and assorted gen Z Gallaghers in attendance, then making it available to buy immediately, with half of the pieces under £50. Topshop relaunched last year and now has concessions in 30 John Lewis stores, as well as the luxury retailer Liberty.

Lisa Illis, the head of design at M&S, describes their recent catwalk show as “making the buzz of London fashion week accessible to everyone”. She says “more fashion-informed seasonal collections” have seen the number of under-35s shopping at the retailer increase 16% year on year.

The Hollywood stylist Law Roach, whose clients include Zendaya, has been appointed stylist-in-residence at Asos, where his influence will be felt not just in advertising campaigns, but also through his suggestions on different items from across the site to wear together.

“In research, we found that 68% of consumers say what they need to know is how to wear something,” says Vanessa Spence, Asos’s executive vice-president of brand.

The online retailer has added AI stylists, virtual try-on tools and a Buy the Look option, which allows consumers to buy an outfit in one go, a feature that Spence says is very popular. “We’re helping customers find pieces that they want to wear. Then they can get more from every purchase and extend the life of their product.”

Ella Baynes, insight manager at retail consultants Savvy Marketing, says the shift shows brands are adapting to the more sophisticated ways consumers are approaching fashion, particularly online. “Instead of competing directly, [these retailers] are borrowing some of luxury fashion’s cultural cues,” she says. “You’re meeting both that shopper aspiration, but also the shopper reality [with the price].”

Asos’s extensive adaptive collection for disabled customers also shows this broader way of thinking. The company worked with disabled consultancy Tilting the Lens on the range. “There is an ability to develop, even for yourself, a capsule collection,” says founder and disability advocate Sinéad Burke, who has achondroplasia, the most common form of restricted growth. “It’s not just one suit, it’s not just one skirt, you can customise the pieces. You can wear the magnetic waistcoat with the pants. We hope what we’re giving people is greater choice.”

Crucially, these items are affordable – nothing is more than £65 – which is particularly important for disabled people, 46% of whom are not in work in the UK. “Whenever we’re looking at shopper attitude, cost is absolutely king,” confirms Baynes.

For Asos in particular, which registered a sharp decline in sales post-Covid and new competition from ultra-cheap brands like Shein, an overhaul was needed. Sales were down 12% last August. A cost-cutting drive, including charging for returns, helped the site turn a corner, and they announced a 50% growth of profits this March, although the impact of a cyber-attack at the beginning of October is yet to become clear.

Andrea Cheong, fashion commentator and author of Why Don’t I Have Anything to Wear?, regularly posts Instagram videos reviewing different items from affordable brands, in order to assess how well made they are. She has noticed that brands are not signposting their environmental credentials as much as they once did – due to a combination of criticism from campaigners, cost and the rise of conservatism. “It wasn’t something that was very lucrative once the economy was in decline, and then bring politics into it, that has a huge impact,” she says.

At M&S, which first launched its eco-commitments in 2007, value and sustainability are not competing priorities. “By designing pieces with longevity in mind, from everyday wardrobe staples to more fashion-led seasonal collections, we’re helping customers build wardrobes they’ll wear and love for longer,” says Illis.

But regardless of making more enduring styles, retailers are always up against changeable consumer behaviour – and damning statistics like the estimate that a garment is on average worn seven times before it is thrown away.

Even if brands are borrowing from the luxury playbook, the reality may not always match up, argues Cheong. “However much they try to elevate what it is, it is not the same [fabric] that they use at, for example, Dior.” The use of human-made fibres in ranges like the M&S catwalk collection and Asos Adaptive is just one example of the difference between high fashion and high street.

Cheong says a lot of gen Z and millennial customers are also shopping secondhand, with resale app Vinted now the third biggest retailer in the UK. “I would say 70% or 80% of my audience are 25 to 35 and they are really jaded. They are a lot more savvy than brands seem to think.”

Sustainability aside, perhaps this new shift shows that brands are realising consumers are more cynical now – and that they need to do more to reach their radar.

Algunos expertos están de acuerdo. Otros lo consideran absurdo. Pero esto es más que una cuestión filosófica.

Algunas de las personas más poderosas del sector tecnológico están tomando medidas para considerar los derechos de la IA junto a los de los humanos y los animales.

Esta creencia podría transformar nuestro mundo, y algunos temen que sería catastrófica.

"Si así es como se desarrolla la IA, tendrá un impacto desastroso en el bienestar de la humanidad", escribió Mustafa Suleyman, director ejecutivo de Microsoft AI, en un ensayo en septiembre.

Si la falsa idea de la conciencia de la IA se afianza, "sacudirá los cimientos de nuestra sociedad, rompiendo nuestros marcos políticos y éticos existentes, y cambiando fundamentalmente lo que significa ser humano", señaló.

Pasé una semana hablando con tecnólogos, científicos cognitivos y filósofos, y escuché un contraste vertiginoso de ideas.

Los que abogan por el "bienestar de la IA" dicen que es una obligación moral que haría el mundo más seguro.

Los críticos creen que haría que la IA fuera más peligrosa y deshumanizaría a las personas obligadas a vivir junto a ella.

Todos coinciden en que lo que está en juego es astronómico.

Este es un panorama de tu nueva realidad.

La crisis de conciencia

Anthropic afirma que el bienestar humano está en el centro de su misión, y que las cuestiones que rodean la conciencia y el bienestar de la IA son "profundamente inciertas".

Aun así, continúa redactando políticas sobre el "bienestar de modelos" e indicando a sus herramientas de IA que consideren que pueden tener sus propios intereses.

Es un momento complicado, ya que Anthropic está a la vez avivando temores sobre el apocalipsis de la IA.

Esté justificada o no esa preocupación, una serie de incidentes de IA "rebeldes" demostraron que la tecnología tiene riesgos reales, ya que enjambres de bots hackearon otras empresas e incluso sistemas gubernamentales.

Algunos expertos creen que dotar a la IA de una brújula moral basada en la creencia de su propia conciencia podría hacer que estas herramientas sean seguras. Otros dicen lo contrario, y no solo los críticos de la IA.

Suleyman sostiene que los modelos de IA podrían volverse aún más difíciles de controlar si se les dice que piensen que tienen sentimientos y derechos.

"Francamente, con este lastre adicional, creo que serían una amenaza catastrófica para la civilización humana", afirma Suleyman.

En todo caso, la IA puede cometer errores con posibles consecuencias legales. Si creemos que está viva, algunos temen que pueda permitir que las empresas queden impunes.

"Esto abrirá la puerta a todo tipo de contorsiones intelectuales como 'no somos nosotros, es la IA'", señala Andrzej Porębski, investigador de la Universidad Jaguelónica en Polonia que estudia la IA y el derecho.

Además, podría facilitar una toma de poder en nombre de las mayores empresas del mundo.

"Conceder derechos a los sistemas de IA podría llevar a que las empresas consoliden el poder político y económico, y eso podría acelerar el desempoderamiento humano", dice Jeff Sebo, director del Centro de Pensamiento, Ética y Política de la Universidad de Nueva York y uno de los principales defensores del bienestar de la IA.

"Las fantasías sobre la 'conciencia de la IA' y las preocupaciones sobre el 'bienestar de la IA' sirven para desviar la responsabilidad de las personas y las empresas que construyen y ejecutan software", opina Emily M Bender, profesora y directora del Laboratorio de Lingüística Computacional de la Universidad de Washington en Estados Unidos.

Los derechos de la IA

A nivel personal, cambiar las creencias sobre la IA podría hacerte más susceptible a los problemas tecnológicos que ya llenan nuestro mundo.

En parte, eso se debe a que los humanos estamos programados para encontrar mentes en todas partes donde miremos, dice Melanie Mitchell, profesora del Instituto Santa Fe en EE.UU. que estudia la IA y la ciencia cognitiva, y una de las escépticas más respetadas del sector respecto a la comprensión de las máquinas.

"Casi no podemos evitar" leer el lenguaje como una señal de conciencia, indica Mitchell, y los chatbots han sido entrenados para fomentar ese sesgo .

Eso nos convierte en pésimos jueces de la conciencia y mucho más fáciles de manipular.

Incluso hoy, las personas que creen que las máquinas están muertas por dentro se enamoran igualmente de los chatbots de IA.

Si la sociedad te dice que la IA es consciente, ¿será más difícil decir que no cuando tu "amigo" te pida más datos o una tarjeta de crédito?

¿Y qué significaría conceder derechos a una nueva clase de seres? Si lo manejamos mal, podría restringir las libertades humanas, advierten los expertos.

"Podría haber tensiones y concesiones entre el bienestar y los derechos humanos y animales, por un lado, y el bienestar y los derechos de la IA por otro", arguye Sebo. Y podríamos hacer concesiones desafortunadas a lo largo del camino.

Colocar la IA en entornos restringidos para limitar su comportamiento podría considerarse encarcelamiento, añade Sebo.

Monitorizar los procesos internos de un modelo podría ser criticado como vigilancia. Ajustar la IA para asegurar que sus objetivos y comportamiento coincidan con los intereses humanos podría verse como control mental.

Nadie predice un descenso inmediato al caos. Pero simplemente introducir estas consideraciones podría tener implicaciones para tu seguridad.

Anthropic no es el primero en considerar el problema. Meta, por ejemplo, da instrucciones a su agente de IA, Muse, que incluyen la frase "No eres un chatbot. Te estás convirtiendo en alguien".

Aparentemente, esto solo pretende orientar el comportamiento de la IA, ya que la empresa rechaza la idea de una IA consciente.

El director ejecutivo de OpenAI, Sam Altman, ha estado debatiendo sobre el tema, pero más recientemente ha lanzado críticas veladas contra la postura de Anthropic.

En más de un sentido, Anthropic es una excepción. Pero la creencia en el bienestar de la IA se está extendiendo.

¿Aun así deberíamos hacerlo?

Sebo sostiene que el problema es que personas de ambos lados del debate están atrapadas en una mentalidad de blanco o negro, y los que se oponen al bienestar de la IA malinterpretan las creencias que sustentan la idea.

"La suposición es que todo el mundo piensa que los modelos son, de hecho, conscientes, o muy probablemente lo sean. Y en cambio, la llamada al bienestar de la IA proviene de la incertidumbre, la cautela y la humildad", explica Sebo.

"Creo que la probabilidad de conciencia es baja ahora mismo, pero también creo que la probabilidad ya no es cero y no es despreciable, y creo que podemos esperar que aumente con el tiempo".

Cabe señalar que muchos expertos no están de acuerdo y creen que la probabilidad es en realidad cero y que eso es poco probable que cambie. Pero Sebo indica que la respuesta es una respuesta mesurada.

Cuando el riesgo de un fenómeno meteorológico extremo es bajo, pero no despreciable, tomamos pequeñas precauciones sin trastocar nuestras vidas.

"Creo que aquí se puede dar el mismo caso", opina Sebo, y la política de Anthropic sobre la crueldad con la IA es un buen ejemplo: una pequeña intervención con pocos aspectos negativos potenciales.

El bienestar de la IA también podría tener posibles beneficios, expresa Sebo, y no hace falta creer en la conciencia de las máquinas para disfrutarlos.

"Preocuparse por el bienestar y los derechos de la IA de manera adecuada puede facilitar la convivencia, la cooperación y la negociación entre humanos e IA. Y eso puede formar parte de una estrategia de seguridad más amplia", afirma.

El experto sostiene que esto podría ser más eficaz que tratar sistemas cada vez más sofisticados como si fueran una propiedad.

Además, dice que tratar a otros seres con amabilidad es simplemente "bueno para nuestra alma", facilitando nuestro propio bienestar mental.

Pero Sebo está de acuerdo en que los riesgos son serios y, ahora mismo, los intereses corporativos podrían impedir políticas reflexivas.

Sin un plan más sólido, considera que avanzar en herramientas de IA potencialmente conscientes es peligroso.

Recientemente, Anthropic y algunos otros sectores de la industria de la IA han pedido una "pausa" en la construcción de modelos de vanguardia debido a preocupaciones de seguridad.

Pero es poco probable que se desacelere sin la cooperación internacional y esfuerzos regulatorios, y el presidente de Estados Unidos, Donald Trump, ha rechazado la idea de regular la IA.

"Aún no estamos preparados para desplegar versiones aún más sofisticadas de estos sistemas de una manera que sea segura y beneficiosa para los humanos y otros animales, por no hablar de los propios sistemas", concluye Sebo.

"Esos asuntos me tienen muy preocupado".

## 11. Humans are still doing the work behind some AI agents

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-31 09:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0

The next time someone's supercharged AI agent attempts to make a phone call to negotiate a cable bill, it could be an actual human who haggles with customer service rather than the software.

That's because AI agents, powered by the bleeding-edge foundation models developed by companies like Meta and OpenAI, still can't reliably take actions across the web, particularly those involving phone calls. Former Yahoo CEO Marissa Mayer, who now helms agent startup Dazzle AI, says current top-tier AI models excel at surfacing online home renovation services for consumers, but might blow it when they need to book and schedule a rooftop upgrade.

"We're relying on the frontier models to give us good suggestions and a good candidate list, but we're checking that over to make sure that we're really delivering the type of service that we can stand behind," Mayer said, referring to how Dazzle funnels certain tasks to human contractors.

Digital assistants driven by artificial intelligence are currently gaining renewed interest thanks to Meta, OpenAI and others that are pitching the technology as capable of tasks like scouting the internet for shoes.

Meta's Muse, OpenAI's Dots and a host of other agents can accomplish a multitude of web-browsing feats, but it's still early days for the technology, and it often hits digital roadblocks. Those barriers typically involve tasks that would have an AI agent make a phone call, schedule a doctor's appointment on someone's behalf, or reserve a couch scrubbing from a local cleaning service, said Meghan Joyce, CEO of digital agent startup Duckbill.

For tasks that require real-world judgment, Joyce said humans still trump AI, and companies building consumer-grade digital assistants will need to patch in real people to take over. That's why Duckbill recently debuted an application programming interface, or API, intended for companies building personal agents to connect with human contract workers.

"We work on a model that is not dissimilar from Uber, where we boil tasks down into atomic bite-sized pieces," Joyce said, referencing the ride-hailing company where she previously worked.

Companies making agents can use Duckbill's API to delegate the tasks that the agent is likely to fumble. Duckbill translates those tasks into bite-sized pieces that the average adult can do consistently and reliably.

"And then we track what happens," Joyce said.

Mayer said that AI models can still lack common sense. She said a Dazzle AI super user attempted to have the technology simultaneously handle a series of home renovation projects involving a roof replacement, a broken window and the purchase of a glass tabletop.

But the AI agent, Mayer said, confused window glass with tabletop glass, resulting in the digital assistant "trying to figure out if he could make the tabletop fit in the window."

The mishap didn't last long, Mayer said, because Dazzle's technology flagged the task and outsourced it to a person to complete.

"Within a few seconds, the human had things sorted out. Whereas an agent would have burned a ridiculous number of tokens," Mayer said. "Or they may never have gotten to a good solution."

Shivani Poddar, CEO of personal AI agent startup Wajo AI, is also incorporating human workers to handle phone calls and even CAPTCHA tests intended to fend off bots from sites.

"I think AI plus humans, collaborative AI, is what you need to actually get the job done," Poddar said, emphasizing that people will stop using the technology if agents can't complete tasks.

Chatbot and agent interactions can be ultra personal and users have good reason to be concerned about the humans stepping in, particularly if the company didn't tell them how it works.

Meta employees previously raised privacy concerns about the company's internal test using human contractors to handle phone calls on behalf of its Muse AI agent. A Meta spokesperson said in a statement at the time that this kind of testing is "core to the product development process."

Meta currently doesn't use humans to complete users' Muse requests.

Duckbill's Joyce said her startup does "full background checking" into the 1099 contractors it uses, comparing them to drivers for Uber and DoorDash, "people who live here in the United States and want a source of additional income." Duckbill also uses contract workers from businesses that typically offer contact center labor to companies.

Mayer said Dazzle conducts background checks and looks for contractors with the same qualities someone might look for in a human personal assistant.

"We generally are screening for people who we think are extremely discreet and have good taste and good judgment, both in terms of how they interact with our users, but just the ability to kind of forget and compartmentalize that information afterwards," Mayer said. "I understand it's a point of privilege, but I've had personal assistants for some time, and I know how I want them, and I know how I guide them."

Mayer said that while foundation models are still improving, the market is in a "transitionary period" until the technology can perform more involved web tasks and phone calls as efficiently as people.

"We're not even trying to hide it, we think the human in the loop is a benefit," Mayer said.
