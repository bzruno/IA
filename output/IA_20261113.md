## 2. OpenAI launches ChatGPT Wallet, allowing its 'dots' AI agents to make purchases for users

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-13 15:56
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 8.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/13/openai-chatgpt-wallet-dot-purchases.html

OpenAI on Friday announced ChatGPT Wallet, a new payment feature that enables its "dots" artificial intelligence agents to make purchases on behalf of users.

The company built ChatGPT Wallet in partnership with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, and it allows cardholders to maintain their existing credit card rewards and protections. Eligible users enter their card information once, prompt their dot to find items to buy and approve each purchase with a security check.

OpenAI said its dots agents will never actually access a user's card details. Instead, their payment networks will provide the dot with a "payment token," which contains a scoped card number, security code and expiration date that is specific to a given transaction.

"Our goal here is to really focus on merchants and our network partners and start building out a really great foundation that's safe and easy to use," Chaya Nayak, who leads product for monetization and payments at OpenAI, told CNBC in an interview.

ChatGPT Wallet comes as a growing number of consumers are exploring how to use personal AI agents to manage their calendars, book travel and complete other day-to-day tasks. OpenAI introduced the dots agents during its annual developer conference in late September, shortly after Meta's Muse offering topped the App Store charts.

Purchases made through ChatGPT Wallet could eventually kick-start a new revenue stream for OpenAI, but the company said it is not taking a cut of transactions to start. With the initial launch, OpenAI is working to build relationships with merchants and position itself as a serious platform for shoppers, especially as vendors are beginning to experiment with personal agents.

OpenAI has pushed deeper into AI-enabled commerce over the last year, but some of its early attempts failed to take off. The company introduced an "Instant Checkout" feature last fall, which it quickly pivoted away from. By March, it had rolled out a revamped shopping experience that aimed to make it easier for users to find and compare products.

Nayak said ChatGPT Wallet builds on OpenAI's previous efforts, particularly around product discovery.

"We think that this is the next step, where if someone found something they're really excited to buy, then we want to be able to get them through to that endpoint," she said.

ChatGPT Wallet is initially available to OpenAI's Pro subscribers in the U.S. who are over 18 years old. The limited rollout is intentional, and the "right approach to make sure things are working," said Luke Gebb, executive vice president and global head of innovation at Amex.

OpenAI's ChatGPT chatbot supports more than 1.2 billion weekly active users and nearly 100 million paying users as of the end of the third quarter. With such a broad user base, Gebb said OpenAI can decide to "turn up the volume" on ChatGPT Wallet when it wants to.

"It just makes sense to watch it, start it in a contained environment, and then keep rolling from there," Gebb told CNBC in an interview.

Merchants and vendors are still reckoning with the rise of personal AI agents, and some companies have opted to forgo them entirely. United Airlines said Wednesday that it will block agents from accessing its systems to book tickets, and Amazon prevents the agents from purchasing products on its platform.

OpenAI said Friday that agentic commerce is still in its "early days," and it published tools and guidance alongside its ChatGPT Wallet announcement to help businesses start to navigate the transition. The company explained how merchants can make sure their websites aren't inadvertently blocking OpenAI's agents, for instance.

"It's going to take time for the ecosystem to build the right infrastructure to be able to make this experience really amazing and seamless across all of their sites," Nayak said. "Really, who we are speaking to here is the networks and the merchants."

## 3. Export-banned Nvidia chips are behind China’s most powerful AI models — uh-oh, Jensen!

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-13 15:40
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/13/export-banned-nvidia-chips-are-behind-chinas-most-powerful-ai-models-uh-oh-jensen/

The leading open-weight models developed by Chinese companies were trained on Nvidia chips that the U.S. government restricted from shipping to its largest geopolitical rival, a new research effort claims.

The apparently widespread access to Blackwell GPUs among Chinese frontier labs shows the magnitude of the problem facing the leading chip designer. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang left the company this week after admitting to his affair with Alice Huang, executive of Megaspeed, a company implicated in smuggling Nvidia chips into China. Alice Huang has also been alleged to be an agent of China’s intelligence service.

While Jensen Huang’s relationships with a customer and two employees have drawn the most public interest, Nvidia’s potential corporate liability for export control violations likely factored into its decision to part ways with Huang.

U.S. export controls are intended to protect the country’s leadership in AI capabilities against China, but Huang has long sought to open up that market. Blackwell GPUs are banned from export to China or Chinese-owned firms, while the Trump administration allows Nvidia to export the H200, a less powerful chip, provided shipments take a circuitous shipping route that allows the U.S. to claim a 25% tariff.

Will DePue, a former OpenAI researcher who leads the nonprofit research group Unemployment Labs, analyzed the weights — the unique structure — of large language models released openly by DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, and Ant Group. The weights still reflect the configurations of the chips used to train them, and DePue was able to find compelling evidence that the most closely watched models, including Moonshot’s Kimi’s K3 releases and DeepSeek’s V4 models, bear fingerprints from training on Blackwell chips.

White House technology adviser Michael Kratsios said earlier this year that Moonshot had used export-banned Nvidia chips, citing U.S. intelligence sources. Epoch AI, a nonprofit research organization, estimates that about two million advanced chips entered China in 2024 and 2025 through schemes designed to hide their final destination.

That doesn’t mean that these models were trained entirely on Nvidia chips, or that export control laws were necessarily violated; the chips could have been in an offshore data center, which has proven a popular way for Chinese firms to access compute and driven an industrial revolution in Malaysia. Epoch’s data estimates that 275,000 Nvidia AI accelerators were imported to Malaysia in 2024 and 2025.

However, the ostensible goal of the export bans is to prevent exactly that: Chinese AI labs training on the most powerful chips. China hawks have long called for stronger controls on who uses chips, not just who owns them or where they are installed.

“If we care about the U.S. tech edge, it doesn’t make any sense to me that you would not allow Chinese companies to access chips in mainland China or Hong Kong and Macau, but then say, actually, if you just go over to Malaysia, you can do whatever you want,” Sam Bresnick, a researcher at the Georgetown Center for Security and Emerging Technology, told TechCrunch this summer.

Megaspeed, the company led by Alice Huang, has been at the center of that trade for several years through its subsidiary Speedmatrix, and has been the subject of investigations by both the U.S. and Singaporean government. Friday, Bloomberg also reported that a company with links to Alice Huang was involved in smuggling banned Nvidia chips to a data center in Shanghai.

In May, the short-selling research firm Culper published a report arguing that Nvidia had a “China problem” because a significant share of its revenue was driven by Chinese demand, either through remote data centers or smuggling in violation of U.S. law. It detailed Megaspeed’s relationship with Nvidia and alleged that Alice Huang’s companies are backed by Chinese firms that are barred from accessing U.S. chips.

Ironically, short-seller Culper’s bet against Nvidia wasn’t driven by the expectation of a U.S. government crackdown. Instead, it was focused on the Chinese government’s response to the bans, which was to recommit to its domestic chip industry.

If China succeeds, billions of dollars in Chinese demand for Nvidia chips could dry up. Some 600,000 H200 chips are reportedly stockpiled in Hong Kong, awaiting shipment into China. While Chinese companies might want the chips, observers have no consensus on why they haven’t been cleared for import.

Graham Webster, a research scholar at Stanford who focuses on U.S.-China technology policy, said he doesn’t buy the idea that holding the chips back would drive domestic production, since demand for chips is so large. Other theories for the hold-up include preparation for a potential trade deal that would involve importing a large volume of U.S. goods, or concerns about potential surveillance technology in the chips.

For now, U.S. companies have limited obligations to track what their customers do with controlled goods like AI chips. However, Jensen Huang’s personal and professional entanglements with Alice Huang create a much bigger problem for Nvidia if government investigators confirm that chip smuggling took place and that he was aware of it. Nvidia and Huang have both vouched for Megaspeed’s data centers, saying Nvidia officials had visited them to confirm they were legitimate.

The U.S. government’s treatment of Nvidia and Megaspeed is also under scrutiny. Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick, who oversees the agencies that license chip exports, could reportedly lose his job after reports that his investigators failed to uncover suspicious chip buyers and were too cozy with Nvidia officials.

It was the resignation heard across the world. Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s embattled CEO who co-founded and led the company for 33 years, had officially stepped down. “It’s like a bomb went off,” one neocloud founder told TechCrunch when he first heard the news.

Simulation assumptions:

- Fixed setup: The can starts at a known position, with an anchored robot and clear, flat workspace. No vision was used and collision checks were limited.

- Simplified can: Deformation and liquid motion are not modeled, so this example does not test crushing.

- Scripted grasp and motion: A horizontal grasp uses a 2 N·m drive-effort cap chosen through simulation tests, not calibrated to fingertip forces or recommended by the manufacturer. Contact and friction hold the beverage can as the arm follows fixed waypoints without replanning. For hardware gripping-force specifications, see the Robotiq product sheet.

These results apply to this scripted setup. They do not establish robustness to uncertain object positions, different payloads, or real hardware.
