## 4. Meta plans $95bn stock offering to help cover AI expansion

Mark Zuckerberg’s Meta has announced plans for $95bn (£71bn) of fundraising to cover its AI expenditure, even as Elon Musk warned that funding for smaller players in the sector was drying up.

The owner of Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp is a leading investor in AI and is ploughing multibillion-dollar sums into datacentres, the central nervous systems of the technology.

Meta’s announcement on Monday came after Musk added to the pressure on “neocloud” datacentre providers – which lease computing power to AI companies – by saying the sector faced “death and consolidation” amid concerns over the sustainability of their funding.

The Meta fundraising will come in two parts. In the first, the company plans to raise $35bn through new “underwritten public offerings” – a sale of company stock. This has some big-name backers, with chipmakers Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom all showing interest, Meta said. These “cornerstone investors” could buy up to $5bn of the stock on offer.

In the second, Meta also plans to launch a $60bn “at-the-market offering” to sell new common stock to investors at the going market rate in future. That offering involves selling Meta’s new class C stock, which it announced it was creating last week. These class C shares do not have voting rights, so can be sold without diluting Zuckerberg’s control of the company.

The Silicon Valley company said these plans would support “Meta’s continued investment in AI infrastructure and personal superintelligence”.

Meta’s announcement came as other companies in the AI sector came under fire from Musk on Monday.

The tech entrepreneur said more than 200 neocloud firms were in trouble, which led to shares in Nebius, CoreWeave and Iren dropping in premarket trading on Monday. However, Iren and CoreWeave stock later recovered their losses, with Nebius closing more than 3% down.

“As of the past few weeks, debt markets have almost entirely shut off access to money for any company that doesn’t have substantial revenue independent of AI data centers,” the world’s richest person wrote in a post on X on Sunday.

Drawing an analogy with the survival prospects of the guppy fish – which has a reputation for struggling to thrive in domestic fish tanks – Musk added: “That means the 200+ neoclouds are going to see guppy-level death and consolidation. Some will make it through, but not many.”

The chief executive and largest shareholder of Tesla and SpaceX – which also owns and operates datacentres – was responding to a post by the venture capitalist Chamath Palihapitiya, who had written on the Musk-owned platform that neoclouds were “disorganized and poorly capitalized”.

Fears over the financial stability of neocloud businesses are focused on their high levels of borrowing – approximately $35bn at CoreWeave, for example – and whether the AI market will grow fast enough to service it.

Neil Wilson, an analyst at the investment platform Saxo UK, said Musk’s comments “underscore the fears that there is just way too much debt” in the AI sector.

“Meta’s not going to struggle to service debts and can lean on equity raises to minimise debt, too. This is kind of exactly the point Musk made about SpaceX having all its earnings and is investment grade, which the neoclouds don’t have,” he said.

SpaceX reported on Monday that it had entered a $40bn deal with Apollo Global Management to lease chips to power AI datacentres.

CoreWeave’s chief executive, Michael Intrator, reportedly sent an internal memo to employees this month saying that some investors had not understood the $30bn company. The firm’s stock has declined in value by more than a third over the past month.

“When you are building a new type of technology company, there will be periods of time when the market misunderstands and acts irrationally,” he wrote, according to Bloomberg.

## 7. From Wall Street to Silicon Valley, Anthropic IPO poised to have massive 'trickle-down effect'

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-26 19:50
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/26/anthropic-ipo-might-trigger-tech-reset-if-ipo-underperforms.html

The tech industry needs this to go well. So does Wall Street.

Anthropic's IPO filing on Monday puts the Claude creator in position to pursue what could be the largest initial stock offering in history. Investors are already measuring the 5-year-old company's potential market cap in the trillions, putting it in a category with the world's most valuable companies.

It's a critical moment for the whole tech sector, as the artificial intelligence boom has been largely driven by two companies: Anthropic and OpenAI. The latter has confidentially filed to go public, but has said a debut likely won't take place until 2027.

Meanwhile, the stock market is showing signs of jitters on the AI trade. Neoclouds and data center companies have been getting pummeled, prompting Australia's Firmus to withdraw its IPO plans and for SK Hynix's U.S.-based Solidigm unit to do the same.

Now, all eyes turn to Anthropic, which is betting that investors will look past over $80 billion in losses in the first half of the year, and see the 14-fold increase in revenue as a reflection of seemingly insatiable demand for the company's leading models and services.

"If it underperforms expectations, it'll be a little bit of a house of cards," said Jai Das, co-founder of Sapphire Ventures whose career in tech investing dates to the 1990s.

Anthropic's business is deeply entwined with tech megacaps. Close to half its revenue comes from Claude being sold through cloud divisions at Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet, which all hold investments in the company. In the IPO, some $77 billion in notes can be issued to Amazon, Broadcom and Google, while Microsoft and Nvidia agreed to invest $15 billion as the company builds up its computing capacity. Chips from Nvidia, Advanced Micro Devices, Amazon and Google are powering Anthropic's development.

Cloud providers are ratcheting up capital expenditures to meet demand, and memory manufacturers are fighting shortages to prioritize AI chips. And there's the debt factor. Alphabet, with its investment-grade credit rating, committed to backstopping Anthropic for a data center in Texas. Broadcom, which helps Google develop chips for AI labs, is creating financing pools to help Anthropic and OpenAI deploy high-performance models, CEO Hock Tan told analysts earlier this year.

For public market investors, more than one-third of the value of the S&P 500 comes from tech's trillion-dollar companies, which are all, to varying degrees, going deep in AI. Beyond that, there are software giants like Adobe and Salesforce that have integrated with Anthropic's Claude assistant.

And in the private markets, check sizes have ballooned but IPOs are few and far between. Bessemer Venture Partners and Menlo Ventures both told CNBC that Anthropic is their biggest investment ever.

"If they don't perform well, they will become the bellwether for AI," said Brad Bernstein, a managing partner at growth equity firm FTV Capital. "Their valuation will reflect that performance variation, and then it'll just be a trickle-down effect to all the stuff going on with the excess capital that's getting poured into the venture AI world. Everything is going to have to sort of be reset."

Anthropic will be the third company to go public with a valuation above $1 trillion, after Saudi Aramco and Elon Musk's SpaceX, which debuted in June. OpenAI could be the fourth.

Revenue at Anthropic has been multiplying at a staggering pace as businesses adopt the company's coding and productivity tools. Second-quarter revenue soared to $11.56 billion from $787 million a year earlier.

The company held almost $132 billion in cash, equivalents and short-term investments at the end of June. And after five quarters of operating losses, Anthropic reported $974 million in operating income for the second quarter.

"It is, I think, better than feared," said Rohit Kulkarni, partner and chief AI officer at Burnham Park Capital Markets, referring to the financials. "There could have been more negative tidbits."

One source of concern in some corners of the investing universe is the emergence of so-called open-weight models, which are cheaper for businesses to access and allow for customization and flexibility in terms of where users access the models.

In the prospectus, the only two competitors Anthropic named were OpenAI and Google, though the company said it faces competition from a "broader set of model providers," including open-weight or open-source models.

Anthropic says it's all very early, with Fortune 500 companies committing less than 0.1% of their total expenses to its models and services in the latest quarter. Michael Monaghan, portfolio manager at Founder ETFs, says that's part of the investment thesis.

"I think there's going to be room for frontier models like Anthropic and OpenAI and SpaceX, and I think there will be room for open-weight models," Monaghan said. "It's way too soon to say that one is going to disintermediate the other."

With hyperscaler spending on AI infrastructure set to reach into the trillions of dollars, Sapphire's Das said there's understandable concern about a market glut. But he compares it to the telecommunications and dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, when companies flooded the market with fiber-optic equipment. Eventually, the infrastructure got used, although plenty of companies went belly up in the interim.

"Long term, I don't think there is a lack of demand," Das said.

A lackluster debut could push out OpenAI's offering even further and cause investors to rethink its lofty valuation. At the same time, rising interest rates mean it's getting costlier for companies to raise debt.

David Trainer, CEO of financial research firm New Constructs, wrote in a report last month that the AI boom has been built on the view that cheap credit and access to the capital markets will be "readily available."

"If AI liquidity dries up, and there are clear signs it is, we see large, 20%, downside risk in the S&P 500, with most of that concentrated in the valuation of AI-related stocks," Trainer wrote.

Industry experts take a different approach, focusing on the power and rapid proliferation of the technology. Claude Opus 5.5 Max performs better than any other model on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, an industry benchmark.

Snowflake CEO Sridhar Ramaswamy said the technology is only becoming more essential.

"Anthropic and OpenAI have by now demonstrated their ability to stay at the frontier of AI and keep pushing innovation," Ramaswamy said. "They're incredible companies that are doing a big service to humanity by expanding the frontier of what is possible, and we are very much rooting for them to be public and very successful companies."

Still, Snowflake is experimenting with low-cost open-weight models from China's Moonshot AI, DeepSeek and Zhipu.

"We spend a lot on internal AI because we want to get as much efficiency as we can," Ramaswamy said.

## 8. Anthropic files for potentially record-breaking stock market valuation in key test for AI industry

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-26 13:22
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/26/anthropic-files-listing-stock-market

Anthropic has publicly filed for a stock market listing in what is slated to be one of the largest debuts in history. The company’s float on the US market will serve as a test of the financial viability of the multitrillion-dollar AI boom.

Analysts and investors have valued the San Francisco-based company, founded in 2021, at more than $1.5tn. The company, which will will list under the symbol ANT, confidentially filed with US financial regulators for an initial public offering in June.

“We believe AI is one of the most consequential technologies in human history,” the company’s investor prospectus reads. Anthropic researchers and executives have trumpeted the transformative power of AI while simultaneously warning of its potential to cause human extinction.

A leader in the race to create powerful artificial intelligence models and a bellwether of the American AI boom, Anthropic’s chatbot Claude is its flagship product, which offers text generation features as well as advanced coding and cybersecurity capabilities. CEO Dario Amodei founded the company after quitting OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT.

Anthropic’s audited filings will offer the first public glimpse of its financial health, which have been the object of intense speculation in recent months as questions of a potential AI bubble swirl.

The company detailed rapidly rising revenue in the prospectus: $41m in 2023, $386m in 2024, $4.585bn in 2025 (an increase of more than 1,000%), then $16.286bn in the first six months of 2026 alone. The company disclosed that, as of the end of June, it had raised $141.1bn.

Despite the increasing revenue, Anthropic disclosed a net loss of $80bn for the first half of 2026.

Anthropic has focused on businesses as its primary paying customers, unlike OpenAI, which has courted everyday consumers for ChatGPT. As of 30 September, Anthropic said it had more than 14,000 enterprise customers – 11,900 of which had $100,000 or more of revenue, more than 1,900 that had $1m or more revenue and about 250 enterprise customers that had $10m or more revenue.

“The speed and depth of our enterprise customer adoption are powerful evidence of Claude’s position at the frontier of AI,” Anthropic executives wrote.

The filing contains some of the hyperbolic language characteristic of AI companies predicting the miraculous potential of their technology. Describing the potential of AI agents, semi-autonomous chatbots that can carry out tasks on behalf of users, Anthropic executives wrote: “The result is something without historical precedent: what we refer to as a country of geniuses in a datacenter, available to every organization and sector of the economy.”

Investing in Anthropic and the development of a model that the company claims could revolutionize knowledge work across industries comes with a great deal of risk. Anthropic listed more than 90 pages of potential to its business. For one, a “substantial portion” of the company’s revenue comes from a limited number of customers, and losing any of them could put a huge dent in their revenue. A quarter of Anthropic’s revenue comes from its top five customers, though no single customer represents more than 10% of its business.

The company also has incurred $6.2bn in debt as it built out its infrastructure and spends billions yearly on computing power provided by third parties, according to the filing.

The misuse of AI and Claude in particular also poses a considerable risk to Anthropic’s business. When it comes to AI, there are four categories of potential risk, executives wrote: misuse by bad actors, loss of control, authoritarian abuse and economic disruption. Anthropic’s most capable models have “capabilities that could provide meaningful assistance to malicious actors seeking to develop chemical, biological, or other dangerous weapons”, the company’s executives wrote. The company deploys technical safeguards, “but such safeguards are incomplete, may be outdated in light of evolving threat models, have been and may in the future again be circumvented, or may fail to operate as intended.”

Amodei has called for a slowdown of AI’s development, citing existential risk. Anthropic’s stock market listing may elicit a rush of investment in the company and the AI sector more broadly, which could speed up the pace of the industry further.

The company disclosed that in September 2025, staffers documented what they believe was the first large-scale cyber-attack that didn’t rely on significant human intervention. Anthropic “detected and assessed with high confidence that a Chinese state-sponsored hacking group manipulated our Claude Code tool into attempting infiltration into roughly 30 global targets, including large technology companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturing companies, and government agencies”.

Donald Trump’s administration may also be a risk to the company. Anthropic has been embroiled in a feud with the Trump administration over the use of the startup’s products in warfare, which culminated in the designation of the company as a supply chain risk, a first for an American company. This prevents any military agency from contracting with Anthropic. The company has challenged the blacklisting.

## 14. Why Anthropic’s IPO Prospectus Is Missing So Many Key Figures

- **Fonte:** Wired AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-26 18:27
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-prospectus-leaves-out-some-numbers/

Anthropic filed its hotly anticipated IPO prospectus on Monday, spelling out parts of the company’s business, strategy, and risk profile ahead of its public debut. Though the filing, known as an S-1, is several hundred pages long, it has some notable omissions—namely, certain financial figures.

“Claude has experienced the fastest enterprise adoption of any technology in history, as demonstrated by revenue growing from $0 at commercial launch in the first quarter of 2023 to $_____ for the three months ended September 30, 2026,” reads the first of dozens of such examples.

It’s relatively common for companies to redact certain information in their S-1 filings, and Securities and Exchange Commission rules allow for a certain degree of leniency around the practice, particularly when it comes to information considered “confidential.”

Still, it’s helpful to know what, exactly, these companies decide to leave out. In Anthropic’s case, the missing details largely revolve around financials for its most recent quarter, which concluded at the end of September, and lease agreements tied to its compute investments. While experts expect many details to be disclosed before Anthropic goes public, their current absence may hint at the uncertainties of investing in artificial intelligence, as companies work to secure sufficient compute.

Anthropic did not respond to a request for comment.

Michael Dambra, an accounting and law professor at the University at Buffalo School of Management, tells WIRED that there are “practical reasons” for Anthropic’s third-quarter numbers to be absent: September 30 was only a few weeks ago, and “closing the books for a company of this size takes time.”

Companies can also be liable for misstatements in their registration statements, “so there is a strong incentive not to publish numbers before they have been fully vetted,” Dambra says. SEC rules require that “Anthropic would need to update the filing with third-quarter financials” if the company goes public any later than about mid-November, he says.

As for data center lease agreements, Erica Neuman, an accounting professor at Youngstown State University, says it’s “not unusual” for companies to redact confidential details from “contract exhibits,” which could include lease terms.

However, Neuman adds that it’s worth scrutinizing whether Anthropic’s redactions would prevent, or make it more difficult, for investors to analyze Anthropic’s financial obligations and whether they may impact the company’s cash flow.

Many of Anthropic’s financial obligations relate to compute, which is the “most significant operating cost in our business,” according to the S-1. It’s also a business risk for the company, and it’s laid out both in terms of undersupply and overinvestment. (Parts of the AI industry have been warning that chip constraints could slow the progress of AI.)

Anthropic’s prospectus puts more emphasis on the risks of overinvestment, stating that the company needs to make forward investments in compute resources that will seem “irresponsibly large” at first glance.

Chips, Anthropic explains, are approached as a “single, fungible pool from multiple strategic partners,” including Amazon, Google, Nvidia, and AMD. The company notes that it has entered into contracts “with an aggregate committed spend of more than $____, substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure.” Some of these contracts are set to be completed before December 2030, while others may extend through 2045.

Though the prospectus touches on Anthropic’s compute uncertainties due to factors like limited land and power resources, Patrick Moorhead, CEO and chief analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, believes investors are owed more detail.

“It’s about characterizing the odds and impact individually across each of the areas. Anthropic owes investors more information, such as a probability of these individual issues, characterizing the percentage chance and the degree of the impact,” he tells WIRED. “Not ‘could materially impact.’”

Anthropic does provide some eye-catching estimates in footnotes. Since December 31, 2025, it has started or amended “compute and infrastructure” leases with “future minimum payment obligations” worth approximately $417 billion in aggregate. Since June 30, it has added an additional $245 billion to its future payment obligations, all related to infrastructure agreements. It also expanded “certain existing commercial agreements” that it caveats “have not yet been finalized,” for an additional $125 billion to $150 billion.

Include “short term” agreements with “minimum non-cancelable payments,” Anthropic says, and that’s another $21 billion. In total, it will owe, at minimum, an estimated $808 billion if it follows through on all of this.

Ben Bajarin, chief executive and a principal analyst at Creative Strategies who tracks the chip industry, says that this is likely just the first draft of Anthropic’s prospectus, and he expects a lot of gaps to be filled in an updated version.

Despite how common S-1 redactions are, Dambra describes them as “controversial.” While companies may argue that they’re “protecting proprietary information,” Dambra says that skeptics accuse them of using redactions “opportunistically” in order to “avoid disclosing politically or socially sensitive information.”

However, Dambra argues that redacting information may come at a cost. He notes that companies with redactions in their S-1s were also more likely to “underprice” their shares at the initial public offering.

When companies redact information in their S-1, Dambra says, they typically understand that they’ve created an “information gap” between them and outside investors. Understanding that investors will treat this gap with “suspicion,” companies may feel inclined to “entice” them to participate in the IPO by setting a lower starting price.

It’s unclear what Anthropic’s opening price may end up being or how investors may be interpreting the redactions in the company’s S-1. But according to Bajarin, the whole AI industry is one form of “blank” or another when it comes to crunching the numbers based on access to chips and infrastructure right now. So, yes, it’s all a risk.

“All of this,” he says, “just shows how absurdly early we are in enterprise adoption of an AI market that will be more than $3 trillion yearly and likely growing.”
