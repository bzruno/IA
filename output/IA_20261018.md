## 3. What are self-replicating ‘AI worms’, and why is Anthropic worried about them?

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-18 10:30
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/18/anthropic-ai-worm-worries

Warnings about what artificial intelligence might do in the future – together with alarming reports of what it has gotten up to already – are being issued so quickly that even security experts have difficulty triaging what to worry about.

Yet a report this week from Anthropic’s offensive research team grabbed attention by describing a new, practical threat, one devoid of human characteristics or apocalyptic musings. The red team, as such internal testers are called, said it created a malicious AI program that spread autonomously through internet-connected “smart” televisions and could do the same inside other ordinary devices. While machines running AI usually need a fair amount of computing power, the researchers said that their program split parts of itself among 15 televisions and was still able to function by using a bit of power from each.

In addition to evoking the horcruxes that kept pieces of Lord Voldemort’s soul alive within objects in the Harry Potter series, the report alarmed some professionals because the generally weak security of smart televisions and many other “internet of things” devices would be very hard to correct, even with a concerted effort.

“It’s something to worry about just like every other wave of cyber-attacks. We need to mitigate the possible effects,” said Joshua Saxe, who led development of Meta’s AI security effort. “I’m concerned right now that folks in the cybersecurity community are not taking these kinds of real risks as seriously as they should be.”

The damage of such “AI worms” could be astonishingly large. Self-replicating computer viruses, which need not trick a human to propagate, are known as worms, and earlier worms have wreaked some of the worst havoc in the history of the internet.

That legacy includes the Morris worm of 1988, which arrived before the mainstream consumer internet, when a graduate student released a program that took advantage of various software flaws and weak passwords, copying itself so rapidly that many infected machines slowed to a stop. The US and Israel brought worms into international espionage with the stealthy Stuxnet in the lat 2000s, which sabotaged Iranian nuclear centrifuges. Other worms like the Russian NotPetya and North Korean-attributed WannaCry have caused tens of billions of dollars’ worth of damage worldwide.

Many of the world’s most destructive worms have depended on known vulnerabilities that device owners have not patched, sometimes months after patches became available. The Anthropic team predicted that a worm like theirs could function in the same way and be harder to stop, because smart devices receive patches less often than phones or laptops. Worse, they theorized that an AI worm could keep adapting to other types of hardware by researching newly disclosed vulnerabilities.

What differentiates AI worms from typical reproducing viruses is that AI models can learn as they go, said Ariel Herbert-Voss, an early security expert at OpenAI who now runs AI security startup RunSybil. Existing cybersecurity infrastructure is not ready for the proliferation of such threats, Herbert-Voss said. “We need to better enable our security teams. We need to be giving them more resources to fix problems.”

Worms could also be used for benevolent purposes, suggested Chris Inglis, a former deputy director of the National Security Agency who served as National Cyber Director in the Biden White House.

“The question of whether AI worms are plausible has been settled by several breakout events over the past six months,” Inglis said by digital message. “The better question is how we will employ them, counter them, and/or combine them with other emerging technologies.”

Anthropic researchers said a successful AI worm would need to have more capability on less powerful devices than is possible right now – but they added that current trends will make that feasible by year’s end. Their research follows a paper published by experts at the University of Toronto a few months ago, which infected a full-size computer and could spread to similar machines more rapidly than the television worm.

“The capabilities have been developing very rapidly,” said co-author and Toronto PhD student Jonas Guan.

Guan and other experts said that, as a practical matter, an AI worm should not top anyone’s list of AI fears. The Anthropic version moves very slowly, Guan said, and worms have predictable and detectable actions that can be blocked at multiple layers. Basic security hygiene, including requiring strong passwords and two-factor authentication, would stop most worms and other hacking, experts said.

“Nothing about this is truly feasible or impactful,” said Juan Andres Guerrero-Saade, vice-president of research at security firm SentinelOne. “It’s nifty stunt hacking that’s getting way more amplification in service of the ridiculous doomer narratives that suggest models have a mind and will of their own.”

Anthropic did not respond to an interview request.

Outside Washington, many in the AI and security worlds believe greater government oversight of AI is now essential. But members of Congress have introduced mutually conflicting bills, while the White House has so far created only a small review board.

Security researchers and laypeople alike find themselves weighing the motivations behind fearful prognostications from AI companies, which range from espousing quasi-religious beliefs, to seeking legislation to exclude rivals or save money, to hyping the anticipated multi-trillion-dollar public valuations of the San Francisco AI giants.

“The claim that a lot of this is marketing is correct, but the reality dwarfs any PR,” said Gadi Evron, who is running a conference on AI security in San Francisco this month called Unprompted. “Whatever claims the labs are making, they are not warning us enough. We need to understand that nobody can stop the AI storm.”

## 4. Microsoft pulls out of AI datacentre project in Essex over power supply concerns

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)

- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/oct/18/microsoft-drops-huge-ai-datacentre-project-in-essex-over-power-supply-concerns

The development of a huge AI datacentre hailed by the UK government has received a fresh setback after Microsoft pulled out of the project over concerns about power supplies.

The development by the British company Nscale, heralded by ministers as the country’s largest AI datacentre when it was announced in 2025, was planned to open at Loughton in Essex next year.

Doubts had already emerged about the scheme after the Guardian revealed in March that the location was still an operational scaffolding yard.

Now there are further concerns after Microsoft, one of its largest planned customers and expected to have been the anchor tenant, informed the company that it was pulling the plug on its agreement.

It comes a month after it emerged that power supply problems meant the project would face a lengthy wait before coming online.

Keir Starmer’s government had highlighted the Loughton project when announcing the UK’s AI strategy in 2025, describing it as the “largest UK sovereign AI datacentre”. Datacentres are the central nervous system of AI technology such as chatbots.

The end of the deal with Microsoft, first reported in the Telegraph, means Nscale has lost its only publicly announced customer for the Essex datacentre. It is understood that Microsoft remains on board for another project the company has in Norway.

Nscale, which is backed by tech companies including Nvidia, has positioned itself as Britain’s “sovereign” datacentre provider but the loss of an anchor tenant for its Loughton project could undermine its ambition to float on Wall Street this year with a potential valuation of $35bn (£26bn).

Targeting the rapid growth of AI to boost the economy, the UK government’s proposals to harness the technology have focused on two Nvidia-backed companies: the London-based Nscale and the US company CoreWeave.

Official figures show investments in AI are among the factors helping Britain’s economy to beat expectations of a weaker growth performance this year as the war in the Middle East rattles global business activity.

Nscale is a “neocloud”, one of a number of companies with deep ties to Nvidia that are building the key infrastructure for the AI boom. It is this relationship with Nvidia that meant it and CoreWeave were chosen as key players in the UK’s sovereign AI push, which happened under Starmer.

There is, however, a growing chorus of voices which suggest that neoclouds may be a vulnerability in the broader AI economy, especially as delays pile up across partners.

Driven by hopes of an economic transformation, many are taking on increasingly risky debt, including companies vital to the AI boom. If Nscale pushes back its initial public offering in New York, these vulnerabilities may come into sharper focus.

The former UK deputy prime minister Nick Clegg and the former Meta chief operating officer Sheryl Sandberg are board members at the company. Clegg holds a 0.25% stake that could be worth $88m if the company floats at a $35bn valuation.

It emerged earlier this month that UK Power Networks (UKPN), which owns and maintains the electricity grid across London and the south-east of England, had informed Nscale that it would not be able to supply enough power to the company’s Loughton datacentre until the early to mid-2030s.

Nscale said it was “committed to the project” but the failure to find a quick fix to the power supply issue underlines how much energy has become a hindrance to datacentre development.

The scaffolding yard site has been cleared by Nscale, and the company is investigating whether it can generate power onsite and whether it can speed up the connection process.

An Nscale spokesperson said: “We remain committed to both the Loughton project and the UK, and we are exploring alternative power sources for the site, in addition to other expansion sites in the UK.

“Our relationship with Microsoft remains strong and long-term, and this unfortunate London-wide grid issue has no impact on our other contracts.”

## 5. Kylie Minogue songs already used to train AI as tech firms ‘strong-arm’ Australian government, music body warns

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)

AI has already been trained on the works of Australian artists including Kylie Minogue and Nick Cave without compensation, the peak global body for music rights has claimed, as the Australian government has been warned against giving in to “strong-arming” from tech companies seeking to weaken copyright law.

The International Confederation of Music Publishers (ICMP) – which represents Sony, Warner and Universal as well as thousands of independent publishers on six continents – has warned that of the dozens of countries approached to water down copyright laws, Australia appears to be the only to consider doing so.

John Phelan, the ICMP’s managing director, told Guardian Australia AI companies were asking policymakers to “legitimise larceny”.

“They’re trying to strong-arm Australian policymakers into ‘if you don’t do this, we won’t invest’.”

In July, Anthony Albanese said it would be “theft” if artists did not retain control over whether their work was used to build and train AI, and what they were paid for its use.

Music, film, media and news organisations had expressed concerns the government was being lobbied by global tech companies behind closed doors to weaken Australia’s copyright laws to help train AI models, without compensating the creative sectors.

But government and opposition members of a parliamentary committee on artificial intelligence appeared to revisit the issue this week, describing the current situation as a stalemate.

They said AI models were already training on copyrighted works in the United States without compensating creators – but that without changes to local copyright laws, those companies were withholding training frontier AI models in Australia.

In a joint letter to the prime minister this week, the inquiry’s Labor chair, Jo Briskey, and its deputy chair, Liberal senator Jonathon Duniam, recommended the government “examine current copyright settings” while ensuring creators retain control and are paid.

The independent senator David Pocock has raised concerns that the committee was coming to a position on copyright predetermined by the prime minister, after staffer edits were seen in the letter. Briskey has rejected the claim.

AI companies’ demands

The ICMP has been tracking how AI companies are attempting to change copyright law in different jurisdictions to allow training. Phelan highlighted methods that the companies had used to train their models on music, such as with spreadsheets containing millions of lines of music available on YouTube.

A key claim of the AI industry is that its models are not trained on Australian content due to copyright rules. However, in evidence provided exclusively to Guardian Australia, ICMP showed Google’s Gemini was able to reproduce lyrics from Australian artists including Kylie Minogue, G-Flip and Nick Cave.

Phelan also pointed to an AI training dataset reported by the Atlantic in June, which contains more than 12m songs pulled from YouTube videos, including tracks from Minogue, Cave and Hilltop Hoods.

Google was approached for comment.

Phelan said 62 countries had considered making their own legal changes but none of the others had bought into AI companies’ demands.

“There isn’t a single country among the other 61 [countries] … which has introduced a new text and data mining exception for commercial purposes, or reduce the idea of exclusive rights and copyright,” Phelan said.

“The world is very much watching what Australia does.”

“It’s fair to say the global music industry, as well as all creative industries worldwide we work with, are appalled by the idea of a statutory scheme or a government deal which would interfere with how Australian industries work and contract around the world.”

Anthropic’s special envoy, Jeffrey Bleich, told the AI committee last week the company was not trying to dictate terms, but had shared information with the Australian government about training and what the implications would be “with respect to our capacity to operate here”.

“You can’t license the entire internet. Billions and billions of information exists there which is available to be viewed and studied, and that’s essentially what training is,” he said.

“Under existing Australian law, it’s our understanding that that could be determined to be illegal if we weren’t able to find a licence with every single person who had rights within Australia.”

OpenAI’s chief of economic policy, Adam Cohen, said the fair use exception in US law “fundamentally protects” the development of new technology. He pointed to agreements made with News Corp and the Guardian as examples of where the company makes deals for the use of work, despite arguing US law did not require such.

Phelan said he believed panic had set in among the AI companies over the sheer cost of investment and the need to make a return, coupled with the ongoing litigation over copyright in the US. He said they may be looking to Australia for a legislative solution.

“They’re seeing the legislative side as being the way to try to [find] a place to do business without licences, which will secure their future business model.”

In a submission to the inquiry, Guardian Australia said that undermining copyright protection would have a “profound impact on civil society” and copyright-protected material should only be available for AI training with the rights holder’s explicit prior authorisation.

## 8. Wikipedia often gets taken for granted – but its history is fascinating, and its future is fragile

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-18 11:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/18/wikipedia-human-history-ai-future-under-threat

A few years ago, I was struck by the tension between how Wikipedia was used, and how it was talked about. Everyone was on it all the time, and yet its reputation had stalled at “don’t trust it”, because it was the “free encyclopedia that anyone can edit”.

Clearly people were trusting it, so much so it had become part of the fabric of everyday life since it was founded by Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger in 2001. But despite the fact it now has been consulted trillions of times by billions of people (English Wikipedia alone is 5.2bn words long), it inspires an odd lack of curiosity. Even people donating to Wikipedia might say the money was “for the factcheckers”, though there aren’t any – not paid ones, anyway. Meanwhile, the internet in the social media era had begun to feel like a much uglier place, and some digital nostalgia was setting in: here was this holdover that had somehow survived; a website that functions – and even looks – much the same as it did 20 years ago.

You could say that I fell down a Wiki rabbit hole, especially when it came to the website’s early years. Almost everything important about the project came together quite quickly. Its amateurish, homegrown ethos was off-putting to a lot of writers and journalists, and that meant many questions about why it had worked had no answers. Most of the people seriously interested in Wikipedia were a handful of academics – so I decided to write a book about it.

I’m not a tech journalist. Instead, I have a background in the humanities. Wikipedia actually does, too: philosophy, and even off-piste disciplines like town planning and poetry, played a much bigger role in its inception than pure technology. Ward Cunningham, who invented the wiki in the 1990s – that’s a webpage anyone can edit, and the foundational tool of Wikipedia – thought very carefully about how the power of computers might be democratised. He drew on all sorts of oblique concepts for it, such as constructivism and the Zen concept of wabi-sabi – beauty in imperfection – and it’s remarkable how well the culture he created has persisted. Especially wabi-sabi – Wikipedia is, after all, always in a state of incompleteness.

Philosophy drew Wikipedia’s co-founders together as well. Though Wales worked in tech and finance, he corresponded with philosophers for years. And though Sanger was an academic philosopher, he spent lots of time arguing online – only “amateurs” seemed to share his sense that philosophy had life-and-death stakes. Lots of people in the dotcom boom started online encyclopedias, but Wales was the only one to hire a philosopher. In fact, the only person he hired was a philosopher. It was such an unpredictable choice, and an important clue to what made Wikipedia work: it sprang out of this digital “republic of letters”, where all sorts of people were thinking about facts and why they were important.

Because Wikipedia is so massive, writing an “exhaustive” book on it is impossible, like trying to represent a mountain with a grain of sand. Instead, I concentrated on the human history – the small, sometimes very small, number of people who really influenced what Wikipedia became. Wikipedians can be difficult, and the most ornery get a bad wrap – even the Wikimedia Foundation’s leadership has made comments about their “Dorito-stained fingers”, or tendency to stay home on a Saturday night. But by the end of the process, I was in awe of them, and I still am.

Wales and Sanger are prominent, but many other important early Wikipedians are unknown. The first woman to edit Wikipedia, Gaytha Langlois, hasn’t had much attention. She wrote about two things dear to her: Rhode Island and standard poodles – small states and big dogs. Wikipedia has always had a gender imbalance, which has proved stubborn – Sandi Toksvig is only the latest in a long line of critics trying to address it. She is part of an effort to create a more balanced alternative to Wikipedia called Mappa Mundi, which has the potential to feed back into Wikipedia as well.

I became fascinated too with a little-known contributor who had the first biographical page for a woman on Wikipedia (today, it would be called a user page). Her name was Ruth Ifcher, and she was a very intelligent grammar pedant, with a PhD, who had been forced out of the workforce with severe agoraphobia. Wikipedia came along later in her life and opened a whole new avenue for creative and meaningful work.

Proust said that neurotics alone “composed our masterpieces”, and that’s true here.

Neurodiversity is so central to Wikipedia that one former head of the Wikimedia Foundation told me the encyclopedia is a “city built by Asperger’s”. You can sense that energy in familiar topics – planes, trains and automobiles, comic books, weather, and all the rest. Often, Wikipedians are so prolific that they write too much, a phenomenon called “fancruft”. “Road geeks” (like trainspotters for roads) kept having their work about minor roads deleted, so a whole lot of them left in a huff, and started a separate online wiki encyclopedia for roads. That’s almost a microcosm of the inclusionists v deletionists debate, which is the biggest philosophical rift on Wikipedia – a rolling argument about what should be included, and what shouldn’t.

The co-founders had once believed that 100,000 articles would be “a reasonable size” for an encyclopedia, because that’s how big Britannica was. Today, Wikipedia has 68m, and Pew Research estimates that it served 1.9tn page views between 2015 and 2025. Because it is so monumental, I never expected Wikipedia to feel fragile or vulnerable – but as I was finishing the book, AI had begun to encroach on Wikipedia in an existential way by diminishing the web traffic it relies on, for instance, or by editors contributing huge volumes of poorly written AI-generated text. People ask me “is Wikipedia under threat?”, and it is, but so is everything from Hollywood to pure mathematics. I do think it will survive, but the quaking foundations made it feel urgent to say “we saw this made, and we won’t see anything like it again”.

Chris Thomas is all-in on AI gadgets.

Thomas, 43, wears a pair of Meta's camera-equipped glasses, a Fieldy note-taking pendant around his neck and a wristband from Amazon's Bee, all of which record his daily life. A Pittsburgh-based business owner and a livestreamer, Thomas relies on the devices to help him stay organized, pay bills on time and set reminders.

"I'm forgetful AF," Thomas said in an interview. "My life is so crazy that not having to remember to remember is fantastic."

Not everyone is on board with his lifestyle. Earlier this week, a bystander at a local bar noticed his Meta Glasses.

"He was like, 'I don't feel comfortable,'" Thomas said. "I don't turn it off, I take it off completely because people don't really believe it's off."

It's the type of interaction users are increasingly navigating as more tech companies introduce AI-powered, always-listening devices and features, which have been marketed as useful tools to transcribe meetings, keep track of conversations, set reminders and offer personalized recommendations.

The latest example of these gadgets arrived Thursday with Amazon's Bee Frontier wearable and Bee Eclipse, a card-shaped device that attaches to a smartphone. Both devices are designed to record, transcribe and analyze a user's day.

Apple's latest Apple Watch Series 12, unveiled in September, will have Siri Recap, which "ambiently" takes notes throughout the day and generates summaries of conversations. Another feature called Live Rewind can recap 15 seconds of a conversation via a text transcription.

Meta's popular smart glasses can take photos, capture video and record voice notes or reminders. Startups including Plaud and Pocket also developed card-sized note-taking devices, while Friend offers an AI necklace.

The devices have fueled concerns that they could erode privacy, by enabling covert recording without obtaining bystanders' consent and collecting sensitive data that's potentially accessible to others.

Amazon, Google and other makers of voice-activated smart speakers faced similar privacy concerns when their devices first hit the market, in some cases leading to lawsuits and regulatory fines.

But this new generation of AI devices is also testing the limits of privacy law.

Unlike earlier smart speakers that were activated via a so-called "wake word" and sometimes stored recordings or sent audio to the cloud, some of these products can continuously listen, interpret speech, and extract its meaning without retaining the underlying audio. This capability has raised a largely untested question about whether that processing itself can amount to an unlawful interception.

"With new technology like this, my statement is always to embrace with caution," Karen Panetta, dean of graduate education at Tufts' School of Engineering and a fellow at the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, said.

She added, "If I'm giving permission for myself to be recorded, or for everything I look at to be recorded, that doesn't mean everybody else around you has given that consent."

Apple and Amazon appear to have sidestepped some of the privacy concerns that dogged earlier voice assistants.

Apple says the feature does not store audio, send recordings to its servers, or involve human review. Amazon has said it doesn't store audio from Bee devices, and conversations are processed in a secure environment "where no one, including Amazon, can access your data."

But that may not resolve the legal question.

Many state wiretap and eavesdropping laws are written around whether a private communication was intercepted or its contents were acquired — not simply whether a conventional audio recording was created.

Colin Zick, a privacy and data security attorney at Foley Hoag, said Apple appears to have learned from the first wave of litigation over voice assistants. But the new technology raises questions those earlier cases never had to answer: Can a system that is continuously listening, interpreting speech and turning it into a summary still amount to an interception if it never saves the underlying audio?

Some state laws define interception broadly. Massachusetts, for example, defines an intercepting device to include equipment capable of "transmitting, receiving, amplifying, or recording" a communication, while the "contents" of a communication can include its substance and meaning.

In Zick's view, a device that continuously receives speech and produces a written summary could potentially fall within that language even without retaining an audio file.

And in some states, the bigger legal risk may fall not on Apple, Amazon or other AI gadget makers but on the person wearing the device.

"A non-wearer did not buy the watch, agree to Apple's terms, or choose to have their conversation summarized in the app," Zick said.

Daniel Pi, an associate professor at the University of New Hampshire Franklin Pierce School of Law, said New Hampshire's statute is broad enough that he believes the Watch's new audio-intelligence features would qualify as an interception. The law covers not only recordings, but the "aural or other acquisition" of the contents of a communication.

That could create an unusual situation in which an ordinary consumer turns on a feature built into a mainstream device and, at least technically, runs afoul of state law by using it during a private conversation without everyone's consent.

Pi said a user in New Hampshire could potentially face civil or criminal liability for doing so, though he stressed that the practical risk of prosecution is far lower. It could be difficult to know whether someone had the feature enabled in the first place, and he said he would be surprised to see prosecutors pursue cases involving a bare statutory violation where no one was harmed.

The legal questions are also different from those raised by earlier smart speakers and voice assistants. Those cases often centered on devices mistakenly activating or capturing speech that was not intended as a command. Here, the feature is designed to acquire and interpret the substance of a conversation — and the user has deliberately opted in.

That changes the analysis, Pi said, and could also shift responsibility toward the wearer rather than the technology company itself.

The broader privacy concern extends beyond who is legally responsible.

Michael Stokes, a former CIA officer and senior vice president of emerging technologies at Veilant, said the bigger shift is that people can be swept into AI-powered surveillance without owning or knowingly interacting with the device doing the collecting.

"I think the bystander problem is really the concern I have — that you can be surveilled without owning the technology," Stokes said.

He pointed to the growing number of cameras, microphones, and sensors embedded in consumer devices, from smart glasses to watches and AI recorders. The difference now, he said, is that AI can rapidly interpret what those devices collect and draw meaning from far larger volumes of information than earlier generations of consumer technology.

For Stokes, that makes the Apple Watch part of a much larger change in the privacy landscape. During his time at the CIA, he helped develop the concept of ubiquitous technical surveillance, describing a world in which everyday devices continuously generate information that can reveal identities, locations, and associations.

The concern is not simply that more data can be collected, he said, but that the cost of making sense of it has fallen dramatically.

In the past, Stokes said, intelligence collection could require thousands of hours of work to gather, sort and analyze information about a person. AI increasingly automates that process, making it cheaper and easier to extract useful information from data that may have been gathered incidentally.

That creates a new kind of exposure for people who never opted into the technology in the first place — whether it is a private conversation picked up by a nearby wearable or information collected by a device they do not own.

"We no longer have these pockets of small privacy that we thought we did in the past," Stokes said.

Aida Baradari, founder of the startup Deveillance, is trying to give some control back to people worried about being recorded without their consent.

The company created a hockey puck-shaped gadget, called the Spectre I, that uses AI to scan for nearby devices and sends out "adversarial signals" that scramble a person's speech before microphones can process it.

Baradari said she watched as more AI wearables hit the market and felt users should have a greater say in how they interact with them.

"We need to be able to use technology while not giving up our core values or rights," she said.

Financial regulators fret about this for a number of reasons. One is that if such services become widely used, they could have destabilising herding effects. With deposits able to be moved around the financial system at a moment’s notice, it could accelerate a bank run in times of uncertainty.

The answer is probably that we will need case law to find out, which will only come when something goes badly awry.

That is alarming because legal ambiguity about who owes what to whom when things go wrong can be an accelerant to financial instability.

We learned that lesson back in the 2008 financial crisis, when it turned out that some banks had built financial products with such fiendishly complex liability structures that they were effectively impossible to value – unleashing panic.

While it may not be much of a loss to society if internet-enabled kettles and fridges do not take off, the financial analogue to a dumb appliance would be Georgieva’s shoeboxes – and that would have costs for those consumers, and the smooth running of the economy as a whole.

As Bailey somewhat apocalyptically put it: “If we don’t have trust in money, we don’t operate as a society.”

It is unfortunate this moment comes as the US – the crucible of the AI boom – is governed by a man whose main contribution to the debate has been to seek to rename AI “superintelligence”.

The UK has pledged to take up the issue of AI safety as part of its agenda for the G20 presidency next year, and G7 finance ministers announced in Bangkok that they would carry out simulations to test the resilience of the global financial system to cyber-attacks.

That could help to highlight vulnerabilities, but the questions at stake are much wider and more complex than whether criminals can hack the banks – and are urgent now, just as the prospects for international cooperation seem bleak. Superintelligence will seem anything but super if its misuse becomes a trigger for financial chaos.

Providing educational materials and offering field trips are ways that industries can maintain their social license to operate in a community.
