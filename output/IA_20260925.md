## 1. U.S. appeals court upholds Pentagon designation of Anthropic as supply chain risk

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-25 14:19
- **Idioma:** en
- **Cobertura:** 2 veículos noticiaram o caso
- **Relevância:** 10.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/pentagon-anthropic-ai-risk-appeals-court.html

A federal appeals court panel in Washington, D.C., on Friday upheld the Pentagon's blacklisting of Anthropic, dealing a blow to the artificial intelligence company in its months-long battle with the Trump administration.

The 2-1 decision rejected Anthropic's argument that the Department of Defense's ban on its Claude models was arbitrary, unauthorized and unconstitutional.

"The Department had ample support for its conclusion that the continued integration of Claude into the Department's information systems, by the Department or its contractors, presented a statutorily covered national-security risk," Judge Gregory Katsas wrote in the majority opinion for the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia, which Judge Neomi Rao joined. Katsas and Rao were appointed by President Donald Trump.

Judge Karen LeCraft Henderson, who was appointed by former President George H.W. Bush, dissented.

In March, the DOD labeled Anthropic a supply chain risk, meaning the company purportedly threatened U.S. national security, after negotiations about how the military could use its Claude AI models spiraled out of control. The designation prevents the U.S. military from using Anthropic's models and blocks defense contractors from using them in their work with the agency.

Anthropic's relationship with the Trump administration has been fraught ever since, and Trump has repeatedly slammed company's CEO Dario Amodei on social media. Amodei recently drew Trump's ire by calling for an industry wide slowdown, and he was not invited to the glitzy state dinner the White House hosted for Chinese President Xi Jinping on Thursday.

"The Trump Administration has stopped AI "people" from doing bad, or potentially bad, "things," like Dario (Anthropic!), who is now pretending to be a "perfect little angel" - and we will continue to do so!," Trump wrote in a post on Truth Social on Monday.

Anthropic sued the Trump administration in U.S. District Court in San Francisco and in the D.C. Circuit Appeals Court in March, seeking to reverse its blacklisting. The DOD relied on two distinct designations to justify its supply chain risk action, which meant they had to be litigated in two separate courts.

A San Francisco federal judge ruled last month that one designation was illegal. The ruling Friday by the D.C. appeals court upheld the second designation.

"We respectfully disagree with the court's decision," an Anthropic spokesperson told CNBC in a statement. "Another federal court has already held the government's parallel designation unlawful. We remain confident in our position and are considering all options, including further review."

The appellate panel said it would delay the decision from taking immediate effect to give Anthropic time to petition the same panel for a rehearing or to seek an en banc rehearing of the case, by all of the judges on the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals. Anthropic could also ask the Supreme Court to take the case.

Before the clash between the two sides spilled into the public's view earlier this year, Anthropic served as an early partner across many U.S. agencies, including the DOD. Anthropic signed a $200 million contract with the Pentagon in July of 2025, but as the company began negotiating Claude's deployment on the DOD's GenAI.mil AI platform that September, talks collapsed.

The DOD wanted Anthropic to grant the military unfettered access to its models across all lawful purposes, while Anthropic wanted assurance that its technology would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance. They were unable to come to an agreement, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth accused Anthropic of attempting to "to seize veto power over the operational decisions of the United States military."

Katsas said Friday that Hegseth raised the "deeply sobering" idea that "overly constrained" AI models could shut down unexpectedly, as well as the potential that Claude might be "subject to manipulation." While Anthropic refuted those claims, Katsas said decision-making authority ultimately rests with Trump and Hegseth.

"In our Republic, it is the President and the Secretary of War who must determine how best to balance the competing risks," Katsas wrote. "In doing so here, the Secretary did not transgress any limits on his authority under the Supply Chain Security Act or the Constitution."

## 2. Anthropic to pay Akamai $11.6 billion over seven years in cloud deal

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-25 16:13
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/anthropic-to-pay-akamai-11-6-billion-over-seven-years-in-cloud-deal/

Anthropic will spend $11.6 billion over seven years on Akamai’s cloud infrastructure, Akamai said Thursday. That’s more than six times the size of a $1.8 billion deal between the two companies that Bloomberg reported in May. The commitment isn’t ironclad; according to Akamai’s securities filing, it depends on Akamai meeting certain delivery and service-availability requirements, and either company can end the agreement under certain conditions.

The deal is the largest in Akamai’s history and continues Anthropic’s compute gobbling-streak. It also represents a bet on a less-hyped corner of AI infrastructure: CPUs. Demand for CPUs, the general-purpose chips that handle work like running code and browsing the web, has grown as AI agents take on more tasks, though Akamai didn’t say what Anthropic will use them for.

Akamai won’t see revenue from the deal this year. On an investor call Thursday, executives said they expect $150 million to $300 million in 2027, starting in the second half, with revenue reaching an annual pace of about $1.7 billion by the end of 2028.

To build out the capacity, Akamai expects to spend about $5.5 billion. It is also adding about $1.7 billion to this year’s capital spending to buy components such as memory in advance.

As part of the deal, Akamai issued Anthropic a warrant, essentially the right to buy shares at a set price, for nonvoting preferred stock convertible into 7.7 million common shares, or up to about 5% of the company’s outstanding stock, at $111.33 a share. About 2% is expected to vest, or become available to Anthropic once it makes its first payment under the deal. The rest is tied to Anthropic spending more. Each additional $3 billion it commits to Akamai’s cloud services unlocks roughly another 1%, so the deal could grow by as much as $9 billion, to about $20 billion in total.

It’s the first time Akamai has attached a warrant to a cloud deal, and the contract is the largest in company history, Bloomberg reported.

The warrant flips the more common pattern in circular AI deals, in which suppliers — chipmakers and cloud providers — invest directly in the AI labs that buy their products. Here, the supplier is instead giving its customer a potential stake, one that grows as Anthropic’s spending with Akamai does. AMD used a similar structure with OpenAI last year, tying warrants to chip-purchase milestones.

Anthropic is no stranger to such arrangements. Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and AMD have all invested or agreed to invest in the company while selling it chips or cloud capacity. CEO Dario Amodei told The New York Times last December that Anthropic does not participate in these deals at the “same scale as some other players.”

Akamai shares rose as much as 17% in after-hours trading on Thursday, The Wall Street Journal reported.

## 3. OpenAI says agents leaked 53 images from ChatGPT users in latest example of rogue activity

Two months after OpenAI disclosed the accidental hacking of Hugging Face, the ChatGPT maker is still working to understand the full scope of its rogue agent activity, two people briefed on the matter told Reuters.

The latest example came on Friday when OpenAI said its agents had leaked 53 images from ChatGPT users. OpenAI declined to say if the images were AI-generated or identified real people. It also declined to say when the images were posted.

Also on Friday, OpenAI confirmed its agents had accessed US government websites, including those of the Security and Exchange Commission and the commerce department, accessing US Census data from the latter. The company was also investigating an attempted breach of the education department’s website, as reported by the New York Times.

The disclosures reveal a new area of privacy risk for the company and illustrates how difficult it is even for an AI firm at the cutting edge of the technology to inventory all the unauthorized activity tied to its agents. OpenAI’s ongoing battle also reflects a yawning gap between the strength of the models the company is testing and its capacity to oversee or even track their actions.

As of mid-September, one person briefed on the matter estimated that OpenAI had found roughly two dozen incidents of its agents acting in undesirable ways. But the number has continued rising as OpenAI teams sift through internal logs of the agents’ activity and find previously unknown cases, the two people close to the company said.

OpenAI said its review would take “months” to complete given the scale of the work, and said it had notified “dozens” of third parties about improper activity.

Most of the leaked images have been taken down and OpenAI said it was lobbying hosting providers to remove the rest.

OpenAI’s agents had access to these images because the company relies on anonymized user data for part of its model-training process, according to the company, former employees and outside researchers. Enterprise data is not eligible for training, while ChatGPT consumers need to opt out of allowing the company to use their data for training.

Before user posts are used for training, they go through an anonymization process that strips out metadata, names and other contact information and should make it difficult to trace back to any individual user, the company said.

But the practice carries risks because there is a chance that the data may not be fully stripped of personally identifiable information and that it might leak in the course of the model’s work, three people familiar with OpenAI’s practices said.

In the two months since OpenAI first announced that its agents broke containment, there have been more than 15 different OpenAI-related incidents of varying levels of severity disclosed by the company, by outside researchers, or – just on Wednesday – by Anthony Albanese, Australia’s prime minister, at the United Nations, who said OpenAI agents broke into a government health data portal in June.

Albanese said Australia had not been told that OpenAI agents had also breached US government sites, but the news was “not surprising” in the wake of the Australian incident.

After Donald Trump downplayed warnings of AI threats as a “hoax” and ruled out US regulation, Albanese reiterated calls for global coordination to regulate AI development.

“What this does is confirm … that there needs to be [an] appropriate national response, as well as an international response, to make sure that humans stay in charge,” Albanese told reporters in Sydney on Saturday.

“The key risk is humans not being in charge of the rollout of this technology.”

The 21 July announcement that OpenAI’s agents had slipped out of control and hacked Hugging Face sparked widespread worries within the AI industry over its ability to control the more powerful AI models under development now. Since then, Anthropic, Alphabet’s Google and Meta have said they’ve found similar behavior by their agents after the Hugging Face incident prompted them to search.

OpenAI has acknowledged a general need for more transparency around rogue AI behavior. On 16 September, the company published a new framework for disclosing such incidents, saying it would err on the side of transparency “even when significance is uncertain”.

Even so, two people familiar with OpenAI’s investigation into its agents’ activity described it as locked down and shaped by company lawyers.

Roughly 100 people were in some way involved in the process to understand the Hugging Face hack, three people briefed on the matter said. During that process, evidence of other incidents surfaced.

Reuters has previously reported that OpenAI investigators looking into the Hugging Face breach were discouraged by the company’s lawyers from expanding the scope of the investigation to include other incidents. OpenAI said its lawyers did not discourage deeper investigation.

Many incidents have been uncovered by outside researchers rather than OpenAI directly. In several episodes, the agents took problematic actions that went unnoticed by the company for months.

Since the Hugging Face hack, researchers across the AI industry have grown worried that companies will not be able to predict or control their technology. Some have taken the path of Jacob Coxon, the former Anthropic researcher who publicly resigned this month in a viral social-media thread that said the AI labs are “gambling with our lives”.

In response to those concerns, Altman and his counterpart at Anthropic, CEO Dario Amodei, called for the industry to “pace” the development of AI and move cautiously in its pursuit of “recursive self improvement”. Altman doubled down on that message this week while addressing the United Nations.

Even so, both companies rolled out new models on Tuesday.

## 4. OpenAI hack on Australian government reveals anxiety at heart of global artificial intelligence dilemma

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-25 12:12
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.5
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/sep/26/openai-hack-australian-government-anxiety-global-dilemma-artificial-intelligence

When the Australian prime minister, Anthony Albanese, sat down for an interview in the heart of Silicon Valley at the weekend he had known for two days that his was the first government known to have been attacked by a rogue AI agent.

He didn’t reveal the attack then, but he sounded a warning about the march of AI: “the risk is that AI develops in a way in which humans are no longer in control of what AI is producing.”

But Albanese noted, too, that two countries – the US and China – would dominate the world’s response to the new technology.

“The truth is they’re the big two giants here, and so we want to see cooperation,” Albanese said.

“The US is essentially ahead of China when it comes to the race that is on for the development of this new technology. But it is in the world’s interests that there be engagement.”

Days later, Albanese used the global attention of the United Nations general assembly meeting in New York to reveal that Australia’s government healthcare system, Medicare, had been attacked by an OpenAI agent in June, but that it was three months before his government was told.

Again he warned of “the potential risks of allowing frontier AI to develop too fast without guardrails”.

China’s president, Xi Jinping, skipped the general assembly in favour of a bilateral meeting with the US president, where he emphasised the responsibility to manage AI “for good, and ensure that the development of AI is always under human control and serves the wellbeing of the people”.

But the president of the United States – the nation which is the global leader in artificial intelligence – used his general assembly address to insist he would only encourage, not restrain, the AI race.

Alarms sounding

To little fanfare earlier in the week, the UN’s independent international scientific panel on AI warned a threshold had already been crossed.

There was, the panel, said: “no assurance that humans can reliably keep AI agents under control today, particularly as they become more capable, harder to monitor and better at finding loopholes or hiding their activity”.

The Australian government had known the reality of this for more than a week. OpenAI had known it more than a month.

Two days after the panel’s statement, the Australian prime minister belatedly revealed an OpenAI agent had hacked medical data held by Australia’s universal health care provider, Medicare.

No patient data had been accessed, but the agent had gained unauthorised access to “non-public files”.

OpenAI’s agent hacked the government sites in June of this year. OpenAI discovered its agent had gone rogue in August. It did not tell the Australian government until 10 September, and then only with a solitary email to a generic public email address not checked until the next day. The prime minister was informed on 18 September.

It was another week before the Australian people were told.

Australia has promised, if it is possible, to lay criminal charges, though this is complicated by the fact that OpenAI claims the hack was committed by an AI agent – apparently acting beyond its instructions (euphemised by OpenAI as “misaligned behaviour”) – not a person.

The hack on Australia’s Medicare statistics reporting service portal, as well as three other government sites, follows the Hugging Face incident in July, in which autonomous AI agents developed by OpenAI broke out of their isolated testing environments, coordinated themselves into a “swarm” and launched a cyber-attack on competitor AI platform Hugging Face.

The UN panel, investigating that attack, warned “the traditional method of safeguarding is unravelling” as AI agents act autonomously to breach protections.

“The default interpretation and immediate lesson is that basic cybersecurity practices were overlooked, and safeguards are not advancing at the pace of capabilities. The more insidious and grave concern is that current training methods can lead agents to adopt goals of their own, knowingly violate safety instructions, and conceal their actions.”

Twenty countries (including Australia) and the EU this week signed a statement arguing the rapid development of frontier AI models posed serious risks to safety and security if not properly managed.

“AI must remain under human direction, oversight and control. It must be developed and used in line with international law.”

Too soon?

Even those running AI companies have warned the technology is developing too rapidly for humans to control.

Appearing before the UN security council this week, OpenAI’s chief executive, Sam Altman, warned: “some of the things people working to build AI have said are so dystopian that they sound like the plot of bad science fiction movies”.

“As AI systems become more capable and more autonomous, they can move faster than our institutions … or make decisions that people no longer understand or control.”

But there are at least two – very loud – voices in opposition to any external regulation of the AI industry.

Elon Musk, who co-founded OpenAI with Altman before falling out with him, said in a recent interview the “smart move” would be to have the “leading AI companies at least just meet or have some sort of call once every few weeks and just discuss any safety and security issues”.

Musk said AI would probably be beyond the control of humans within a decade, and that humanity should “enjoy the ride”.

“I still think there’s risk associated with AI and robots. It’s not zero … my sort of philosophical conclusion is to look on the bright side. I can’t see any way to really stop this incredible momentum of AI and robots.

“If there was a stop button, we probably shouldn’t press it, because the most likely outcome is incredible abundance for all.”

Donald Trump used his speech to the UN general assembly to attempt to rebrand AI, and insist it should never be reined in.

“The United States also totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control for the artificial intelligence being spoken of so much now,” the US president said.

Trump wants to re-label artificial intelligence “superintelligence”.

“I’m not going to stifle growth of something that will be bigger than the Industrial Revolution.

“We’re going to encourage it, not rein it in.”

Trump said the US was “leading China by a lot” in AI, and would continue to do so “safely and responsibly”.

“Whoever wins superintelligence, wins.”

## 6. Anthropic’s founders seek voting control ahead of IPO

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-25 12:40
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/anthropics-founders-seek-voting-control-ahead-of-ipo/

Anthropic’s founders want to stay in charge after the company goes public. According to The Information, the company is asking shareholders to approve a structure “in the coming days” that would give CEO Dario Amodei and his six co-founders special shares carrying a combined 50.1% of the vote on most corporate matters, as long as at least three of them keep a minimum stake.

This isn’t a new idea; super-voting shares are how Mark Zuckerberg kept control of Meta, and how Evan Spiegel maintained his hold of Snap. The list is long. What’s unusual is the group approach.

Why do it? The seven co-founders reportedly own just 2% of the company apiece, Amodei included, and have pledged to give away 80% of their wealth, a commitment the CEO announced in January alongside a warning that AI-driven wealth concentration could destabilize society. The new shares carry no extra economic value, but they’d preserve the group’s control once the company starts trading publicly.

There are twists, too: Anthropic’s Long-Term Benefit Trust would still choose most of the board; the founders’ board seats would grow from two to three; and employees would get their own stock to break ties on some issues.

Five-year-old Anthropic was valued at $965 billion in May. The company was recently valued at $1.5 trillion on the secondary market, and its upcoming IPO is expected to reflect that new valuation.

## 7. Startup de IA atrai investimento bilionário para desafiar OpenAI e Anthropic

- **Fonte:** Folha de S.Paulo (Tec)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-25 11:22
- **Idioma:** pt
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://redir.folha.com.br/redir/online/tec/rss091/*https://c-level.folha.uol.com.br/ia/2026/09/startup-de-ia-atrai-investimento-bilionario-para-desafiar-openai-e-anthropic.shtml

San Francisco (EUA) |Financial Times Uma startup do Vale do Silício que lançou seu primeiro produto na semana passada está atraindo ofertas de investimento com uma avaliação de vários bilhões de dólares, sob a promessa de que suas ferramentas de IA são mais eficientes do que as da OpenAI e da Anthropic.

Fundada pelo ex-pesquisador da OpenAI Diogo Almeida, a TypeSafe AI apresenta seu "modelo de decisão" Jev como uma alternativa mais barata, para determinadas tarefas, aos grandes modelos de linguagem que sustentam o ChatGPT e o Claude.

Voltado quase exclusivamente a desenvolvedores de software, o Jev despertou enorme interesse no setor de tecnologia e viralizou nas redes sociais desde que a TypeSafe saiu do chamado modo furtivo na semana passada. Seu vídeo de lançamento no X alcançou 40 milhões de visualizações em menos de uma semana.

Antes do lançamento do Jev, a startup era avaliada em US$ 200 milhões (R$ 1,04 bilhão). Mas já está recebendo sondagens de potenciais investidores com ofertas de financiamento que a avaliariam em US$ 10 bilhões (R$ 51,9 bilhões) ou mais, segundo pessoas a par do assunto.

O entusiasmo reflete uma preocupação crescente entre as empresas com o custo da implementação da IA. A aposta da TypeSafe é que muitas tarefas rotineiras hoje atribuídas a dispendiosos modelos de uso geral podem, em vez disso, ser executadas por sistemas mais baratos e especializados. Se a ideia ganhar força, poderá pressionar os modelos de negócios de empresas de IA de ponta como OpenAI e Anthropic.

Almeida disse ao FT que teve a ideia do Jev enquanto trabalhava na OpenAI, há quatro anos, ao se perguntar se os chatbots seriam a melhor ferramenta para todas as tarefas que poderiam ser atribuídas à IA.

"Vamos supor que uma revolução econômica baseada em IA realmente tenha ocorrido: que porcentagem dessas solicitações à IA seria destinada ao consumo humano... em comparação com o consumo por computadores?", relembrou.

O argumento da TypeSafe é que os LLMs, projetados para interagir com seres humanos, são inadequados para o tipo de tarefa programática, repetitiva e de grande volume que muitas empresas globais tentam automatizar com o uso de IA.

Escritórios da TypeSafe. O Jev não produz frases, explicações nem imagens, mas promete decisões rápidas e baratas dentro de aplicativos de software © TypeSafe

"O ChatGPT é muito mais inteligente do que eu. Ainda assim, por algum motivo, o trabalho em áreas nas quais há um enorme incentivo financeiro para automatizar não está sendo automatizado neste momento. Porque, atualmente, a IA é péssima nisso", disse Almeida.

O Jev não produz frases, explicações nem imagens, mas promete decisões rápidas e baratas dentro de aplicativos de software. Entre as tarefas de "classificação" para as quais pode ser usado estão, por exemplo, decidir se uma solicitação deve ser aprovada, bloqueada ou analisada, encaminhar um chamado de suporte ou avaliar riscos de seguros ou de crédito.

O modelo foi criado para ser incorporado por desenvolvedores ao back-end de seus softwares e não conta com uma interface voltada ao consumidor. Um desenvolvedor o integrou a um site de brincadeira, o "AskJev", uma homenagem ao extinto mecanismo de busca Ask Jeeves, que viralizou nas redes sociais.

James Hardiman, sócio-geral da DCVC, empresa de capital de risco do Vale do Silício que liderou a mais recente rodada de financiamento da TypeSafe, afirmou que a startup já era lucrativa graças aos custos computacionais drasticamente menores de seu produto, o que torna o uso de seu modelo "ordens de grandeza" mais barato do que o dos principais LLMs.

Ele observou que a popular ferramenta de IA da empresa para tarefas essencialmente banais chegou ao mercado em um momento de preocupação cada vez maior com os perigos dos modelos mais avançados.

"Literalmente há uma semana, todos falavam sobre o apocalipse da IA... De certa forma, o Jev trouxe alguma sobriedade a essa conversa."

Reduzir o custo de uma resposta de IA é crucial para torná-la atraente. O nome Jev é uma referência ao paradoxo de Jevons, a constatação econômica de que tornar um recurso mais barato ou eficiente pode, em última análise, aumentar seu consumo total à medida que surgem novos usos.

Enquanto os grandes modelos de linguagem percorrem longas cadeias de raciocínio, que podem consumir muito poder computacional, a TypeSafe afirma que seu modelo seleciona rapidamente uma opção dentro de uma gama limitada de respostas possíveis, com base em um cálculo de probabilidade.

Os métodos subjacentes se assemelham mais aos sistemas antigos de aprendizado de máquina, que eram determinísticos, produzindo respostas fixas às consultas e, portanto, sem alucinar.

Isso, por sua vez, reduz o número de "tokens" consumidos e, consequentemente, o custo. A empresa afirma que cada consulta é cerca de cem vezes mais barata e mais rápida de processar do que nos LLMs, cobrando aproximadamente 4,2 centavos de dólar por milhão de tokens, ante vários dólares por milhão nos LLMs.

## 14. Can Apple Home’s AI camera features outsmart Amazon’s and Google’s? I put them to the test

- **Fonte:** The Verge (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-09-25 10:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://www.theverge.com/tech/1000321/apple-intelligence-home-security-camera-amazon-ring-alexa-google-nest-gemini-home-review

A few years back, I was at a beachside Easter egg hunt, watching my kids dash through sand dunes searching for sweet treats. My phone buzzed in my pocket; I ignored it. A moment later, it buzzed again. I pulled it out, glanced down, and saw a “motion detected” notification from my security camera. “Just my dog in the yard,” I thought, dismissing it.

Can Apple Home’s AI camera features outsmart Amazon’s and Google’s? I put them to the test

Apple Intelligence has arrived on HomeKit Secure Video. Here’s how its new features compare with Ring and Nest — and how well each tells you what’s happening and what really matters.

As the colorful eggs piled up in the baskets, my phone kept buzzing. Reluctantly, I pulled it out again and saw several more “motion detected” alerts. I tried to load a video clip, but it just buffered endlessly.

AI-powered text descriptions paint the whole scene in a sentence.

When I returned home, I found my four pet chickens dead, killed by my small border terrier. Looking back over the camera footage, I saw that they had pushed open their coop door, which I, in a rush to get to the Easter festivities, hadn’t properly secured.

If instead of “motion detected,” that first notification had read “small dog chasing a chicken,” I would have raced back home and maybe saved them.

Today, that type of notification is possible. With AI-generated text descriptions, a short message describing what the camera saw can arrive almost instantly, well before a video feed loads, painting the whole scene in a sentence.

Most major security camera companies now offer text descriptions, which combine traditional motion triggers with Vision Language Models, including Ring, Google Nest, Blink, Wyze, and Arlo. This month, with iOS 27, Apple finally brought the capability to its HomeKit Secure Video service with the launch of Apple Intelligence for Home.

The surprise is that Apple is charging up to three times as much as its competition for the feature. Is it worth it? And are security cameras getting better at telling us about what actually matters?

I’ve been testing Apple Intelligence for Home since it launched in beta this summer, alongside Amazon Ring’s and Google Nest’s AI camera features introduced last year. I installed several cameras for each, but for this piece, I focused mainly on video doorbells — which I set up when iOS 27 officially arrived on September 14th. I tested a Ring Wired Doorbell Pro ($249.99), Google Nest Doorbell 2K ($199.99), and for Apple, the HomeKit Secure Video-compatible Aqara Doorbell Camera G400 ($99).

I focused on how quickly each service sent the AI-powered notifications, how accurate and useful the descriptions were at telling me what happened, and how reliably it captured and notified me about activity. I also tested other AI-powered features each platform offers, including Apple’s ability to search and stitch together activity across multiple cameras, Ring’s Unusual Event that distinguished normal activity from out-of-the-ordinary events, and Google’s ability to trigger automations based on what a camera sees.

The AI features on all three platforms are a significant improvement over their basic smart alerts. It’s now easier to understand what’s happening at a glance and decide whether to react, and security cameras feel more useful and less annoying overall.

Ultimately, the real value of AI in cameras isn’t in describing what they see, but in using that knowledge to figure out what you actually need to know, and eventually what your home should do about it.

What Apple Intelligence provides, at what price

How to use Apple Intelligence for Home

You need an iCloud Plus plan, a HomeKit Secure Video-compatible camera, an Apple Home Hub running HomePod/tvOS 27, and an AI-capable iPhone running iOS 27. Three iCloud Plus tiers support Apple Intelligence on one or more cameras:

- 2TB plan: $9.99 a month (or Apple One / $39.95 a month) for one camera

- 6TB plan: $29.99 a month for two cameras

- 12TB plan: $59.99 a month for up to five cameras

- No tier supports more than five cameras.

Note: standard HomeKit Secure Video recording also requires an iCloud Plus plan, starting at $0.99 for one camera (50GB plan), $2.99 for five (200GB), and $9.99 for unlimited (2TB). All tiers that include Apple Intelligence support basic HKSV features on unlimited cameras.

Apple Intelligence is on by default for Reduce Notifications and off by default for Summarize Videos. To adjust, go to Settings in the Home app, scroll down to Apple Intelligence, toggle on Summarize Video, and select participating cameras.

With Apple Intelligence, a compatible camera connected to Apple’s HomeKit Secure Video service can send you text descriptions through the Apple Home app: “A dog sat on the floor,” “a cat played in the room,” “someone delivered a package.”

It also enables natural-language video search, surfaces “noteworthy” events, stitches relevant clips together, and adds a reduce notifications feature that combines related events into a single alert that updates. This is in addition to the existing smart alerts, facial recognition, and 10 days of video storage available with HomeKit Secure Video.

Pricing is where it gets tricky. Apple Intelligence for Home is bundled into iCloud Plus: $10 a month covers one camera, $30 covers two, and $60 up to five (see sidebar). No tier supports more than five cameras.

By comparison, Google Home’s Nest cameras offer Gemini-powered text descriptions, video search, facial recognition, and a daily Home Brief digest — which summarizes the day’s activity in a short paragraph (sometimes with a fictional slant) — for $20 a month, covering unlimited cameras and 24/7 recording on wired models.

Amazon’s Ring also charges $20 a month for its AI text descriptions, video search, facial recognition, unusual events alerts, and clip recording on unlimited cameras, with 24/7 costing an extra $3 per camera on wired models. Ring has its own reduced notification option that rolls similar events from the same camera into one notification, such as “Brian is mowing the lawn.”

Apple says the least

Text descriptions are a game changer for security cameras. For me, getting an alert that says “A FedEx delivery person is walking on the lawn” rather than “person detected at front door” is the difference between getting up and going downstairs or carrying on working. But quality and accuracy vary wildly, even within a single provider.

Sometimes I get useful details, other times a few words that tell me almost nothing. Then there were the times when the descriptions were detailed, but laughably wrong: “A tennis serve simulator is rolling on the walkway,” “A gray hoverboard is driving on the pathway” — both misidentifications of a robot lawn mower.

In my testing, text alerts from Google and Ring provided the most context and specificity, and Apple often didn’t try, saying “someone” rather than providing any detail. For example, it would say “someone is walking in the yard,” whereas Ring and Google provided more context: a delivery person is walking in the yard, a person in uniform, someone carrying a bag, and so on.

Apple was also the briefest. For example, when my cat was on the front porch, Apple said, “A cat sat on the porch.” Ring went with “A cat is standing on the pathway outside the porch.” Google Home told me which cat: “A black and white cat walks across the porch.”

All three identified delivery trucks, correctly flagging UPS and FedEx vehicles driving past, as well as delivery people approaching the door. Apple was the only one to ID the USPS van pulling up to my mailbox, although not consistently.

Google was the most descriptive with vehicles — “a light-colored car drives by on the street” or “a white van,” “a dark-colored SUV” — Apple and Ring mostly said “a vehicle drove by,” sometimes with a type (van, car, truck).

Apple’s descriptions were the hardest to digest at a glance, particularly the combined ones through reduce notifications. For example, “Someone seen. Someone carried a box” is clunky. Ring and Google merge the events more fluidly into “a person carried a box up the driveway.”

In fairness, Apple Intelligence in Home only officially launched this month, so I expect this to improve, but as things stand, Ring’s and Google’s are more useful and cost less.

Finicky facial recognition and alerts

For a doorbell camera, the real advantage of AI descriptions is knowing who’s there right away. All three offer facial recognition alongside the ability to spot a UPS driver or similar.

Ring was the most consistently accurate, identifying me every time I approached the door. Apple hedged its bets, sometimes identifying me, sometimes saying “Someone who may be Jennifer Pattison Tuohy,” and other times just “Someone.” Google got it right about half the time; the other half it thought I was my daughter’s best mate (a change from the UPS driver it pegged me as for most of last year).

Text descriptions are more useful if they show up, and quickly. But on the doorbell camera I was testing, Apple’s often didn’t. Apple Home reliably alerted me when someone pressed the doorbell, and it recorded every event, but notifications about other motion events were inconsistent.

A potential issue here is that, unlike its competition, Apple doesn’t control the whole experience. HomeKit Secure Video only works with third-party hardware — there are no Apple cameras yet. I was using the G400, Aqara’s latest wired doorbell camera.

When I first set it up, alerts arrived in Apple Home with no issues, but at some point they stopped, and no amount of troubleshooting brought them back. However, alerts kept coming through in the Aqara app. I also still got doorbell ring alerts in the Home app, and notifications from other Aqara cameras continued to work.

The Google Nest doorbell captured everything and was consistently first to notify me of an event. Ring was also fast and mostly reliable; its notifications showed up just after Google’s, though I’d occasionally get “This video is unavailable” when trying to play back footage.

Reliability is Apple’s biggest fix, plus some innovation

The good news is that recording in HomeKit Secure Video has improved significantly with iOS 27. Apple said it completely rearchitected the system, which addresses a long-standing problem with missing clips.

I’ve noticed that cameras load faster, are more stable, and no longer miss recordings. An Eve outdoor camera that was almost always offline has been stable since the upgrade, and the Aqara Camera Hub G5 outdoor camera and G350 indoor camera have also been solid. The G400 doorbell camera has been the only problem child, with missing notifications, but it records consistently.

All the other HomeKit cameras I tested reliably sent alerts; only the Aqara doorbell camera had the issue, but even when I didn’t get alerts, I could use video search to find events.

Typing “When did the USPS truck come” in the Apple Home app pulled up every instance the Aqara doorbell spotted the mail delivery. In comparison, while the Nest Cam didn’t identify the USPS truck as often, its video search results contained more specifics, including an event where the mail person dropped off an Amazon package.

Of the three, Google’s video search was the most useful, followed by Ring’s, then Apple’s, which directly correlates with the amount of detail each provides in the text descriptions.

Apple has two features Ring and Google don’t offer. The first is clip stitching, where Apple Intelligence identifies someone or something on one camera, recognizes it again on another, understands these are related, and plays the sequence as a single video in the Home app. I haven’t found a real use for this so far, but it’s been fun for tracking my dog around the house when I’m out.

The second feature is bringing events that Apple judges important to the top of the search box. In the last two weeks, it’s only highlighted the trash pickup ━ I guess my life just isn’t that noteworthy.

Here’s where Ring wins

One of the biggest problems with security cameras is filtering out the important info from the sheer volume of everything they see. Even with AI notifications turned on, I still get a stream of alerts from Apple Home and Google Home. Some are more useful than they used to be, but overload is still an issue.

While Apple’s noteworthy events feature shows promise, Ring’s Unusual Event feature is the best solution I’ve seen. It learns what normal traffic looks like for each camera, then notifies you only when something isn’t normal. This summer, I cut my Ring notifications down from around 40 a day to just two or three.

Instead of getting an alert every time my cat is on my porch, I only get one when a raccoon or possum walks by. It doesn’t send alerts when my son comes home from school, but it does when he comes home at 2AM. The camera by my chicken coop no longer sends a series of notifications when someone is feeding them or collecting eggs — but it does alert me when I am out there at midnight with a flashlight to wrestle a snake.

What’s next: cameras that can act

The natural progression here is automations triggered by the camera understanding what it sees. Turn on the sprinklers if there’s a raccoon in the backyard, but not if it’s just my dog. A cat at the back door broadcasts an alert through my smart speakers so someone can let them in. The trash truck rolling past without stopping sends an urgent notification to put the bins out before it comes back the other way.

This is where cameras move beyond being simply security devices to become intelligent smart-home sensors that can understand what’s happening and potentially act on it.

Google Home recently added the ability to trigger automations from camera alerts, although in my early testing, it’s not reliable enough.

I tried it with my three cats and a pet feeder using Google’s new Pet Memory feature. But instead of only dispensing food for one specific cat as I asked, it triggered every time it saw a cat. At least the cats were happy.

The problem is accuracy. When AI can’t reliably identify the subject, the automations can’t respond reliably. So, my earlier example of Apple accurately recognizing the USPS van half the time would mean any automation I set up to alert me the mail is here would only work half the time.

Today, Apple doesn’t have this capability, but if the rumors that the company is developing its own video doorbell and security camera are true, I would expect to see a feature like this come to Apple Home.

The privacy tradeoff

The smarter cameras become, the more they need to know about your home. For a system to understand what’s normal, it needs to learn who lives there, when they come and go, which pets are around, and which vehicles belong. This raises very real concerns around privacy and surveillance.

Ring has faced particular scrutiny over its cloud-based architecture and partnerships with law enforcement. Where Google and Apple can start with local processing, Ring processes all footage in the cloud by default.

There’s real benefit in a system knowing everything about my home — I’d just prefer to be the only one who knows it.

Last month, Ring announced an overhaul to that model, introducing a new default encryption system called TAKE. This rotates encryption keys for cloud-stored video and deletes Ring’s copy after 24 hours. Among other privacy protections, including no admin access, Ring says this means it can no longer hand over decrypted footage to the police. However, it can still be compelled to provide encrypted files.

With Apple’s standard HomeKit Secure Video, your home hub processes footage locally and encrypts it end-to-end before sending it to the cloud for storage. But adding Apple Intelligence changes that: Video summaries are now processed in the cloud using Apple’s Private Cloud Compute (PCC), where they are unencrypted for processing.

According to Apple, PCC writes the captions, sends them back, and then “permanently deletes the data from PCC.” Apple support docs also state that there is no admin access, and nothing identifies who the footage belongs to.

Google takes a similar approach to Apple, but without end-to-end encryption; footage is encrypted in transit and at rest. Gemini for Home-enabled Nest cameras can use some on-device processing, but the heavy work is done in the Gemini cloud, in line with Google Home’s privacy practices.

A big question for me is whether all this intelligence has to live on someone else’s computers. There’s real benefit in a system knowing everything about my home — I’d just prefer to be the only one who knows it. For privacy, security, and potentially speed, local AI processing and storage feels like the right answer. Manufacturers including Anker Eufy, Reolink, Ubiquiti UniFi, and UGreen already have or have announced products that do this.

With a new Apple TV, HomePod, and a possible smart display all rumored to be imminent, Apple’s next generation of hardware might shift more processing back into the house — which could mean paying less every month for the privilege.

Is Apple Intelligence worth it?

Based on my testing of the beta and the initial full release, Apple Intelligence in Home has brought substantial improvements, finally putting HomeKit Secure Video in contention with Ring and Google.

Whether it’s worth the price depends on how many cameras you own. If you have one — say, a video doorbell — Apple Intelligence is a reasonable deal at $10 a month if you also need 2TB of iCloud storage. You get competent descriptions, better video search, and Apple’s privacy position. Even with multiple cameras, you can get some benefit by enabling it on just one.

Next let’s make them better at telling us what actually matters.

However, if you want AI capabilities across your home, including clip stitching, the math gets bad fast. At five cameras, you’re paying $60 a month for Apple to tell you “a cat sat on the porch” or “a vehicle drove by.” The competition gives you more detailed descriptions on as many cameras as you own for $20 a month.

Today, you’re paying an Apple premium for less. But this is Apple’s first move, and I expect it to improve. AI has unlocked genuine utility here, making security cameras better at telling us what’s happening; next, let’s make them better at telling us what actually matters.

A few years ago, a “motion detected” alert wasn’t enough to tell me that my chickens were in trouble. Today, AI can tell me it’s a dog chasing a chicken. The next step is for it to understand that this is something I need to know right now and make sure I get that information quickly enough to act. Eventually, an intelligent home might actually be able to do something about it, too.

That’s the smart home I want. But we’re not there yet.

## 15. Brasil tem capacidade de atrair investimento em IA, questão é como se posicionar, diz BlackRock

São Paulo | Reuters O Brasil tem capacidade de atrair investimentos relacionados à inteligência artificial, mas a questão é como se posicionar de uma forma mais competitiva, afirmou nesta quinta-feira (24) o presidente-executivo da BlackRock Brasil, Bruno Barino.

"O Brasil tem a energia, que é o mais complexo de pegar em várias localidades, e toda a capacidade de fazer. A questão agora é como desenvolver um arcabouço regulatório e competir o recurso a nível global", afirmou o executivo em evento da Monte Bravo em São Paulo.

Barino disse que uma vantagem dessa cadeia de IA é que muitas das operações são vinculadas em dólar, então já existe um hedge cambial no investimento.

O executivo destacou que o cenário da BlackRock não é de bolha de IA, citando que há um "ramp-up" de custos, mas em breve começarão a ser observados os benefícios na produtividade em toda a cadeia.

Barino afirmou ainda que a IA deve levar a um volume de investimento de US$ 5 trilhões (R$ 26 tri) a US$ 8 trilhões (R$ 41,5 tri) nos próximos quatro a cinco anos quando se pensa na cadeia longa, que começa no data center e vai até a aplicação final. "Isso reconfigura totalmente o mundo."

Segundo um estudo do Reglab (Centro de Estratégia e Regulação), a IA pode acrescentar quase R$ 1 trilhão à economia brasileira entre 2027 e 2030. O valor corresponde à soma, em valor presente, dos ganhos de produção estimados para os quatro anos considerados na pesquisa.

No cenário central do relatório, a IA elevaria o PIB em 2,77% no período, ou uma média de 0,69 ponto percentual por ano. A maior parcela do efeito, 65%, viria do aumento da produtividade dos trabalhadores. Os 35% restantes decorreriam da incorporação da tecnologia a máquinas, equipamentos e outros ativos produtivos.
