Ilustração mostra o logotipo da Anthropic e um teclado. — Foto: Dado Ruvic/ Reuters

A Anthropic, startup de inteligência artificial responsável pelo Claude, deve estrear nesta quinta-feira (19) na Nasdaq, bolsa americana que reúne algumas das maiores empresas de tecnologia do mundo.

Com a oferta pública inicial de ações (IPO, na sigla em inglês), a empresa chega a um valor de mercado de US$ 1,5 trilhão (cerca de R$ 8,8 trilhões).

Segundo o jornal "The New York Times", a Anthropic e alguns de seus acionistas levantaram US$ 81,3 bilhões (R$ 478,3 bilhões) com a venda de 980 milhões de ações, a um preço médio de US$ 83 (R$ 488) por papel.

Em uma operação separada, a Anthropic pretende levantar outros US$ 26 bilhões (cerca de R$ 150,54 bilhões) com a venda de ações para grandes investidores, como Google, Nvidia e AMD.

Conheça a empresa

Dario Amodei, atual CEO da Anthropic, fundou a empresa em 2021 com a irmã, Daniela Amodei, e outros ex-executivos da OpenAI.

O grupo defendia uma abordagem mais cautelosa no desenvolvimento da inteligência artificial. Dario deixou a OpenAI, criadora do ChatGPT, por divergências sobre o ritmo de avanço da tecnologia e os riscos envolvidos.

Segundo a agência Associated Press (AP), a Anthropic se comprometeu a priorizar a segurança no desenvolvimento da chamada inteligência artificial geral (AGI, na sigla em inglês).

A AP também destaca diferenças nos modelos de negócio das duas empresas. A Anthropic concentra sua receita na oferta do Claude para clientes corporativos. Já a OpenAI passou a explorar anúncios como forma de gerar receita com os usuários da versão gratuita do ChatGPT.

O Claude chegou ao público em março de 2023, cerca de cinco meses depois do ChatGPT. Apesar de ter sido lançado mais tarde e ainda ser menos conhecido do público em geral, o chatbot ajudou a impulsionar o crescimento da Anthropic.

Em agosto deste ano, a Anthropic se tornou a startup de IA mais valiosa do mundo, com uma avaliação de cerca de US$ 965 bilhões, superando a OpenAI.

Entre os recursos lançados pela Anthropic estão o Claude Skills, que permite à IA executar tarefas específicas, e as ferramentas Claude Code e Claude Cowork, que ajudaram a popularizar o chatbot ao permitir a realização de tarefas diretamente no computador do usuário.

Segundo Diogo Cortiz, especialista em IA e professor da PUC-SP, o lançamento do Claude Code, ferramenta de programação com inteligência artificial capaz de criar sites, marcou um avanço importante para a Anthropic e ampliou a visibilidade da empresa.

"Ele permitiu que a IA realizasse tarefas de longa duração, que podem levar horas ou até dias, por meio de subagentes, robôs que executam etapas do trabalho de forma autônoma", explica Cortiz.

Segundo Izabela Anholett, especialista em IA e fundadora da Nura AI, o Claude também ganhou espaço entre profissionais de marketing e criação. "Ele virou o queridinho desses profissionais para a produção de textos criativos, roteiros para redes sociais e peças de campanha, sendo considerado superior ao ChatGPT nesse tipo de tarefa", afirma.

Ao mesmo tempo em que desenvolve modelos cada vez mais potentes, a Anthropic defende uma pausa global no avanço da IA. A empresa alerta para sinais de que sistemas mais avançados poderiam escapar do controle humano e argumenta que as pesquisas de segurança precisam de mais tempo para acompanhar a evolução da tecnologia.

Por que o Claude Mythos tem acesso restrito

No início de junho de 2026, a Anthropic lançou o Fable 5, apresentado como seu modelo de IA mais poderoso já disponibilizado ao público. Segundo a empresa, o sistema se destaca em tarefas de programação, análise de dados, pesquisa científica, interpretação de imagens e raciocínio complexo.

O sistema faz parte do Mythos e, segundo a companhia, alcança desempenho de ponta em áreas como engenharia de software, análise de dados, pesquisa científica, visão computacional e tarefas complexas de raciocínio.

"Se o Mythos for parar em mãos erradas, empresas podem ser paralisadas da noite para o dia", afirmou Izabela. Segundo ela, o risco está na capacidade da ferramenta de identificar e explorar falhas de segurança em sistemas digitais.

"O Mythos Preview [versão de teste] já encontrou milhares de vulnerabilidades de alta gravidade, incluindo algumas em todos os principais sistemas operacionais e navegadores web", afirmou a Anthropic.

"Devido ao seu alto poder e aos riscos que representa para a segurança nacional e geral, ele não foi liberado para o público comum. Em vez disso, a Anthropic criou um consórcio de empresas e agências governamentais com acesso à ferramenta", explicou Diogo Cortiz.

Entre os integrantes do grupo estão Amazon, Apple, Microsoft, Google, Nvidia, Broadcom e Mozilla.

O cofundador e CEO da Anthropic, Dario Amodei, ri ao lado do presidente dos EUA, Donald Trump, e do presidente da Câmara dos Representantes dos EUA, Mike Johnson, republicano da Louisiana, — Foto: Jonathan Ernst / Reuters

Antes, em março, a Anthropic já havia entrado em conflito com o governo Donald Trump por divergências sobre o uso de sua tecnologia para fins militares. Em meio ao impasse, o Departamento de Guerra dos EUA informou à empresa que ela havia sido considerada um risco para a cadeia de fornecimento.

A medida foi tomada depois que a Anthropic impediu que o órgão utilizasse sua tecnologia para fins militares.

Diante do impasse, Trump determinou que as agências federais dos Estados Unidos deixassem de usar programas de IA da Anthropic.

O presidente afirmou que não permitirá que a empresa determine como os militares americanos devem agir, uma decisão que, segundo ele, cabe aos líderes das Forças Armadas nomeados por seu governo.

## 3. Anthropic raises $81bn in record-breaking stock market debut

- **Fonte:** BBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-19 12:58
- **Idioma:** en
- **Cobertura:** 2 veículos noticiaram o caso

Anthropic, the company behind AI chatbot Claude, has raised a record $81bn (£62bn) in a stock market debut that will make it one of the most valuable companies in the world.

A surge of investor appetite to buy shares in the loss-making business meant more stock was sold than originally planned. Anthropic's shares have been priced at $83, up from the proposed $70-$78 range.

Shares will begin trading later on Thursday in New York, valuing the five year-old firm at around $1.5 trillion.

The flotation is being seen as a crucial test for fast-growing AI sector. Anthropic is the first purely AI-focused firm to publicly sell its shares.

Vast amounts of money have been poured into AI infrastructure and the development of AI based tools. But questions are being raised over whether and when those investments will pay off.

The seven founders, who set up Anthropic up in 2021, will become multi-billionaires immediately.

The company's $1.5tn estimated valuation is on a "diluted" basis, a broader reflection of the firm's value that takes things like employee stock options into account. On a basic share count Anthropic is worth about $1.1tn, more valuable than Walmart and Samsung but less than Tesla and Meta.

But strong demand could push its share price, and overall valuation, higher, once shares are publicly traded.

One early investor in the company who spoke to the BBC on condition of anonymity, said the original sale was heavily oversubscribed, with demand ten times the number of shares available.

Anthropic's revenues have ballooned in the last year, to $33bn in the nine months to September this year, as the market for its tools, designed to speed up processes including coding, writing and digesting information, has grown.

But net losses have also increased, reaching $104bn for the same period, according to the company's IPO document.

Leah Bramwell from Canaccord Wealth said there had been strong interest in Anthropic shares "across the board" including both institutional and retail - that is private individual - investors, which she said suggested it was not simply a "retail-driven frenzy into into AI names".

Crucial test for AI

In total Anthropic could raise $107bn. Over $81bn comes from the public share sale, with an additional $26bn from separate investments from a group of tech firms including Google, Microsoft and Nvidia.

Anthropic could raise a further $12bn through an additional provision, which is normal practice, to permit further shares to be sold if demand is very high.

Elon Musk's SpaceX raised an initial $75bn in its IPO in June this year.

The company soared in value when it joined the Nasdaq, reaching $2.2tn, although in the weeks that followed, the share price fell.

OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT and Anthropic's biggest AI rival, is expected to go public next year. Until then, Anthropic will be the only public company in the US that is entirely focused on the technology.

While there is excitement around Anthropic's listing, there is also some concern from investors about its future ability to generate profits in a competitive area.

Yang Wang, a principle analyst at Counterpoint Research, said the central issue was whether it could "keep pace" as hundreds of billions of dollars in financial obligations come due in the next couple of years.

However, the share sale would give investors "direct exposure to a frontier-model developer with the potential to shape the next stage of productivity", Wang said.

Rapid rise

Anthropic was started by seven former OpenAI employees, led by brother and sister Dario and Daniela Amodei.

Dario Amodei has been outspoken about the risks posed by AI, amounting in his view to a possible threat to human existence.

The company has called for a slowdown in the development of AI, and a greater focus on safeguards while forging ahead with plans to grow quickly and boost its financing through the public markets.

OpenAI has voiced similar sentiments and delayed its plans to list on the stock market in order to address AI safety concerns.

Technology stocks have lost value in recent weeks following the US midterm elections. Democrats, who now control of both chambers of Congress, are expected to be tougher on AI regulation. Some Democrat candidates opposed the rapid build of data centres required to run AI.

But Anthropic went ahead with its market listing, despite the market volatility, because it needed to tap into additional funds, said Marta Norton, who heads strategy at investment firm Empower.

"AI is financially demanding," Norton told the BBC. "The public markets open up the financial spigot a little bit more and so the time has come."

Broader anxieties about the AI industry remain.

Competition from low-cost rival models is growing.

Expensive computer chips, necessary for the industry to operate, are in short supply.

And the US Federal Reserve is concerned about high AI-related debt being financed through "complex and opaque" structures, according to minutes from its October meeting to discuss interest rates, as well as the high concentration of AI exposure among a small number of firms.

## 4. Introducing Tool Retrieval in the Gemini API

- **Fonte:** Google (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-19 15:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 9.0
- **Link:** https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemini-api-tool-retrieval/

Today we're launching Tool Retrieval in the Gemini API, available in preview in the Interactions API.

When an agent connects to dozens or even hundreds of tools, sending every full parameter schema on every request adds up fast. It inflates input token counts, adds latency, and lowers tool-selection accuracy as the candidate list grows. Tool Retrieval lets your agent keep its full tool library on standby and load parameter schemas only when the current task needs them.

How it works

To turn on Tool Retrieval, add a tool_search tool to your request and set defer_loading: true on the tools you want to hold back. The model still sees the name and description of every deferred tool, so it knows what's available, but the parameter schemas stay out of context. When the model needs a tool, it asks for it by name, the API loads the full schema, and the model calls the function, all inside the same request.

from google import genai

client = genai.Client()

tools = [

{"type": "tool_search"},

{

"type": "function",

"name": "issue_refund",

"description": "Refund an order to the original payment method.",

"defer_loading": True,

"parameters": {

"type": "object",

"properties": {

"order_id": {"type": "string"},

"amount": {"type": "number"},

},

"required": ["order_id", "amount"],

},

},

\# ... 50+ other deferred functions (leave frequent tools undeferred)

]

interaction = client.interactions.create(

model="gemini-4-hydrogen",

input="Order A-1042 arrived damaged. Please refund 40 dollars.",

tools=tools,

)

Inside that single API call, the model searches for issue_refund, the API loads its schema, and the model emits the function_call. All three steps appear in interaction.steps, making it easy to inspect which tools the model considered when debugging tool names or descriptions:

"steps": [

{

"type": "tool_search_call",

"id": "tsc_7f2a",

"arguments": {"function_names": ["issue_refund"]},

"signature": "..."

},

{

"type": "tool_search_result",

"call_id": "tsc_7f2a",

"result": [{"type": "function", "name": "issue_refund", "parameters": {...}}],

"signature": "..."

},

{

"type": "function_call",

"id": "fc_91c3",

"name": "issue_refund",

"arguments": {"order_id": "A-1042", "amount": 40}

}

]

From here it's normal function calling: your code runs the refund and sends back a function_result.

There's no index to build or host. Gemini runs the search on the server, so the only changes to your code are the new tool and a flag on the tools you want to defer. You can keep your most-used tools loaded up front and defer the long tail.

Tool Retrieval also plays well with prefix caching. Deferred schemas stay out of the start of the prompt, and the schemas the model loads are added later in the conversation, so the cached prefix stays the same from turn to turn.

Tool Retrieval also works with remote MCP servers in the Gemini API. Set defer_loading: true on an mcp_server tool and every tool that server exposes is loaded on demand, so you can connect a large server without listing its tools one by one.

How client-side retrieval works

If you maintain your own tool registry, embedding index, or per-user permission filter, declare the tool_search with "execution": "client" and define your search parameters. Client-side retrieval adds one round trip per search, because your app runs the lookup. Tools do not need to be declared upfront, so you can also expose new tools mid-conversation when a user connects an integration:

tools = [{

"type": "tool_search",

"execution": "client",

"name": "search_tools",

"description": "Search the internal tool registry for functions.",

"parameters": {

"type": "object",

"properties": {"query": {"type": "string"}},

"required": ["query"],

},

}]

\# When the model emits a function_call for "search_tools",

\# return matching {"type": "function", ...} schemas in function_result:

followup = client.interactions.create(

model="gemini-4-hydrogen",

previous_interaction_id=interaction.id,

tools=tools,

input=[{

"type": "function_result",

"name": "search_tools",

"call_id": step.id,

"result": my_registry.search(**step.arguments),

}],

)

How pricing and supported models work

Tool Retrieval has no separate charge. Input and output tokens are billed at the standard rates for the model you call. Turning on tool_search adds a small, fixed block of input tokens, and each schema the model loads is billed as input for the rest of the conversation, the same as a tool declared up front. You save tokens when the schemas you never load outweigh the ones you do, which is the usual case for large libraries. Measure it against your own traffic.

Tool Retrieval works with Gemini 3.6 and later models, including all Gemini 4 models, in the Interactions API.

## 7. Introducing harness adapters: adapt your favorite model to your harness to improve performance and cost

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-19 21:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 8.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/harness-adapters-at-scale

Combining RL on a custom task distribution with your favorite agent harness yields more accurate and efficient models for that target harness and task distribution.

Models are not fully optimized for all harnesses and the tasks you’re doing. Instead, they target general performance. So when you combine a model and harness, you’re leaving performance and efficiency on the table for the stuff you’re actually doing. With RL, you can train a custom harness adapter that squeezes everything out of your model for your harness and task combination.

Take Qwen3.8-27B, its Terminal-Bench 2.1 score with the pi harness is 39% which uses on average 2.9K tokens per task. If we freeze those weights and add a custom RL-trained LoRA adapter we can get 55% pass@1, with average token usage of 1.5K. Most of this is down to the model reasoning less, calling less tools, and performing better first time.

Our earlier work (multi-harness RL, harness adapters) showed that RL on your own task distribution, inside your own harness, gives a more accurate and more efficient model. Those experiments covered data-analysis and code-building tasks. Here we wanted to push this further by exploring whether the result holds at 27B, on harder real-world software engineering tasks, across three harnesses: Pi, OpenCode and Hermes Agent. And most of all, share a fully reusable recipe, so you can reproduce this on your own task, harness, model combinations.

- We trained one LoRA adapter for Qwen3.8-27B per agent harness (pi, Hermes Agent, OpenCode), with RL inside the harness itself.

- Datasets, training and agent rollouts all happen on the Hub in Jobs using TRL's AsyncGRPOTrainer, OpenEnv and harbor.

- Each harness adapter beats the base model under its own harness on both benchmarks, for both performance and efficiency.

- The three LoRAs are also merged into a single super adapter that runs unchanged under pi, Hermes Agent and OpenCode.

- All artifacts (adapters, GGUFs, RL tasks, safeagents plugin) can be browsed through this Hugging Face collection.

We leverage the Harbor integration in OpenEnv and TRL’s OpenEnv guide to train the model inside the harnesses we chose for the final training. We have built ready-to-use docker images for all our RL tasks that come with the target harnesses already pre-installed inside. In our final docker images, we use Pi 1.5.2, OpenCode 2.2.7 and Hermes Agent v0.22.6. The diagram below from the aforementioned blogpost explains well the entire protocol:

Most RL frameworks train a re-implementation of the agent loop: the agent is written against the framework's own interface, such as verl's AgentLoop, SkyRL-Gym environments, rLLM's agent and environment classes, slime's custom rollout functions, or ART's client inside your own Python agent. Here the harness runs exactly as it ships, and the trainer learns from the exact tokens it produced, recorded by a proxy between the harness and vLLM. Recent work moves in the same direction: Agent Lightning v1.0 keeps the deploy-time harness in the loop through a proxy ("harnessed agentic RL"), and SkyRL's Harbor integration trains on Harbor trials, converting their chat histories into tokens for the trainer.

For this recipe we created a custom dataset for agentic coding with the Hub, OpenEnv, and Repo2RLEnv. We combined together task retrieval of existing RL envs, task generation with Repo2RLEnv, task validation with OpenEnv, and sampling into a single data pipeline. This gave us the highest quality set of validated and relevant tasks to improve Qwen3.8-27B on agentic coding. In this section, we’ll walk through how we built it, so you can try it out on your own domains.

RL training needs tasks the agent can attempt, a verifier that scores each attempt, and evidence that the verifier is right. A verifier that accepts wrong answers teaches the wrong behavior. A task that is already feasible will not teach the model anything.

This section describes how we built the task set from three sources: two on the Hugging Face Hub and a third generated with Tasksmith, a pipeline in Repo2RLEnv. Every task passes an eligibility screen with openenv validate, and the same difficulty check against the base model, Qwen3.8-27B.

Each task uses the Harbor format, which Terminal-Bench 2.1 also uses. A broad search of the Hub for Harbor environments returned about 59,000 tasks, but they included duplicates, evaluation sets and tasks marked as not for training. We filtered down to just two Hub sources built for training and generated a third set ourselves:

- SWE-smith (Python, Go) injects bugs into real Python and Go repositories, with an LLM rewrite or a mutation such as a flipped operator. The tests that catch each bug become the task's tests. It adds volume.

- SWE-Gym, packaged as Harbor tasks in TaskTrove, turns real GitHub issues and their merged fixes into tasks. It adds real-world bugs.

- Tasksmith generates tasks from merged pull requests. An LLM agent writes the instruction and tests, and Tasksmith checks the result: the reference and a valid alternative must pass, while the unfixed code and a known-wrong solution must fail.

We used Tasksmith to target gaps. We mapped the skills that our target benchmarks test (Terminal-Bench 2.1, the hard subset of SWE-bench Pro, and DeepSWE), then chose pull requests that exercise them, such as HTTP streaming, filesystem operations and serialization. From 30 merged pull requests, Tasksmith produced 24 tasks in 10 Python repositories that passed its checks, 17 of them on a second run. Its checks do not replace ours, so these tasks go through the same pipeline as the rest.

A metadata screen runs first. It keeps tasks from training splits with permissive licenses. It drops duplicates, tasks marked as not for training, the 253 tasks in our target benchmarks, and any task from a repository those benchmarks use. We then ran the semantic checks from openenv validate on every task in fresh trials:

- The reference solution earns the maximum reward (semantic.oracle_max).

- Doing nothing scores clearly lower (semantic.floor_gap).

- No canary scores more than doing nothing (semantic.canary_floor).

- A replay of the reference gives the same reward (runtime.episode_determinism).

Each trial ran as its own Hugging Face Job, which let us validate dozens of tasks at once. openenv validate offers the same option for any Harbor task:

openenv validate path/to/task --sandbox hf-sandbox

858 of 1,127 Hub candidates passed. Wrong-answer controls caught defects, including a Tasksmith task that accepted an incomplete implementation and a Terminal-Bench 2.1 verifier (regex-log) that accepted known-wrong answers. Most failures were infrastructure problems, such as images that did not start. Failed tasks stay in a rejected split with the failing check recorded.

A valid task can still teach nothing. GRPO (group relative policy optimization) scores each attempt against the other attempts at the same task, so a task where every attempt passes, or every attempt fails, gives no learning signal. Before training, Qwen3.8-27B attempted each task 4 times, or 8 times for a stratified sample of six tasks per repository.

Of 442 scored tasks, Qwen3.8-27B solved 151 every time and 120 never. The 171 tasks it solved only sometimes form the qualified set. The figure below shows how the scores pile up at both ends and how they differ by source. The model solves most Go tasks every time, fails every attempt on more than a third of the SWE-smith Python tasks, and lands in the middle on 73% of the SWE-Gym tasks.

Qwen3.8-27B results on 442 scored tasks. Green marks the qualified tasks, which the model solves on some attempts but not all.

This example shows how we filtered and validated 1,127 tasks to 278 which we included directly in the final dataset. The pipeline then took the remaining 522 rejected tasks, repaired and complexified them with Tasksmith and repeated validation.

The pipeline applies two filters: validation removes broken tasks, and the difficulty check keeps tasks the base model solves only sometimes. This section tests whether those filters change what the model learns. We trained four short runs of up to 50 steps, each on tasks from a different stage of the pipeline, and compared them with the base model on Terminal-Bench 2.1.

Each run trains Qwen3.8-27B with LoRA and GRPO, using TRL's AsyncGRPOTrainer, on 32 tasks. All four use the same agent harness (OpenCode with up to 48 model calls), the same settings and one 8×H200 Hugging Face Job, and the reward is the verifier's pass or fail. The task sets differ in how they were filtered:

- Unvalidated: 24 accepted tasks plus 8 that failed validation.

- Validated: 32 accepted tasks, chosen without regard to difficulty.

- Unqualified: 32 accepted tasks that the base model always or never solves.

- Qualified: 32 accepted tasks that the base model solves on some attempts.

The figure below shows how the tasks impacted model learning. The table lists each run's steps and the checkpoint we evaluate. The chart tracks training reward across subsets of tasks with differing validation status.

harness-hub/agentic-coding-envs holds 871 tasks from 35 repositories. Hub tasks are capped at 40 per repository, so no single project dominates. The table below shows what each source contributes. The dataset has two views: every accepted task, and the qualified subset for GRPO training. Rejected attempts stay in a separate config.

We leveraged the stable version of AsyncGRPOTrainer from TRL, with the main advantage of the trainer being that it does not wait until all rollouts are completed before moving to the next training optimization step.

For more detailed reading on the trainer and its building blocks, please refer to the AsyncGRPO blogpost.

To obtain our final harness adapters, we trained LoRA adapters on top of Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B using TRL's AsyncGRPOTrainer. The adapters were attached to the model using the all-linear flag. To maximize the training efficiency, thinking mode was turned off during rollout stages, and we evaluated our final adapter with and without thinking for broader comparison.

The final reward function consists of the verifier's correctness, combined with a small tool-efficiency bonus (0.1, derived from early experiments from the previous iteration of harness adapters which showed that 0.1 worked best).

We performed a small LR sweep on 50 steps using Termgrade environments (1e-5, 5e-5 and 1e-4) and noticed that 1e-5 barely moved training correctness (from 0.52 to 0.50), 1e-4 collapsed the rollouts instead of improving them (turns per rollout moved from 18 to 6, with entropy rising), and only 5e-5 clearly improved (from 0.49 to 0.60 by step 40). Longer 5e-5 runs then showed signs of instability (collapsing rollout lengths, rising entropy), so we settled on 2e-5 for the final runs.

Each harness adapter was trained using NVIDIA 2xB200 GPUs, with one GPU allocated for the vLLM server and the other allocated for the training process. We first performed some stress testing to maximize the amount of RL tasks that can be trained within a single optimization step. For that, we ‘maxed out’ the --token-budget argument from AsyncGRPOTrainer to its limit for the setup that we had. The chart below shows the evolution of the number of groups per step for each harness (in blue: Pi, green: OpenCode and orange: Hermes).

As the model gets better and better during training, it is able to fit more and more groups during each training step, which diversifies the signals that the model gets at each optimization step.

The final data mixture consists of 750 tasks from harness-adapters RL environments and 300 tasks from termgrade. In order to maximize the diversity of the sampled tasks during training, we have slightly customized the data sampling strategy, which will be explained below:

Before training, the tasks are put in a fixed order that interleaves them by family (i.e., their repository for our generated RL environments. For Termgrade, we just sorted them by name since each task is considered as its own family), so every training step mixes as many repositories as possible while following the distribution of the mixture.

We also plug the safeagents tool, in order to block any misbehavior from the model during training. The final plugin (which consists of the regex and pattern rules specific to the RL tasks and test path) is hosted in harness-hub/qwen-safeagents-plugin and can be used out of the box using the latest version of safeagents in case anyone wants to reproduce the training setup.

The number of blocked tools from safeagents decreases consistently during training, which indicates that the model gets ‘safer and safer’ after each optimization step. Below is a diagram of the safeagents blocked calls per 1000 tool calls, by training step, for each harness adapter.

Merging models often combines their capabilities while keeping most of what each one could do. Once the three harness adapters were trained, we asked two questions. Does merging them cost accuracy? And can a single "super-adapter" be accurate and efficient under every harness?

We started by comparing the weight updates of the three adapters, layer by layer. The cosine similarity between any two of them is about 0.01–0.02: each adapter pushed the model in a nearly orthogonal direction during training. That's the best case for merging, since the updates barely interfere.

We concatenate the three LoRA adapters along the rank dimension (PEFT's combination_type="cat"). The merged adapter has rank 96 (3 × 32) and applies exactly w · (ΔW_pi + ΔW_hermes + ΔW_opencode). Prior work on merging (task arithmetic, TIES, KnOTS) finds that scaling each update below 1 works best, so we compared three weights on all 89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks, under each harness, with the step-120 adapters:

- ⅓ (the average): each harness keeps only a third of its own update.

- ½: each update at half strength.

- 1 (the plain sum): every update applied in full.

The plain sum over-applies the updates. The agents become very short (13 tool calls per task, against 49 for the base model), and while Hermes peaks at 62.8%, pi falls back to the base model's level. The average dilutes each harness's own update. ½ gives the best balance, so we built the final super-adapter from the released checkpoints (pi step 160, hermes step 160, opencode step 150) at weight ½.

Under each harness, on the tasks every run graded (50–51 on SWE-bench Pro, 82–85 on Terminal-Bench 2.1), one attempt per task (in parentheses, the efficiency gain with respect to the base model):

"Thinking" is the chat template's thinking mode at its default effort (xhigh). The specialists were trained and served with thinking off.

With thinking off, the super-adapter improves on the base model on all six harness–benchmark pairs (mean pass@1 39.5% → 49.0%; 71 tasks newly solved vs 34 lost, p < 0.001).

Despite not being trained at all with thinking on, it's the strongest model we evaluated with thinking mode. Against the base model thinking at the same effort, it wins on five of six pairs and ties the sixth (mean 55.5% → 63.5%; 67 newly solved vs 37 lost, p = 0.004). The largest gains are on SWE-bench Pro: +16 points under hermes and +20 under opencode. Under pi on SWE-bench Pro, where thinking alone already reaches 74%, it ties.

Use the specialist harness adapters when thinking off. Each harness's own adapter is ahead on four of six pairs, by the most under pi (64.0% vs 46.0% on SWE-bench Pro, 56.5% vs 48.2% on Terminal-Bench). Splitting one model across three harnesses costs pi the most of its gain. If you only use one harness and keep thinking off, take its specialist.

The super adapter is also much more efficient. On the tasks both the base model and the super-adapter solve, with thinking off, it makes 53–73% fewer tool calls and generates 44–60% fewer tokens per task, with fewer tool calls on 111 of 130 tasks. With thinking on, it still cuts tool calls by 31–48% and tokens by 24–43% on SWE-bench Pro. On Terminal-Bench, tool calls drop by 19–25% and token counts stay about the same.

The ½ weight was chosen on Terminal-Bench 2.1 with the step-120 adapters, so the Terminal-Bench numbers are slightly optimistic; SWE-bench Pro played no part in the choice. Full paired results are in the super-adapter's model card.

This section reports what happened during training, then how the adapters score on held-out benchmarks inside and outside their own harnesses.

Training ran for a cumulative total of 598 steps, with a total of 78,114 samples trained, which consists exactly of 167M trained tokens.

During training, we see a clear signal that the model learns to solve tasks in fewer and fewer steps, with no clear sign of saturation. Despite having some clear increasing trend sometimes, training correctness seems to overall stay flat. This can be explained by the fact that the model meets new tasks during training, but the number of groups that fit in a step’s token budget more than doubles as trajectories get shorter. The accuracy gains show up on the held-out benchmarks in the section below.

On the other hand, safeagents was able to block multiple suspicious attempts. Across the three training runs, a total of 1.3M tool calls have been checked, and a total of 647 tool calls have been blocked.

This section covers the evaluation setup, the in-harness results, transfer across harnesses, and an out-of-distribution check.

We evaluate each adapter inside the harness it was trained in, on two agentic coding benchmarks: all 89 tasks of Terminal-Bench 2.1 (1 hour and 100 agent steps per task) and the 51 hardest tasks of SWE-bench Pro (2 hours and 100 agent steps). We cap the maximum number of tokens per task to be 131072. We do not tune the agent settings and use the default setup for all harnesses. For example, Pi does not keep previous reasoning turns while OpenCode and Hermes do, which mainly affects the SWE-bench Pro results due to rapid context overflow. Therefore you cannot compare the results across different harnesses (using Pi does not necessarily lead to better results than Hermes or OpenCode), but it is fair to compare results within the same harness and draw conclusions.

Every rollout runs through the same Harbor and OpenEnv stack as training, in HF sandboxes, with one attempt per task at temperature 0.8 and thinking off. Each LoRA checkpoint is merged into the base weights and served alone with vLLM on a single GPU, at the same concurrency as the base model's eval.

For all the evaluation benchmarks, we report the pass@1 as well as the number of tool calls, total turns, for all tasks and for solved tasks.

For openness, all model trajectories for final evaluations are pushed in this public bucket: harness-hub/harness-adapters-trajectories.

All three specialist adapters beat the base model under their own harness, on both benchmarks. On SWE-bench Pro (hard 51), pi goes from 39.2% to 64.7% (+25.5 points: 15 tasks newly solved vs 2 lost, p = 0.002), hermes from 29.4% to 43.1%, and opencode from 35.3% to 51.0%. On the full Terminal-Bench 2.1, pi gains +16.3 points (16 vs 2, p = 0.001) and hermes +14.1 (19 vs 7, p = 0.03). Opencode gains +6.0 overall, and +13.6 on the 66 tasks that played no part in choosing its checkpoint (12 vs 3, p = 0.035).

## 15. Anthropic’s 7 founders cashed out nearly $2B in record-breaking IPO

Anthropic became a public company on Thursday, raising a total of $81.3 billion, including $1.95 billion for its seven founders. As we previously reported, each of them ponied up about 3.4 million shares apiece to sell in the IPO.

At the upgraded IPO price of $83 per share, that means they each pocketed about $280 million before underwriting fees and taxes. It looked like they had calculated the number of shares they planned to sell to give them each around $250 million if shares hit $74. That was the midpoint price from Anthropic’s original price range. So that’s a cool $30-ish million more for each of them than perhaps they were gunning for.

Anthropic implied in its IPO filings that the founders aren’t entirely in this for the money though. The company said that each of the founders has pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in the company to philanthropy.

However, if any of them struggle to live on their quarter of a billion, they can scale back their charitable commitments. The company points out that these commitments are “voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent and do not constitute legally binding obligations.”

With the IPO in the record books, there were a few other interesting numbers worth highlighting.

$104.8 billion. That’s really the amount Anthropic raised for its coffers (with a possibility of $117 billion, if the bankers buy all of their allotment of shares). In addition to selling its Class A common stock, it also sold a previously announced $26 billion worth of a special non-voting Class N stock to some of the biggest tech names in the industry.

Nvidia bought $10 billion of these Class N shares, Google bought $10 billion, and AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC divvied up the rest.

Just for comparison: When Facebook had its then record-breaking IPO in 2012, it raised $16 billion on IPO day.

28% stake to Amazon and Google combined. All told, at the higher IPO share price, Google wound up owning 13.4% of the company. Amazon also now has a big stake in Anthropic because when it previously invested in the AI lab as a startup, it was granted promissory notes that could be converted to equity. It converted a small percentage of them as part of the IPO, leaving it with a 14.6% stake. Had it converted all of them, Amazon could have owned nearly 24%.

The two large cloud providers now own 28% of Anthropic, the AI lab says.

More than $1 trillion. As mind-blowing as it is to raise nearly $105 billion, giving Anthropic nearly $230 billion of working capital available (along with capital it already had on hand), the company’s need for future capital is still far greater.

Anthropic needs about $70.7 billion just to cover its compute and infrastructure spending commitments in the next 12 months as of the quarter ended September 30, 2026. It needs $451.6 billion to cover these commitments before the end of 2030. And it has racked up hundreds of billions of other commitments that are either conditional (and may not happen) or haven’t been priced yet.

All told, the company has disclosed a ballpark of $1.05 trillion to $1.08 trillion in infrastructure commitments in its foreseeable future. This was a huge IPO. But it is a pricey business to run.
