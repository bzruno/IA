The outspoken Liberal senator Andrew McLachlan has raised the alarm about the approach of his party and the Labor government to AI, saying artists’ copyright must not be “sacrificed” for investment and building new datacentres must not be prioritised above environmental concerns.

As Labor considers copyright changes in an AI policy suite including datacentres, online safety, energy resources and economic benefits, the South Australian backbencher added weight to mooted constitutional challenges as to whether watering down copyright law would be an illegal acquisition of property.

“The works [artists] create must not be sacrificed on the altar of global corporate profiteering,” he said.

“Artists’ property rights must be respected and protected. It is unimaginable that as a nation we might value the machine over beauty created by the artist.”

Although the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, has declared it would be “theft” if creatives weren’t paid for their work, some AI companies have rejected making agreements under existing copyright laws, arguing it is too difficult.

AI companies have said they need legal certainty on the copyright issue before investing in Australia, with Anthropic putting forward an “opt-out” model which would allow it to scrape music, books and journalism unless a rights holder signalled an objection.

Leading voices in the recording industry have raised alarm about any changes to weaken existing copyright law.

McLachlan said the work of artists “nourishes the soul of our nation”, and demanded art be prioritised over the demands of big tech.

“The principle of fair pay for a fair day’s work must not be forgotten in the age of social media platforms, datacentres and artificial intelligence,” he said. “AI provides amazing opportunities for our nation. If there are trade-offs then the costs must be clearly articulated and understood.”

Labor and the Coalition have largely agreed on AI-related issues; Senator David Pocock has complained about a major party “stitch-up” on AI regulation. This week, the shadow home affairs minister, Jonno Duniam, and the shadow cybersecurity minister, Aaron Violi, said AI was “too important to become an unnecessarily partisan political contest” and they would “engage constructively” to ensure an “enduring and multipartisan approach” to the technology.

But fellow Coalition members Ross Cadell and Michaelia Cash have backed concerns raised by Aria that granting AI companies permission to scrape copyrighted material from the internet could amount to an acquisition of property by the government; the constitutional imperative that the government must acquire property “on just terms” is emerging as a key concern in the copyright debate.

McLachlan said any potential compensation arrangements must be considered on an individual basis for each artist, adding it would be difficult to ensure an industry-wide scheme would be fair.

“As a Liberal, I believe property rights are foundational to a healthy democracy and a strong economy,” he said.

“The state should never seek to compulsorily acquire or trade away an individual’s property rights except in very limited circumstances and not without just compensation.”

Labor and the Coalition are both in favour of attracting more AI investment and datacentres to Australia but the opposition has warned the government against “selling out” Australian rights holders in a rush to secure investment.

The growth of datacentres in Australia is expected to increase emissions and power costs for households, prompting backlash from environmental groups and some communities.

McLachlan, who has spoken strongly on conservation and environmental issues, said: “Datacentres must not be built at the expense of our biodiversity.

“We must explore ways to build sovereign AI capability in a sustainable way.

“I choose real forests over virtual ones.”

Labor announced has it will set up a new AI safety regulator and implement six key obligations on AI companies after a breach of government sites by OpenAI.

Labor’s AI package is expected to be unveiled before the end of the year.

## 4. Meet the seven co-founders of Anthropic

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-14 09:15
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/14/meet-the-seven-co-founders-of-anthropic.html

Four researchers, a former journalist, a public policymaker, and an engineer founded Anthropic in 2021. Six years later, the artificial intelligence company is about to go public, with trading expected to begin on Nov. 19, in what will be one of the biggest IPOs on record.

Those seven co-founders are poised to each be worth many billions of dollars.

It all started with a defection.

At the end of 2020, siblings Dario and Daniela Amodei were vice presidents at OpenAI, the company that kicked off the generative AI frenzy with the launch of ChatGPT. But the two had grown frustrated with their employer over its approach to AI safety. Fearing a world where AI models' capabilities would progress faster than our understanding of them, the duo launched Anthropic early the next year.

They took a handful of OpenAI colleagues with them: Tom Brown, the company's research engineering lead on GPT-3; Jack Clark, a policy director; Jared Kaplan, a contract research consultant; and Sam McCandlish and Chris Olah, both members of OpenAI's technical staff.

They were all deeply concerned about OpenAI's ambitions to focus on commercialization, and they now stand to be some of the first to cash in on the AI boom.

"They really started Anthropic not because they were hoping to get rich, but they started Anthropic because they like wanted to shape this really important thing in the world, and they thought that other people might do it worse than them," said Jeffrey Ladish, who joined Anthropic as a contract safety engineer in September 2021, and now serves as the executive director of AI safety institute Palisade Research.

Collectively the co-founders own about 10% of Anthropic. Each has about 240,000,000 class A shares, or about a 1.4% stake.

During Anthropic's most recent funding round, those shares — valued at $58.90 each — amounted to about $14 billion in value for each co-founder. If Anthropic's IPO reaches its target of $70 to $78 per share, or a market valuation of nearly $1.4 trillion, it would push those stakes to $17 billion to $18 billion.

"It's extremely hard not to be influenced by your incentives," Ladish said. "You have investors, and money is not just like an end; it's also a means."

Anthropic did not respond to requests for comment.

Dario Amodei

The face of Anthropic is CEO Dario Amodei. He holds the most shares of the bunch: 245,259,100 including shares subject to voting proxies.

Amodei, 43, didn't get his start in business. He comes from the world of research, obtaining a doctorate in computational neuroscience from Princeton University in 2011. He then went to work at Stanford University as a postdoctoral scholar from 2011 to 2014.

Even in the private sector, Amodei's experience was focused on research. He worked at China's Baidu, leading research on speech recognition from 2014 to 2015, followed by a stint at Google Brain working on deep learning and neural networks, foundational scientific frameworks for today's large language models. He joined OpenAI in July 2016.

On the Big Technology podcast in July 2025, Amodei said he got into AI to solve the "almost intractable" and "slow moving" problems of biology.

"I think my reason to start a company was that I had worked at other companies and I just didn't feel like the way those companies were run was really oriented towards trying to have that impact," he said, adding that the desire for impact was often used by companies as an insincere ploy to recruit talent.

At OpenAI, Amodei is credited with co-inventing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), alongside Anthropic co-founder, Tom Brown. RLHF is a fine-tuning method used to align model outputs with human needs. That could mean training a model to speak politely or to not offer violent or harmful solutions to problems.

Daniela Amodei

Four years her brother's junior, Daniela Amodei, 39, serves as the president and board chair of Anthropic. The Amodeis are both San Francisco natives, growing up in the Mission District, just a few miles away from Anthropic's current headquarters.

The pair were teenagers during the height of the dot-com bubble and its subsequent fallout, both attending Lowell High School, a magnet school and one of the most prestigious public schools in the country. After graduating, Daniela went on to study English literature and politics at the University of California, Santa Cruz, earning her degree in 2009.

Daniela started her career in politics, working for Matt Cartwright, a Democrat representing what was then Pennsylvania's 17th congressional district, and then moved into tech, taking on various roles in risk management at Stripe for five years.

In a June interview with Stanford's Graduate School of Business, she said her experience in politics, specifically campaigns, naturally led her to startups and Silicon Valley.

"I was like, 'Wow, a small number of people that work really hard, who are young and driven, can really change the world,'" she said.

Tom Brown

Anthropic's chief compute officer, Tom Brown, 39, is perhaps the most entrepreneurial of the bunch. After graduating from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology with a Master of Engineering in 2010, Brown went on to found two startups, MoPub and Grouper.

MoPub was a mobile advertising business acquired by Twitter in 2013. There he served as founding engineer in 2011. Grouper, where Brown served as co-founder and technology chief, was a Y Combinator-backed group dating app.

After Grouper, Brown pivoted to research, sharing on Y Combinator's Lightcone podcast in August 2025 that he was self-taught on machine learning research, a daunting task since he received a B- in linear algebra at university.

"I kind of flip-flopped on it for six months trying to build up courage to" make the switch, he said.

Alongside co-inventing RLHF, as a researcher at OpenAI, Brown led the training efforts on GPT-3, a model that marked a historic leap from GPT-2, going from 1.5 billion parameters to 175 billion.

Jack Clark

Jack Clark, 37, the head of public benefit at Anthropic, left his career as a journalist in 2016 to work at OpenAI. Clark is now also the leader of the Anthropic Institute, the company's internal think tank that explores AI's social and economic impacts.

A native of the U.K., Clark grew up in the seaside town of Brighton and later studied English literature and creative writing at the University of East Anglia. He then went on to write for publications including ZDNet UK, The Register, and Bloomberg.

In an email to a colleague at Bloomberg at the time, Clark said his decision to leave journalism was because he had "never believed in something strongly before" and that he needed to join OpenAI "and live with the consequences, which I hope are good."

Jared Kaplan

Kaplan, 42, Anthropic's chief science officer, still holds a position as an associate professor in the department of physics and astronomy at Johns Hopkins University. The theoretical physicist got his PhD in physics from Harvard in 2009, working for four years after as a postdoctoral fellow at the SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, a particle physics research facility run by Stanford and funded by the Department of Energy.

Kaplan co-pioneered scaling laws, or the notion that large language models get exponentially better when trained on larger and larger data sets, alongside Dario Amodei. He also pioneered "constitutional AI," or a method of aligning models by using a list of safety principles and policies. His day-to-day work concerns safety assessments for models, responsible scaling policy at the company, and existential risks.

In an interview with The Guardian in December 2025, Kaplan noted that the decision over whether or not to let AI "recursively self improve," or write the code to enhance itself without a human in the loop, would be "in some ways the ultimate risk, because it's kind of like letting AI kind of go," and that he expects this risk will be taken sometime between 2027 and 2030.

Sam McCandlish

Anthropic's chief architect, Sam McCandlish, 36, came to OpenAI as a fellow focused on AI safety. He also led early research on coding tool Codex, which now constitutes a significant portion of OpenAI's revenue.

McCandlish claims he and the rest of the Anthropic co-founders were not following an entrepreneurial spirit when they charted out on their own. In a December 2024 video from Anthropic's YouTube channel, he said, "None of us wanted to found a company. We just felt like it was our duty."

McCandlish, like Kaplan, also came from the world of academia, completing his PhD in theoretical and mathematical physics at Stanford in 2017 and working at Boston University as a postdoctoral fellow the year afterward. His work focused on quantum gravity and tensor networks.

Now, his work focuses on pre-training, or the first step of training a large language model on swaths of data, among other things.

Chris Olah

Chris Olah, 34, heads Anthropic's interpretability research, a branch of study in machine learning that attempts to understand how a large language model "thinks" — or rather, which data inputs and reasoning methods it uses to arrive at its outputs.

Like McCandlish, Olah was not originally keen on starting a company. In the same December 2024 YouTube video, Olah said, "I was arguing for a long time that we should do a nonprofit instead, and just focus on safety research."

Olah had a nontraditional path to Anthropic, dropping out of the University of Toronto after one year and working for short stints as a developer at the government agency Environment Canada and software startup Xelerance.

His career truly took off when he was awarded a Thiel Fellowship in 2012, which he used to pursue research pertaining to 3D printers for two years. He then joined Google Brain, first as an intern and then as a researcher, before moving to OpenAI in 2018.

While together the seven founders may only own about 10% of total equity, they will have a majority vote. Founder LLC, the legal entity set up that owns a single Class F share, gives Anthropic's co-founders a 50.1% majority voting control over the company. While Benjamin Mann, a member of Anthropic's technical staff and also an ex-OpenAI employee, is often called a co-founder, he is not an owner of Founder LLC.

The co-founders are expected to collectively sell over 20 million shares during the IPO, resulting in roughly $250 million in cash per founder, according to Anthropic's prospectus.

They plan to dedicate 80% of the wealth from their personal Anthropic equity to philanthropy, according to the IPO filing, though the pledge isn't legally binding. Anthropic is also setting aside 5% of its shares to form a new public benefit fund — to finance nonprofits, research, and potentially direct community grants — with potential to grow the size of the fund as the company itself grows.

## 5. Beware the New AI-Fueled Ransomware Boom

- **Fonte:** Wired AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-14 10:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.5
- **Link:** https://www.wired.com/story/beware-the-new-ai-fueled-ransomware-boom/

Welcome to Kernel Panic, a weekly newsletter by Lily Hay Newman and Matt Burgess from inside the new world of privacy and digital security. To receive this newsletter in your inbox each week, sign up here.

With so much going on all the time, you may not have thought about ransomware and data extortion attacks in a minute, even though they’ve been among the the biggest cybersecurity threats to institutions—and, by extension, people—over the past decade. But in the new era of AI-powered and automated cyberattacks, the danger posed by ransomware is only increasing as cybercriminals profit by combining established methods with new techniques made possible or more accessible by AI.

It is difficult to determine exact numbers of ransomware attacks, given that victims don’t always disclose incidents and attackers wildly inflate their claims. But researchers who specialize in tracking and attempting to de-duplicate the field agree that claimed incidents have spiked over the last few months. Some put the number of alleged ransomware attacks on leak sites—where groups post alleged victims—at nearly 3,000 attacks over the summer. This would mean a 20 to 40 percent increase since the spring by some estimates. And, crucially, researchers emphasize that while volume always fluctuates somewhat, ransomware attacks overall have been consistently and concerningly elevated in recent years.

“What we see is most of the recent increase was fueled by international attacks; the United States was a little more steady, but still high, still a lot of attacks,” says Cynthia Kaiser, former FBI cyber deputy director who now leads research for the anti-ransomware platform Halcyon. “Internationally we’re seeing a lot more attacks. That to me is the really new part, and I do think it is in part fueled by AI.”

Fully AI-powered ransomware attacks have yet to emerge, perhaps because the criminal business model already works so effectively. But cybercriminals do appear to be using LLMs for some of the more mundane aspects of their attacks.

“We are seeing AI relied on more heavily in certain aspects of the ransomware attack chain, such as creating better phishing emails and in the negotiation process, but the ‘successful’ ransomware groups are still largely human operated,” says Allan Liska, a longtime ransomware researcher and threat intelligence analyst at Recorded Future. “But the fact that these groups are experimenting heavily with AI means that this could change going forward, and change quickly.”

Kaiser notes that broader trends in scamming are always relevant to ransomware, because attackers often use the same types of phishing and social engineering techniques to gain initial access to targeted systems. In Japan, for example, successful scamming has spiked drastically in recent months as AI enables foreign attackers to generate consistently convincing content in Japanese for the first time.

A few ransomware groups—namely, the Gentlemen, Qilin, and Akira—have emerged as some of the most prominent and stable operations in recent months. However, in their shadow is a range of smaller groups, emerging actors, and fragmentation of the ransomware ecosystem. In recent weeks, multiple new ransomware groups have appeared and quickly claimed to have attacked dozens of businesses. Halycon’s analysis says around 111 ransomware groups made claims of attacks between July and September this year.

One new group calling itself Global Secret Group claimed attacks on 48 victims, with more than half of these being listed on the same day, according to security firm ReliaQuest’s analysis. Another, called Storm, listed 71 victims in just 20 days. Not all of the attacks may be legitimate, but they still cause uncertainty for businesses.

“That growth has all happened because, in my eyes, this acceleration that AI has provided to these threat actors, essentially allowing for them to go out and start their own groups,” says Brandon Tirado, the director of threat research at ReliaQuest.

Instead of being beholden to traditional “ransomware-as-a-service” providers, all of the researchers Kernel Panic spoke to say they have seen more lone-wolf operators branching out. Tirado says his team recently managed to identify who is behind one new group and found that it is being run by an inexperienced teenager who has made more than $800,000 in under two weeks. He declined to name the teen publicly.

Halcyon’s Kaiser agrees that more actors are using AI to attempt to strike out on their own with expanded capabilities to trick targets, exploit vulnerabilities, check their code, and even compromise other groups’ remote-access tools to gain redundant access to a victim in case one avenue gets blocked. But she adds that it’s still not clear how successful these upstarts will be long-term. She suspects that many of them may surge initially and then burn out, because they don’t have the institutional knowledge and expanded resources of an established group.

While established attackers typically conduct “double extortion” attacks in which they deploy traditional ransomware that encrypts a victim’s systems while also exfiltrating a target’s data and then threatening to publish it online, those operating independently may be more likely to focus solely on data extortion to simplify their operations.

“The number of attacks that Recorded Future has seen this year already dwarfs the total number for 2025,” Liska says, “but the number of attacks involving encryption may actually decrease year over year.”

## 7. ‘The social contract is broken’: young people on feeling shut out of careers

‘You can do everything right and still lose’ – Aamir, 25

“I’ve been demoralised by the process, just constantly applying, then interviewing. I’ve just been applying to any jobs that I could find. I’d really like to be employed by now. Some companies don’t even give rejection emails … they’re not obligated to reply to the prospective interviewee, but they should be compelled to.”

By Aamir’s estimate, he has applied for more than 1,000 jobs since graduating last year. The 25-year-old has a BA in media and communications and an MA in media studies from two Russell group London universities.

He landed a temporary digital publishing role at a media company, but the contract ended after a few months. Two years later, he is living with his parents in east London, claiming universal credit and looking for work.

“I would like to not be on benefits,” he says. “I would like to have a proper entry-level job in what I studied, but I haven’t been able to get anything. I’m trying.”

Aamir also attends interview coaching through the government’s Restart programme and is completing free Google qualifications to strengthen his CV.

“The universal credit people told me I’d done everything right,” he says. “You can do everything right and still lose.

“I feel like the social contract is broken in Britain. People keep talking about young people not working, but I don’t see anyone asking employers why entry-level opportunities have become so difficult to access.”

Although his focus is on PR and media roles, he has also applied for customer service, retail and receptionist jobs. Recently, he prepared carefully for an interview with a large company that he felt went well, only to spend weeks waiting for an update that never came.

“I just feel really demoralised … I wish they would just give me feedback so it would help for the next interview,” he says.

“I feel like my interviewing skills have improved but it’s still the same. I haven’t found a job yet.

‘Every setback is tricky to pick yourself back up from’ – Alice, 24

“It feels like a generation of talent is being wasted. We are all desperate to work, contribute to society and get on with our lives. Yet so many people have been unable to get jobs, and when they do, it is often things they are wildly overqualified for and has nothing to do with what they studied.”

When Alice, 24, went from her state secondary school to study at the University of Oxford and then on to complete a master’s degree from Cambridge, she never imagined she would end up living at home, unable to secure a job.

After finishing her studies in 2024 she spent six months working in hospitality to fund an Interrail trip around Europe, returning home to Bristol expecting to begin her career.

More than a year later, she is being supported financially by her parents – supplemented by small amounts earned through ad hoc pet-sitting and dog-walking – while applying for long-term work.

Alice has focused on roles in publishing, museums, heritage organisations, the civil service and retail, but is targeting any sector where she thinks she might have a chance, submitting 50 tailored job applications.

She has reached the interview stage five times and keeps track of every role on a spreadsheet. The process itself has become exhausting. “The worst is when you have to upload your CV, then type out your entire work history again, answer detailed questions and write a cover letter. It just becomes completely mind-numbing.”

One employer headhunted her and put her through two interview rounds before admitting it could no longer recruit anyone. She has volunteered at a charity shop for six months but narrowly missed out on two paid roles within the organisation.

She came second in the process to select a deputy manager role at a different shop and then was encouraged to apply for another similar position. However, despite feeling confident about how the interview went, she was again unsuccessful. It was a huge blow.

“I was encouraged to apply; I made relationships with people at the charity, I had the skill set required, and yet I couldn’t get the job. I feel that if I can’t get this job, I can’t get anything.”

Job hunting has taken a toll on her mental health. “There have been occasions where I’ve not been able to sleep. I’ve cried in the middle of the night and just thought: ‘I don’t want to do any of this.’ Every setback is tricky to pick yourself back up from.

“I assumed I’d have a sort of nine-to-five office job,” she adds. “Maybe living away from home in a different city. Nothing exciting, just a fairly nice, normal thing to do. Instead, I just feel really stuck.”

‘Volunteering gives me a reason to get up’ – Liam, 20

“The frustrating thing is employers keep saying you need experience. But if you’ve got no experience, they should give you that. Years ago they’d train you on the job. Now it feels like everyone’s looking for somebody who’s already done it.”

After leaving school at 16, Liam, a 20-year-old from Exeter, completed a level 1 qualification in horticulture, followed by level 2 and level 3 diplomas in land and wildlife management, convinced he was building towards a career in grounds maintenance or landscaping.

Instead, he has spent the past two years trapped in a cycle of applications, rejections, short-lived jobs and disappointments.

“I’ve had interviews, rejections, ghosting, you name it,” he says. “I’ve had jobs where they let me go after a couple of weeks. It’s just soul destroying.”

One employer told him he lacked experience after a trial shift. Another kept him on for just six shifts before saying rising costs meant they could not afford to keep him.

Liam, who is autistic, believes some employers also fail to understand the adaptations that would help him succeed. “Sometimes I worry about telling employers because I think it might put them off,” he says. “I just need things explained clearly and a little bit longer to learn. They need to provide reasonable adjustments.”

He now volunteers two days a week at two different community gardens, planting wildflowers, pruning, cutting grass and helping maintain green spaces while building the experience employers say he lacks.

“Volunteering gives me a reason to get up in the morning. It gets me out of the house, builds my confidence and gives me work experience. Outdoor work is therapeutic. You’re surrounded by nature.”

Since January, Liam has also been attending Connect to Work, a government-backed scheme that helps disabled people and those with health conditions move into employment through personalised job coaching and employer support.

“They’ve really helped my confidence,” he says. “They’re very supportive and I think it’s getting me closer to employment, even if I haven’t got there yet.”

Despite everything, Liam remains determined: “It has taken a toll on my mental health. You go up, you go down, but I keep fighting. I want to be financially independent, and I still believe that one day the right opportunity will come along.”

‘One graduate can do the work of 10 with AI’– Peter, 21

“I got good A-level results and I got a first in my degree, but this hasn’t helped me at all. I’m too young to know what it would have been like a few years ago, but it seems like it was a lot easier then to get work”

When Peter, 21, decided to study computer science at Leicester University, he believed it would give him excellent job prospects. However, the reality has been quite different.

After his course finished in April he initially applied for graduate software engineering jobs in London, figuring it would be easy to commute from his home in Maidstone, Kent, where he lives with his parents.

“Sometimes I would make it to an assessment stage but I have never made it to an interview stage,” says Peter. “With these jobs, I am essentially competing against everyone in the country.

“When I was in primary school, computer science was touted as a high-paying field, desperate for new people. But now that I’ve graduated, it seems that everyone has a computer science degree.”

Peter is also well aware of the devastating impact AI is having on opportunities in the sector. “Why would you employ 10 graduates when you can have one graduate doing the work of 10 with the help of AI. One programmer can do much more than they used to.”

He has applied for more than 50 jobs but cannot afford to move away “to places like Manchester or Edinburgh”. Meanwhile, “there are no graduate-level software engineering jobs locally in Maidstone”.

Peter, who achieved As in maths, physics and computer science, and a B in further maths, believes his lack of any work experience has hampered his search for work.

Yet he remembers at school pupils being “scolded for working too much at part-time jobs when they should have been studying”.

“It seems now that this was actually the correct thing to do,” he says. “I believe that not doing part-time work while I was studying was a huge mistake.”

However, he acknowledges such experience comes at a price: “Imagine if I spent all my time doing part-time work and I got really bad grades, then people would be saying, ‘Oh, you should have studied more.’”

Peter has shifted his focus to look for “entry-level” work at supermarkets and restaurants and has also applied to volunteer at places such as charity shops to build up experience. These applications have been unsuccessful, but he has recently secured two days a week of bar work.

## 8. ‘It’s like the wild west’: how AI has rocked the literary world

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-14 06:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 3.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/books/ng-interactive/2026/nov/14/its-like-the-wild-west-how-ai-has-rocked-the-literary-world

The romance novelist Coral Hart got her break writing books for Mills & Boon, the publisher of bodice-ripper paperbacks. In 2012, she decided to dip her toe in self-publishing. She felt drawn to dark romance, a subgenre featuring twisted, toxic love stories that was taking off online but that publishers didn’t want to touch. Three years later, things were going well enough that she decided to self-publish full-time.

Kindle Unlimited, where Hart sells her books, rewards prolific writers: the algorithm favours those who can produce a new book at least once a month. Hart says she knows of authors who hire more than a dozen ghostwriters to keep pace. By herself, Hart can write 10,000 words a day, but AI could help her produce many more. Since 2025 she has written 370 novels under 21 pen names using AI, earning herself “high six figures”.

Like any Kindle Unlimited author, she is only paid per pages read: it would make no business sense for her to churn out books that readers quickly abandon. “A bad writer or a bad storyteller is not going to get a good book out of AI. You still need to understand craft. You still need to understand structure. You still need to know what a reader wants,” she tells me, speaking via videocall from her home in South Africa. “So, it’s not taking the human out of the equation. It’s taking the friction and the time and the business blocks out of the way.”

One study (awaiting peer review), led by Tuhin Chakrabarty of Stony Brook University, estimates that around one fifth of recently self-published books contain at least 25% AI-generated text. Hart now teaches courses on how to use AI to produce books. Unless disclosing the use of AI becomes a legal requirement, she advises other authors not to do so because of the public response.

“The minute someone discloses [AI use] they get death threats, they get called names, they’re ousted,” she says. Hart is one of her pen names. After she talked to the New York Times earlier this year she was doxed and faced death threats. “I think the anger and hate comes from a place of fear,” she says. In the literary world, many people fear the tech will undercut and replace them. “I think people are very afraid of AI, and people in publishing are delusional in thinking that it’s not going to overtake our industry, that their screaming on social media is going to stop it.”

But the anger comes from another place, too. If large language models can spit out books that people want to read, or produce a convincing pastiche of your favourite writer, that’s almost certainly because they were trained on established writers’ stolen work. Last year, a class action lawsuit involving around 500,000 titles won a $1.5bn settlement from the AI firm Anthropic for alleged use of the writers’ work without consent. Other similar lawsuits are ongoing. Many writers oppose AI for both moral and aesthetic reasons. One group of 70 prominent authors wrote an open letter calling on the big five publishers – Penguin Random House, Hachette, HarperCollins, Simon & Schuster and Macmillan – to promise that they will “never release books that were created by a machine”. The publishers did not promise.

So where do we go from here? Will big name writers win the argument for keeping AI out of literature, or will our bookshelves and e-readers soon be crammed full of AI-generated stories? And if a reader can’t distinguish human from machine writing, does it still matter?

One literary agent told me that when it comes to AI, the atmosphere in mainstream publishing is like “the wild west”. The technology is outpacing the industry’s capacity to reckon with what it means that authors can – and already are – using AI to write their books. “We’re at a time where it’s all very much in flux, and I don’t think anyone has good answers – but certainly everyone’s talking about it,” says Liz Gately, a New York-based book scout.

Instead, there has been a spate of cancelled high-profile book deals and accusations against writers. In March, Hachette withdrew the romance novel Shy Girl from publication over evidence the author Mia Ballard used AI. Ballard claimed to the New York Times that an editor she hired may have been responsible for inserting AI-generated text. This year’s winner of the Commonwealth short story prize, Jamir Nazir, was accused of using AI, after readers – struck by odd phrases such as “she had the kind of walking that turned benches into men” – ran the story through Pangram, a leading AI detection program. He denies it. Pangram subsequently tested other entrants and concluded that since 2025, four regional winners were likely to have used AI.

In July, agents representing the debut novelist Jerry Falade withdrew from an eye-popping $2m (£1.5m) book deal after he was accused of using AI. He denied using AI for anything other than research. And then, last month, the Canadian-Haitian author Thélyson Orélien’s novel was struck off the Goncourt Academy longlist, France’s most prestigious literary prize, after social media users ran his novel through AI detection software. He, too, denied using AI to write his novel, but did admit to plagiarism in some of his other writing, including a short story he wrote more than a decade ago. After conducting its own investigation, his publishers Boréal dropped him in late October.

Falade – who like everyone I contacted in relation to these scandals declined to speak to me on record – wrote an essay pointing out that writing by non-native speakers or in “culturally unfamiliar” English is more often treated with suspicion. He also suggests the accusations against him may be tinged with racism. “I haven’t seen this kind of investigative zeal directed at white authors accused of using AI …” he writes, “apparently that level of scrutiny is reserved for a Black boy from Nigeria.”

It is true that every writer at the centre of these AI scandals has been an author of colour. In May, the author Steven Rosenbaum – who is white – was found to have accidentally inserted fabricated quotes into his book The Future of Truth: How AI Reshapes Reality when he used AI, which he disclosed in the acknowledgements. He said he regretted the errors but added that it “serves as a warning about the risks of AI-assisted research and verification”, which is why he wrote the book. His book wasn’t withdrawn.

One big problem is that when writers are accused, it is hard for them to clear their names. There is no definitive proof either way. Literary agents and book scouts are professional readers and say most AI-generated submissions (and these days they receive many) are very easy to spot. But, clearly, human judgment isn’t flawless, and evidence suggests that non-native and neurodivergent writers are more likely to raise false suspicion. Academic research estimates that Pangram is at least 95% accurate at detecting AI-generated writing, and it has near-0% rate of false positives – suggesting it is extremely unlikely that the tech would falsely label human-generated writing as AI.

Hannah Bowman, chair of the Association of American Literary Agents’ (AALA) AI special committee, says there’s disagreement on the way forward: some agents think AI detection software should be used routinely, others advocate for relying on relationships of trust. “I think it’s a shame that all of these things are getting addressed so publicly,” she says, with many scandals starting when journalists or social media users run work through AI detectors. “I worry for authors when they don’t have a chance to make a case in defence of their work … there needs to be a fair and thoughtful process, where if publishers and agents have concerns, they can discuss them with the authors.”

In the meantime, Gately, the book scout, says many agents have started recommending their writers don’t send documents as PDFs and save multiple drafts of their work (rather than resaving the same document) in case they are subject to AI accusations. The pervasive anxiety among agents and writers manifests in strange ways. One agent told me they were shocked to receive a manuscript from a writer they had worked with for many years – and who professed to hate AI – that used suspiciously “bizarre” words. She contacted the writer, who was “horrified”: the problem wasn’t that she’d used AI; it was that she was terrified of being accused of doing so and her efforts to make her prose sound “non-AI” had backfired badly.

Another unresolved question is if and when publishers should withdraw books. If Falade’s book was good enough to impress multiple publishers and attract an astonishingly high, multimillion-dollar advance, does it matter how it was written? (Falade, incidentally, has recently signed with a new agent.)

Thad McIllroy, a publishing expert and author of The AI Book Revolution, says he was surprised when Shy Girl was cancelled. The cancellation was partly his fault. After being alerted to online rumours that the book was written using AI, he decided to run it through Pangram, and then approached the New York Times. “I felt so stupid when [Hachette] cancelled it because it never occurred to me that that would have been the response,” he tells me. He anticipated that the publishers would either clean up the text or issue a disclosure on how AI was used. Shy Girl started out as a highly successful self-published novel, and the AI rumours started before Hachette acquired the manuscript. Once bought, the publishers edited the text considerably. McIllroy says he therefore finds it hard to believe that no one raised AI-concerns internally. Plus, he says, it’s a “really powerful book” that clearly resonated with readers, so why drop it the moment the story hit the media?

Both AI enthusiasts and haters say there’s an urgent need for more guidance from publishers, who hold the purse strings and are therefore the kingmakers in the books’ world. McIllroy says publishers need to be “brave” and publish extensive AI policies, outlining how they use the technology in-house and what authors are and aren’t allowed to do. He wonders if publishing houses aren’t doing this because they are in denial about AI, or if they are worried about alienating their highest-earning writers, who are almost all vehemently opposed to the tech. Tess Callero, a literary agent with Europa Content who describes the prospect of AI-generated fiction as “scary”, says she thinks publishers are biding their time because they want to keep the door open for AI. “If the big five publishers were coming down and saying we will not accept [AI-generated writing] it would be taken more seriously,” she says. “But they’re not, and you have to wonder the reason they aren’t. Is it because they see a future in which potentially these books could be monetised?”

One issue is US copyright law, which specifies that a book must be “human authored” and considers AI-generated text public domain. “Which is why it’s so problematic for publishers – because if it’s AI, they have no protection, they can’t have exclusive rights,” says Natalie Lakosil, a literary agent and chair of the copyright committee at the AALA. US copyright law protects AI-assisted work, but if the text has been produced by a machine it risks legal challenge (even if the copyright office, which asks for little detail, accepts the registration). It seems likely, Lakosil says, that this principle will be challenged in the courts eventually, but she is doubtful that the “human authored” requirement will be substantially changed. “The purpose of copyright protection is to protect human creation, to protect artists, and AI is not an artist,” she says. That said, she does believe the law needs to find ways to accommodate writers who want to experiment creatively with AI. She says publishing is in the midst of a “freakout period” comparable to the advent of e-books, which didn’t kill the market – as many feared – but did permanently change it.

In 2023, Stephen Marche wrote the crime novella, Death of an Author, using three AI programs – it is, to anyone’s knowledge, the first AI-novel to be reviewed by the New York Times. It could only be released as an audiobook, because of copyright concerns. Marche has been experimenting with AI in his fiction since the early 2020s. “Publishing has both become incredibly moralistic and at the same time it keeps getting rocked by these scandals,” he tells me. “The people like me who are very conscious about its use, and who both understand the technology and its limits and are not using it in a cheap way, in a cheating way, we’re totally excluded. But you know the scammers will find a way.” He says he would love to see an “experimental blossoming of creative forms” using AI, as is already happening in the art world.

The way forward, Marche says, is full transparency: there is a “very clear moral line” in labelling what is and isn’t AI generated, just as we currently distinguish between fiction and nonfiction. Even those who hate the idea of AI writing (myself included) might recognise that on rare occasions it can be powerful, precisely when it is not trying to pass as human. In Vauhini Vara’s moving essay Ghosts, for example, she uses generative AI to express things she cannot bring herself to write about her sister’s death. The piece is a conversation between a human and a machine that explores the expressive limits, and possibilities, of both kinds of language.

Most likely, AI-generated books will enter mainstream publishing in some form, and will introduce more slop into the market. But Marche points out that our culture is already awash with dull, derivative stuff. He recently went to see a Hollywood action movie that was so formulaic that although it was not made with AI it may as well have been. “There’s this garbage out there that’s worthless, some of it is AI generated, some of it’s human generated. You’re trying to make meaningful shit in this context and how do you do that? Some of it’s going to be made by people. Some of it with AI,” he says.

In France, a new prize – the Prix Pascal de Littérature IA – was launched last month for fiction writers who are working with AI. It seems probable that some AI-assisted books will be good enough that discerning readers cannot necessarily tell that a human has not written them. A recent study, also led by Chakrabarty of Stony Brook University and awaiting peer review, found that in blind experiments, when expert readers (students on leading MFA writing programmes) compared passages written by their MFA colleagues and those written by AI models trained on established writers, and then fine-tuned, they tended to prefer the AI generated text. Would they still prefer the texts if they knew they were AI? Maybe some people will be happy to read such books, although even the most beautiful AI-generated text is a Frankenstein monster, borrowed feelings and observations about the human condition stitched together by an algorithm that has no human qualities, no capacity to love or to feel. But probably many readers would choose human writing. Every agent I spoke to felt committed to protecting the human. “We’re in the human creativity business, not in the business of giving robots jobs,” says Doug Wallace, managing director at Andrew Nurnberg Associates International, a network of literary agencies. AI is “not going to be a big problem because we won’t let it become a big problem”.

But then again, as a writer of course I would want to conclude there. Right now, to write without the aid of a machine is to swim against a strong cultural tide. I’ve lost track of how many friends, in professions other than journalism, have looked at me with something close to pity when I tell them I would never use AI to write, not only because of my contractual obligations or the reputational risk but because I still think I can offer something better than a machine. And yet, machine-generated language keeps cropping up in the places you’d expect to offer the greatest resistance to slop: not just in book submissions but in leading newspapers and literary magazines. Among the many minor AI news stories to bubble up while I worked on this article? A piece in the Times Literary Supplement on AI and the crisis of the author, which was removed from the website because of – you guessed it – allegations it had been written with AI.
