“でぶねこ”の重み公開 1兆パラメータのAIモデル「Mistral Large 4」（Le Chonk）がダウンロード可能に（1/2 ページ）

AI開発企業の仏Mistral AIは10月29日（現地時間）、AIモデル「Mistral Large 4」（通称Le Chonk、ぽっちゃりした猫の意味）の重みをHugging Faceで公開した。コーディングやサイバーセキュリティ分野で高い性能を示すという。商用利用もできるが、モデルそのものをサービスとして提供する場合は異なるライセンスが必要となる。

Mistral Large 4はマルチモーダル対応のAIモデルで、総パラメータ数は約1兆。「Mixture of Experts」を採用しており、入力に応じて約520億パラメータを動かす。日本語を含む160以上の言語に対応している。

コーディング性能を測るベンチマーク「Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index」では、中国Z.aiの「GLM-5.3」に匹敵するスコアを記録。1タスク当たりの平均トークン使用量もGLM-5.3より47％少なかった。またAIモデルの性能評価を手掛ける米Artificial Analysisによると、Mistral Large 4は中国企業以外が開発したオープンウェイトモデルでトップクラスの性能という。

Mistral Large 4は、Mistral AIの開発プラットフォーム「Mistral Studio」やAPIなどでも提供する。API料金は100万トークン当たり入力0.70ドル、キャッシュ済み入力0.07ドル、出力2.10ドル。

Mistral AIは6日、重みは非公開の状態で、Mistral Large 4のパブリックプレビュー版を公開していた。なお、通称にも使われている「chonk」は英語圏のネットスラングで、ぽっちゃりした動物、特に猫を指す言葉。公式サイトやプロモーション動画にもぽっちゃりした猫のモチーフが登場している。

## 2. The big questions remaining after Anthropic’s blockbuster IPO prospectus

This report is from this week's The Tech Download newsletter. Like what you see? You can subscribe here.

Anthropic's over 300 page S-1 filing earlier this week contained a lot of information. It was the most comprehensive public statement to date about how a pure-play AI lab makes its money.

After the prospectus went live on Monday, my U.S.-based colleagues and I went through it with a fine-tooth comb, detailing everything from revenue and risks to competitors and computing costs.

The filing also revealed that CEO Dario Amodei and his six co-founders each own over 200 million shares, which could be worth billions if Anthropic hits the over $1 trillion valuation it's expected to.

But despite all the nuggets, a few big questions remain.

Anthropic reported its cost of revenue as $2.38 billion in the first half of 2026, booking $16.29 billion in revenue during the same period and implying a gross margin of 85%.

Violeta Todorova, senior research analyst at Leverage Shares, told CNBC that the first-half revenue compared with just $1.16 billion in the first half of 2025, and asked whether it could grow to "keep pace with expectations."

"It is unclear how much it costs to generate each additional dollar of revenue as computing demand increases," she said.

But Anthropic's meteoric revenue growth slowed in the third quarter. It grew approximately 144% in the second quarter compared to the first quarter, followed by roughly 47% in the third quarter.

"What the prospectus didn't tell us is management's expectations around the sequential slowdown in the growth rate," said Michael Field, chief equity strategist at Morningstar.

"Substantially all of cost of revenue" is associated with compute and infrastructure costs, the filing states. It also says that operating margin and adjusted operating margin are expected to "fluctuate over time, particularly in periods when substantial incremental compute is delivered to us and we incur the associated expenses."

"Investors can see compute falling as a share of revenue, but not how much of the forward commitment is earmarked for training the next generation of models versus serving customers," Nick Patience, AI lead at the Futurum Group, told CNBC. "That split dictates future margins."

Questions also remain around whether compute costs can keep falling faster than commitments grow and if Anthropic can secure enough capacity to serve customers, he added.

Regarding demand across the AI and hyperscaler sector outstripping supply, the prospectus says that it had previously sought "quantities of compute from [cloud service providers] in excess of what those partners were willing to provide."

Later it says: "We have historically operated in a compute-constrained environment in which demand for compute has exceeded the supply available to us."

Anthropic's structure as a public benefit corporation raises questions, too. Seven co-founders, including CEO Dario Amodei, have special voting power through a single Class F share equal to 50.1% of total voting power over matters brought to a shareholder vote.

Anthropic describes its capital and corporate governance structure as having "no direct public company precedent," in the filing.

"We also know now that management plans a series of different class share issues," said Morningstar's Field. "But what we don't know is whether investors will be fine with the lack of control implied, or will this impact the share price. Something we won't know until the shares start trading."

And finally the trillion-dollar question: the IPO valuation that Anthropic targets. The AI lab is keeping schtum about that, for now. It says simply it will be "determined by negotiations among us, the selling stockholders, and the representatives."

Given analyst estimates range between $1 trillion and $2 trillion, it will be a huge market event. If it's at the higher end of the range, it could bring back some of the swagger the AI sector has lost in recent weeks. If on the lower end, things could get ugly.

Apple on Wednesday announced its first laptop with a touchscreen, which it's calling MacBook Pro with ProLED XDR Display.

SK Hynix's shares rose after the company reported record quarterly profit that sharply beat estimates, and announced over $25 billion in dividends and buybacks.

Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices said they will invest a total of $4 billion in Meta after the social media giant announced plans to raise $95 billion to support its investments in AI infrastructure.

Microsoft said Monday that it's offering buyouts to a wide swath of employees as it strives to balance a costly AI infrastructure build-out with capital efficiency.

SpaceX has closed $40 billion in debt financing to buy graphics processing units, or GPUs, used to power AI systems, Elon Musk said on Sunday.

Anthropic's S-1 was a trove of company intel, but one figure really caught my eye. The AI lab has more cash than almost every company in the U.S. And, as my colleague Leslie Picker writes, never before has a company gone public with anywhere near that much.

Meta Ray-Banで向かいの席から撮影→指の動きからスマホのパスワードを解読する盗み見攻撃

イスラエルのテルアビブ大学に所属する研究者らが発表した論文「Watch and Crack: Password Inference from Smart-Glasses Video」は、スマートグラスで撮影された指の動きから、パスワードを解読する手法を実証した研究報告だ。

カフェなどの公共の場でスマートフォンにパスワードを入力する行為は、スマートグラスの普及によって脅威に晒されている。のぞき見防止フィルムやプライバシー表示機能で画面を隠していても、指先の動きさえ録画されればパスワードなどは突破される可能性がある。

また、普通の眼鏡に見える「Meta Ray-Ban」などのスマートグラスだと撮影されていることに気が付きにくい。しかも、前方から撮影されても指を解析されるため、ターゲットは油断してしまうだろう。後ろからのぞき見されるショルダーハッキングに注意しているだけではいけないことになる。

従来の映像解析は、キーが大きいタブレットや4桁のPINコードなど単純な条件に限られていた。今回実証した手法の場合は、相手の機種やキーボードアプリ（Gboard、iOS、Samsung）の事前知識を必要とせず、両親指や人差し指での自然なタイピングから、大文字・小文字・数字・記号の4つの配列すべてにまたがるパスワードを推定できるという。

まず画像認識AI（SAM 3）と手の追跡モデル（MediaPipe Hands）で指先をサブピクセル精度で追跡し、3Dニューラルネットワークでキーを押した瞬間を特定する。バックスペースキーによる入力修正も自動で検出・除外される。

さらに、大文字や数字を入力する際に必ず押されるシフトキーや配列切り替えキーの位置を手がかりに、画面上のキーボード領域を逆算する。そして、映像から算出した各キーの打鍵確率と、約22億件の漏えいパスワードの統計データを数学的手法で統合し、正解の候補を絞り込む。16人の参加者による実験では、全体の77.8%で打鍵数の特定に成功した。

撮影距離は最大1.8mまで有効で、Pixel 10、iPhone 16、Galaxy A53といったスマートフォンの全てで効果が確認された。一方、指の移動が極端に小さく高速に打つ若年層や、迷いながら打つ人、両親指でほぼ同時に打つ人などの場合は精度が低下する限界も確認された。

## 4. Musk and Zuckerberg echo Trump's push to rebrand AI as 'super intelligence' on earnings calls. Others do not

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-30 12:14
- **Idioma:** en

A month after President Donald Trump ordered that "super intelligence" replace the term artificial intelligence within executive branch departments and agencies, his leading tech allies, Elon Musk and Mark Zuckerberg, followed suit on earnings calls. Their fellow tech CEOs were less eager to do the same.

Trump's declaration took place on Sept. 29, while he was hosting an AI luncheon at the White House for industry leaders. At the time, he was working to ensure the growth of AI in the U.S. with limited regulations. The president this week announced on Truth Social that he's banning the use of the terms "AI" and "artificial intelligence" in "all relevant documents" and "everything else."

SpaceX and Tesla adhered to Trump's wishes on earnings calls last week after Musk, earlier in October, changed the name of his rocket company's AI division to SpaceXSI. Speaking on Tesla's call about his work on humanoid robots, Musk said, "Some of them look very impressive, but they're not really using any kind of advanced AI or SI."

Zuckerberg was equally compliant on Meta's earnings call on Oct. 21, emphasizing his company's "goal of delivering personal superintelligence to everyone." However, Meta was already using the term, having created a unit called Meta Superintelligence Labs last year.

While Musk and Zuckerberg have been perhaps the fiercest supporters of the Trump administration among tech's CEOs, they're by no means outliers. Leaders of all the tech giants have contributed to Trump's inauguration festivities or his plan for the White House ballroom, and some have been intentional about getting rid of diversity, equity and inclusion, or DEI, programs that the president opposed.

But, for now at least, they're holding out when it comes to eliminating the term AI.

Amazon CEO Andy Jassy peppered his call with use of "AI" on Thursday, uttering the abbreviation well over a dozen times in his introductory remarks. A day earlier, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said one of the company's three points of focus is "designing AI to empower people." And Alphabet CEO Sundar Pichai was seconds into his quarterly earnings commentary when he said the search giant's "AI investments are driving increased usage and overall user growth."

Trump's attempted rebrand comes just ahead of the midterm elections next week, with the Republican Party in jeopardy of losing control of both the House and Senate. Trump's job approval has been dropping and his economic approval rating just hit an all-time low, according to the latest CNBC All-America Economic Survey released last week.

That's not to suggest tech execs sticking with "AI" on this week's earnings calls are openly defying the president. Trump's preferred term is seeping into other correspondence and documents. Microsoft's annual report, filed last week, included eight mentions of "super intelligence," starting at the top of the letter to shareholders.

"For Microsoft, the central question as we enter this Super Intelligence era is not just how big the opportunity becomes, but how we deliver the real-world outcomes that every country, community, and company demands and deserves," Nadella wrote.

IBM CEO Arvind Krishna, who didn't attend Trump's September lunch, used "SI" alongside "AI" during his company's earnings call last week. "Technology innovation in both hybrid infrastructure and super intelligence is leading to better business performance," Krishna said, adding that IBM is advancing as a "software-led hybrid cloud and AI platform company."

Lip-Bu Tan, CEO of chipmaker Intel, said Thursday the supply shortage the chip industry is navigating has been "driven by the unprecedented demand for super intelligence."

Some in the tech industry have been explicit in their support of Trump's terminology ahead of their forthcoming earnings reports next month.

Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora repeatedly referred to "SI" on CNBC, and posted on X on Sept. 30, "The meeting at the WH on SI was an epic moment."

Then there's Nvidia's Jensen Huang, who runs the world's most valuable company and the chipmaker that's powered much of the AI boom. Along with Musk and Zuckerberg, Huang has been one of Trump's most vocal supporters in tech and the president's top ally on the AI safety debate.

Like Arora, Huang took to X after last month's AI meeting to praise the "White House Accord on Super Intelligence."

"Super Intelligence offers an extraordinary opportunity to advance discovery, productivity, security, health, and prosperity," Huang wrote. "The future of Super Intelligence will be shaped not only by what it can do, but by the confidence it earns."

Maine Sen. Susan Collins, a Republican, was midway through defending her record during the final debate of the midterms when an audience member cut her short.

Maine’s data center revolt will decide who controls the Senate

As tech billionaires pour record millions into Maine’s high-stakes Senate race, rural voters push back against the resource strain, rising utility costs, and phantom jobs of the AI data center boom.

“I’m terrified,” a man shouted. “AI data centers are destroying our climate. They are poisoning our water.” Collins brushed off the outburst, tying the man to her Democratic challenger, Troy Jackson. But the fear echoing through the auditorium wasn’t so easily dismissed. Just days before voters head to the polls, the rapid expansion of resource-hungry data centers has exploded into one of Maine’s most volatile campaign issues.

Maine drew national headlines in April when the state Legislature passed what would have been the nation’s first statewide moratorium on large data centers — had Gov. Janet Mills not vetoed the bill 10 days later. But the fight hasn’t slowed down, with towns across the state adopting temporary moratoriums or putting the question of data center expansion to voters this November. In a September poll of Mainers by UMass Lowell and YouGov, 76 percent of respondents said they oppose building a data center in their community, with 58 percent strongly opposed.

Maine’s hotly contested Senate race is set to be the most expensive Senate race per capita that the US has ever seen, with Democrats spending a projected $81 million and Republicans $114 million. Much of that campaign cash has come from the very industry Mainers and rural voters across the country have been fighting for more than a year. The outcome of the race will help determine which party controls the Senate and help shape federal data center and AI policy, impacting data center construction not just for Maine residents, but for the entire country.

Cheap land, rich water

Maine and other rural states are becoming prime targets for new data centers. While cities run short on land and power, Maine has abundant cheap land, vacant industrial mills and buildings, and ample access to water, thanks to its numerous rivers and waterways. Recognizing the high stakes, major tech companies and private equity firms tied to data centers and AI have invested millions in the midterms to win over skeptical locals.

The issue has sparked fierce local debate, leading towns and cities across the state to enact temporary bans this summer.

Molly White, the researcher, writer, and software engineer behind Tech Influence Watch, says that voters are paying a lot of attention to the issue because they connect data centers to rising costs for utilities. As White points out, many Mainers are also already feeling the pinch due to record-high heating oil costs this fall, and “they’re now looking at an election where the question is, is this candidate going to invite a data center to move in next door, and then my electricity prices go up, or the knock-on effects are going to hurt me even more when I’m already in very dire straits?” she said.

White launched her site in 2024 to track crypto spending in political races, but has since expanded it to monitor AI and Big Tech investments. White says the playbook that AI and data center backers are using in the midterms is very similar to the one the cryptocurrency industry used in 2024.

“There’s a lot going on in this election,” White says, “but I do think it has been a big issue in Maine and across the country, where you’ll go to a political event, you’ll see people asking questions of candidates, and AI keeps coming up. Certainly there’s a lot of questions about the economy, the war, all these different things, but AI is the new one.”

“Certainly there’s a lot of questions about the economy, the war, all these different things, but AI is the new one.”

— Molly White

Data center developers and their political allies often sell rural Maine communities on long-term job creation — a promise economists say rarely materializes. In reality, economic research indicates that most data centers create virtually no net employment once construction is complete.

Historically, Maine’s industry has centered around lumber and timber, shipbuilding, commercial fishing, textiles, and pulp and paper manufacturing. The timber industry contributed around $8.3 billion to Maine’s economy in 2024, but Donald Trump’s tariffs and rising operating costs have hit it hard, resulting in mill closures and layoffs.

Mark Brewer, who chairs the political science department at the University of Maine, says Maine’s history complicates the debate. These former mill towns jump at the chance to rebuild their local tax bases and infrastructure, but past boom-and-bust cycles make residents cautious.

“Maine has a long history of extractive industries providing jobs,” Brewer says, which allowed Mainers to tolerate these industries despite the state’s environmental focus. With data centers thus far, he says, “the jobs part of it’s not there. It’s one thing to put up with a paper mill that’s employing three shifts of 1,000 workers, right? Data centers aren’t doing that.”

For many rural Mainers, the question is not only whether a data center will bring jobs back, but whether to trust the companies promising them or the government helping make the deal, said Nicholas Jacobs, the director of the Public Policy Lab at Colby College in Maine. These residents say they’re concerned about the opaque ownership of these data centers, limited long-term employment, and the risk of wealth being extracted from rural communities.

Tech millions fuel Collins’ reelection war chest

Tech and AI interests see an ally in Susan Collins, the 73-year-old Republican chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee. Like most congressional Republicans, she has largely supported data centers and AI, though she has said she believes data centers should be evaluated on an individual basis. Collins has even used her position as head of the Appropriations Committee to secure $6.2 million in federal funding for a high-performance computing hub data center in central Maine, which is designed to bolster localized AI and biotech research. Her stance has brought in a tremendous amount of campaign donations from Big Tech, AI, and data center donors for her reelection bid.

Collins’ individual donors include Alex Karp, the cofounder and CEO of Palantir; Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz of VC firm Andreessen Horowitz, which heavily backs data centers; and others from Google, Meta, and Microsoft. According to White’s donation tracker, Collins has received $325,644 from AI and crypto donors. Nearly 100 billionaires have donated to Collins’ reelection bid in some form or another.

“It’s one thing to put up with a paper mill that’s employing three shifts of 1,000 workers, right? Data centers aren’t doing that.”

— Mark Brewer

Super PACs like the Republican Senate Leadership Fund dumped nearly $42 million into ads across Maine. Elon Musk gave $158 million to the Senate Leadership Fund this summer, and has spent around $450 million of his own money to support Republicans in the midterms. Musk’s America PAC has spent around $2.1 million in Maine. Pine Tree Results PAC, to which Collins and her husband have personal ties, put $29.1 million into the race to support her. Stephen Schwarzman from Blackstone has donated $2 million this year to Pine Tree Results.

A recent FEC report flagged by the Maine Newsroom shows that Musk’s corporate SpaceX PAC gave $55,000 to Collins’ Victory Committee in August and another $50,000 to the Pine Tree Results PAC.

The campaign and PAC money appears to flow in support of just one candidate, too. Collins’ opponent Troy Jackson only recently filed his FEC paperwork, which shows no obvious data center, AI, or Big Tech ties, though it does show at least four billionaire donors. However, Jackson’s stance is similar to Collins’, and he focuses largely on putting guardrails in place for AI and data center development. He has said he wants to ensure union jobs for the buildout and maintenance of data centers in the state.

Collins’ ties to data centers don’t stop with her donors. Her family has actual stakes in the AI boom. Earlier this month, Forbes ranked Collins’ household fifth in the Senate for AI- and data center-linked investments, with an estimated $300,000 to $800,000 held in her husband Thomas Daffron’s name.

Daffron is a former lobbyist and was paid consulting fees in 2025 by the lobbying firm of Scott Reed. Reed chairs Pine Tree Results, the super PAC with close ties to Collins. And according to American Journal News and The New York Times, Reed’s firm lobbied for a developer that has built data centers in Maine. Collins’ financial disclosure shows that she and her husband hold assets worth anywhere from $4 million to $10 million.

Jackson, who started as a logger before he was elected to the state Senate, has most recently earned money from consulting, and he and his wife hold assets worth anywhere from $147,000 to $430,000. He doesn’t have any reported financial ties to data centers, AI, or Big Tech interests.

Outside money and extractive boom-and-bust fears fuel backlash

Billionaire influence tends to spark partisan backlash, White notes, adding that Mainers resent wealthy outsiders trying to buy their votes. Jacobs agrees, noting that many Maine voters harbor a deep, long-standing skepticism toward anyone from “away” looking to exploit local resources.

“Rural wealth exists, but it is extracted out of the community, and that’s the game of rural living,” Jacobs says. “You harvest your timber; you send it away. You harvest your crops; you send them away. It’s extractive, but there’s a fine line between an extractive economy and an exploitive economy. And so I do think that there is a history and maybe even a culture of wariness towards that form of exploitation.”

Brewer says he is skeptical that the huge amount of campaign spending will sway rural voters’ views about data centers. If anything, voters may punish Collins for her ties to Elon Musk and other unpopular tech billionaires.

“Rural wealth exists, but it is extracted out of the community, and that’s the game of rural living.”

— Nicholas Jacobs

Much of the campaign spending from the super PACS with a vested interest in data centers and AI has flooded the airwaves and mailboxes with hostile attack ads, Jacobs and Brewer note. With broadcast television inventory completely sold out, campaigns have shifted money into radio, social media, and direct mail. The sheer volume has pushed voters past their breaking point, Brewer says, calling the contest “the most negative race” he has tracked in 25 years of observing Maine politics.

According to a recent Maine Monitor report, as of mid-October, $54 million has been spent or committed for negative attack ads, and nearly all of it is coming from outside PACs financed by Big Tech and data center supporters, not the candidates’ campaigns. While no ads have focused specifically on Collins’ support for data centers and AI, some have targeted her ties to Big Tech, billionaires, and data centers through her husband. The National Republican Senatorial Committee tried to tie Troy Jackson to Senate Democrats’ opposition to the Husted legislation in September in a press release.

“That is where the overwhelming amount of money is going,” Jacobs says about the attack ads. “It’s ads, and then paying consultants to write the ads, polling, paying the consultants to do the polls, right? The worst case is that it tarnishes the image of democracy in voters’ minds, and I think that is the most significant consequence beyond any quid pro quo relationship.”

As Jacobs notes, many Maine voters, especially independents, are conflicted about data center investment in the state. They are deeply attached to their community and wary of outsiders changing it, while also worried their children won’t be able to afford to stay or find good jobs without the influx of tech money.

“I think many people throughout Maine worry about the future and whether or not their children will be able to afford to live there,” he says. “Whether there will be jobs. Both of these things are true … Data centers just seem to be this symbolic flashpoint of that tension in rural living.”

O valor supera os US$ 1,3 trilhão (R$ 6,99 trilhões) em hipotecas de alto risco (subprime) em aberto em março de 2007, que contribuíram para desencadear a crise financeira global no ano seguinte.

Assim como muitas hipotecas com taxas ajustáveis naquela época começavam com juros promocionais baixos nos dois primeiros anos, antes de ficarem muito mais caras, os contratos de IA do tipo "take-or-pay" (aceitar ou pagar, em tradução livre) podem obrigar as empresas a pagar por capacidade que nunca utilizarão. O setor logo poderá se deparar com seu próprio e enorme ponto de ruptura.

Ben Thompson, autor do boletim informativo Stratechery, argumenta que, desde que a Anthropic cumpra suas projeções de receita, os investidores poderão concluir que a empresa representa "uma opção de compra razoável sobre um potencial de valorização aparentemente ilimitado".

## 15. Nvidia weighs playing the cosigner for Nscale’s AI data center in West Virginia

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-30 22:20
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/nvidia-weighs-playing-the-cosigner-for-nscales-ai-data-center-in-west-virginia/

Nvidia is in talks to guarantee billions in debt financing for AI cloud provider Nscale’s planned Anthropic data center in West Virginia, The Information reported Thursday. Bloomberg later corroborated the report. Anthropic has signed a $44.6 billion contract for the project’s first building alone, according to Nscale’s IPO filing. In essence, the chipmaker would be co-signing a customer’s loans, a sign of how hard the AI industry is working to keep its buildout plans afloat as investors grow jittery about the amounts of money involved.

The proposed arrangement would help Nscale secure financing for the electricity and construction costs of its West Virginia campus. Under what’s known as a residual value guarantee, Nvidia would step in if Nscale defaulted on construction payments, temporarily take over the facility, and help find a buyer and new tenant, according to The Information.

Nvidia’s earlier support for cloud providers has mostly covered leases and chip purchases, not construction.

The stakes are high for Nscale, which is seeking to go public at a valuation of up to $35 billion despite growing concerns about its ability to deliver on ambitious data center projects.

Anthropic’s contract with Nscale covers the first 460-megawatt building at Nscale’s West Virginia campus, which is expected to use Nvidia’s next-generation Vera Rubin chips. The companies are also discussing a second deal worth roughly $45 billion for another building.

Nscale has struggled to get its projects off the ground. The company had about 25,000 advanced AI chips running in August, a fraction of the nearly 500,000 it plans to deploy. Its West Virginia campus has run into trouble of its own. Runoff from the construction site has flooded nearby homes more than once. In September, after months of negotiations, the project won state certification.

This month, Microsoft pulled out of a planned Nscale site in England after grid power for it was pushed into the 2030s.

Nvidia has been a major financial backer of the sector, extending roughly $170 billion in investments and credit support to neoclouds and large customers, according to The Information. That includes $1 billion in convertible financing committed to Nscale in September.

Neoclouds, which rent out AI computing power without a cash-rich core business behind them, have come under pressure from investors. Shares of CoreWeave, Nebius, and IREN had each fallen more than 30% in October as of last week, CNBC reported.

The proposed guarantee would deepen Nvidia’s financial ties to Nscale while helping secure a major customer for its chips. Anthropic, one of the largest buyers of AI computing power, has spread its infrastructure spending across multiple chipmakers. One investor told The Information that Anthropic’s involvement likely explains Nvidia’s interest in keeping Nscale’s project on track.

The discussions also come as Anthropic faces questions about how it will finance its own rapid expansion. The company filed to go public this week, but Blackstone has warned investors that Anthropic may receive a below-investment-grade credit rating after its IPO. A junk rating would raise Anthropic’s borrowing costs and could push it to keep relying on stronger partners to back its debt.
