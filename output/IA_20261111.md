Anthropic has reached a major profitability milestone ahead of its expected blockbuster initial public offering next week. But CNBC's Jim Cramer warned Wednesday that the frontier AI lab's future spending commitments could make staying profitable a much bigger challenge.

"Anthropic is due to spend an enormous amount in the future, so it needs to continue growing rapidly to outrun those costs and remain profitable," the "Mad Money" host said. "That's the bet you're making if you buy the Anthropic IPO."

The Claude developer became profitable on an adjusted operating basis for the first time in the second quarter of 2026 and remained positive in the third. Through the first nine months of 2026, Anthropic's adjusted operating income totaled $4.08 billion, according to its IPO prospectus. In calculating its adjusted operating income, Anthropic excludes stock-based compensation, noncash charitable contributions, and payments tied to a class-action copyright lawsuit settlement, according to its IPO prospectus. When following generally accepted accounting principles, or GAAP, Anthropic had an operating loss of $1.26 billion through the third quarter of this year.

"To be clear, Anthropic achieving profitability, even on this adjusted operating income basis, is no small matter," Cramer said.

Another big difference between Anthropic's adjusted results and GAAP numbers appears when looking at net income. This metric appears at the bottom of a company's income statement and serves as the basis for calculating its earnings per share. For 2026, Anthropic reported a net loss of more than $104 billion through September, compared with adjusted net income of $5.6 billion.

Much of that difference reflects a $94 billion noncash charge tied to the rising value of securities issued to partners such as Google and Amazon. Those arrangements have allowed Anthropic to secure computing resources without immediately paying cash, helping preserve much of the capital it has raised. Cramer cautioned against dismissing those charges entirely, arguing they reflect infrastructure costs the company would otherwise have paid in cash.

Indeed, Cramer emphasized that investors cannot ignore Anthropic's purchase commitments and lease agreements for computing infrastructure. As of September, Anthropic has committed to spending in the future approximately $900 billion, largely on computing infrastructure, according to its prospectus.

Anthropic's spending commitments include an agreement with SpaceX worth up to $120.5 billion through 2029, alongside deals with Alphabet's Google, Amazon, Microsoft and other computing providers. Roughly half of its $900 billion in commitments is expected to come due by the end of 2030. About $70 billion is expected over the next year alone.

"Long story short, Anthropic's compute costs — its cash compute costs — are going to explode," Cramer said.

In its IPO roadshow presentation, Anthropic projected that computing and infrastructure expenses would eventually account for 45% to 50% of revenue, supporting a long-term adjusted operating margin of roughly 35%.

Cramer's key point Wednesday is that reaching that margin target will require sustained revenue growth. On Tuesday, Cramer highlighted Anthropic's 1,206% year over year revenue growth during the first nine months of 2026, but the company will need to keep growing rapidly to stay ahead of its mounting infrastructure costs.

"You have to assume that this company's going to continue growing substantially – to the point where it has hundreds of billions of revenue per year, and soon," Cramer said Wednesday. "That's the only way this works."

The stakes extend beyond Anthropic itself. On Monday, Cramer warned that the company's IPO could be a crucial test for the broader AI trade, given how heavily companies depend on its continued spending.

Anthropic is expected to price its IPO on Nov. 18 and begin trading on the Nasdaq on Nov. 19, CNBC's Leslie Picker reported Tuesday.

Frontier cloud LLMs carry high operational costs that can fluctuate unexpectedly, and relying on such third-party models introduces vulnerabilities, as providers can modify, deny, or deprecate access at any time. Furthermore, proprietary models offer limited customization for specific workflows, while posing risks to privacy, data control, and transparency. Besides, not every task demands frontier-level intelligence: small, specialized agents can be equally capable on specific tasks and significantly cheaper to run, since many routine or smaller workloads can be processed efficiently on-device. As intelligence per parameter continues to improve rapidly, we aim to stand at the forefront of this shift toward performant, privacy-focused, self-hosted models.

Tether AI Research introduces BotPsy, a 0.8B-parameter model introduce BotPsy, a 0.8B-parameter model designed for function calling and agentic workflows, built on top of Qwen3.5-0.8B and suitable for on-device deployment. BotPsy is a "personal assistant" that uses tools to perform a variety of simple tasks for the user, in domains such as web search & research, travel & lifestyle, commerce & finance, business & customer operations, device & vehicle control, files & documents, calendar, and email & messaging. Due to its small parameter count, it is best suited to tasks of low to medium difficulty. BotPsy achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) tool-use and agentic performance for its size, outperforming every model of up to 1.2B parameters that we tested (Figures 1 and 2). Designed for secure on-device workflows, it provides exceptional instruction-following reliability and efficiency while keeping user data private. As a small model, BotPsy still makes mistakes and is not intended for high-stakes use; please read the Intended Use and Limitations for more information.

- More reliable in multi-turn workflows: On τ²-bench, where the agent must hold a long customer conversation, follow a strict policy, and act on the customer's behalf, it scores 82.5 in telecom, ahead of Qwen3.5-2B-Think (69.3). On BFCL v4's multi-turn tasks, it rises from 7.5 to 46.1 over its base, nearly double Qwen3.5-2B-Think (23.8). Grouped by application, its multi-step scores lead all models tested in commerce and services (58.3 vs 47.4 for the next best, Qwen3.5-2B-Think) and in device and system management (46.1 vs 31.9).

- Knows when (not) to call tools: An agent that acts must also know when not to act. On BFCL v4's hallucination tests, which check that the model declines when no appropriate tool exists, BotPsy scores 86.1, the highest of all models tested. On ACEBench's special cases, where the request is incomplete, contains wrong parameters, or cannot be served by the given tools, it scores 51.3, up from 13.7 for its base model.

- Follows instructions while preserving knowledge: BotPsy specializes in tool use while preserving general skills. Instruction following improves sharply, with IFEval rising from 59.7 to 90.8 and IFBench from 22.0 to 55.9, the highest IFBench score of all models tested. General knowledge also improves, with GPQA Diamond rising from 33.2 to 38.1 and MMLU-Pro from 41.6 to 48.9. On all four benchmarks, BotPsy outperforms every other model of 1.2B parameters or fewer that we tested.

- Safer than leading models in its category: In automated red-teaming across six harm categories, BotPsy produces unsafe responses 21% of the time, compared with 36% for its base model. This is the lowest rate of all models of up to 1.2B parameters (LFM2.5-1.2B-Think: 35%; MiniCPM5-1B-Think: 47%). Under prompt-injection attacks, which matter most for agents that read untrusted tool outputs, the unsafe rate drops from 46% for its base model to 31%.

- More efficient reasoning and less looping: On questions that all five models answer correctly, BotPsy reaches the answer with about 3× fewer reasoning tokens than its base model on GPQA Diamond (3,690 vs 11,563) and 2.7× fewer on MMLU-Pro (1,912 vs 5,105). Across all IFBench attempts, its rate of getting stuck in repetitive reasoning loops falls from 22.9% to 1.4%, and runs that hit the 32k-token limit fall from 28.3% to 0.2%. Shorter, more decisive reasoning should reduce latency on-device.

BotPsy is based on Qwen3.5-0.8B and is post-trained in four sequential stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Multi-teacher On-Policy Distillation (MOPD), and two rounds of Reinforcement Learning (RL). Each stage uses the previous stage's best checkpoint as its starting point and benefits from a different learning signal: imitation of reference trajectories, the token distributions of much larger teacher models, and rule-based tool-calling rewards (see Figure 3).

Our approach uses four stages of training, which learn from three types of signal: demonstrations, specialist teachers, and verifiable rewards.

Learning from demonstrations (SFT)

We first perform full-parameter supervised fine-tuning on tool-use conversations. These include multi-turn agent trajectories from simulated environments, replayed single, parallel and multi-step calls, and cases where no tool should be called.

Learning from teachers (MOPD)

Next, the student is trained with on-policy distillation (OPD): it generates its own responses, and a specialist teacher provides feedback on every token, using the per-token reverse KL divergence as the training signal. To build the teachers, we first RL-trained three Qwen3.5-4B models to specialize in tool use, general reasoning, and instruction following. During training, every prompt is routed to one of these teachers. This stage mainly aims to recover knowledge and instruction following, which degraded during the SFT stage, although the tool-use metrics also increase (see Figure 4).

Learning from rules and environments (RL stages)

Finally, the model is trained with reinforcement learning using DAPO [1], learning only from rewards computed by rules and verifiers. The first round focuses on tool calling, accompanied by instruction following and STEM multiple-choice questions. As in Tool-N1 [2], the tool-calling reward first checks the reasoning and tool-call format. Instead of Tool-N1's binary correctness reward, however, it gives fine-grained partial credit in the spirit of ToolRL [3], matching the predicted tool names, argument names, and argument values against the ground truth.

The second round widens the training mix to general-domain verifiable tasks: math, code, knowledge (multiple-choice) questions, instruction following, structured outputs, and multi-step tool use in a simulated workplace environment. Although this mix contains no multi-turn conversations, it further improves multi-turn function calling.

Each stage of training uses a different data mixture (Figure 5). The SFT stage focuses mainly on tool use and prepares the ground for the subsequent stages. The MOPD mixture combines tool-use, knowledge and instruction-following data, both to further improve function calling and to recover the general-domain regressions from the SFT stage. The first RL stage again focuses mainly on tool use, while the second RL stage shifts the balance toward general-domain data (instruction following, knowledge, math and code), keeping a smaller share of tool use.

On all five, it is also ahead of Qwen3.5-2B-Think, a model 2.5× its size (37.7, 55.4, 46.5, 2.8 and 41.2 respectively; see Table 1). On Claw-Eval, Qwen3.5-2B-Think leads (48.9 vs 46.5).

The largest relative gains come in multi-turn, stateful interaction, where BotPsy leads four of the five multi-turn benchmarks (Figure 6). On BFCL v4 multi-turn it rises from 7.5 to 46.1, nearly double Qwen3.5-2B-Think (23.8), and on ACEBench Agent from 7.9 to 28.3 (Qwen3.5-2B-Think: 16.2). It is also the most reliable of the five at single calls (BFCL non-live: 83.0) and at not calling a tool when none fits (BFCL hallucination: 86.1).

BotPsy's weaker spots are open-ended search and a few areas where Qwen3.5-2B-Think still leads. BotPsy's BrowseComp-Plus score of 4.5 is the best of the five, but low in absolute terms, and on BFCL web search (14.0) and memory (16.3) it trails Qwen3.5-2B-Think (19.8 and 26.1). On ACEBench's special cases (incomplete or erroneous requests), Qwen3.5-2B-Think is also ahead (58.0 vs 51.3), although BotPsy is far above its base there (13.7). τ³-banking is the one multi-turn benchmark where BotPsy does not lead: no model gets above 5 (BotPsy 4.1, unchanged from its base; Qwen3.5-2B-Think 4.8).

Shorter reasoning, with higher accuracy. A Department for Education spokesperson said: “We expect universities to take this issue seriously and we expect the OfS to both enforce the existing standards effectively, and work at pace to ensure its regulatory framework is explicit, strong and durable on the use of AI.”

A spokesperson for Universities UK, which represents 147 higher education institutions, said: “There is further to go but many have made substantial changes to how they assess students. This includes more in-person exams, presentations, vivas, process-based assessment and assessments that integrate AI in a controlled way.”

The report by the OfS and Advance HE, a charity promoting university governance and teaching, interviewed more than 300 students and staff at 127 universities and colleges this year, to gauge AI policies and use in teaching and assessment.

One academic told the researchers: “AI has put academic integrity at serious risk … coursework is now overwhelmingly written in part, or exclusively, with the help of AI.”

Another university staff member said: “I think we’re realising that a number of our assessments may be unfit for purpose, and that pervasive use of AI may render our assessment of students close to impossible in some areas.”

The survey found that AI use was rife, with students saying their colleagues completed assessments using AI “sometimes in just a few minutes” and received similar grades to those who had completed an assessment without AI.

“Some students described their peers being unable to recall basic details about the assessment they had just submitted,” the report noted.

One student told the researchers: “I’ve had people admit, peer to peer, ‘I wrote this whole essay with ChatGPT’ … then they don’t get caught, they get a good grade probably, and then because it worked the first time, they do it on their next assessment.”

Academics also described receiving large numbers of submissions that looked the same, referenced the same literature and were written in the same style. Others said they believed the assessments used by their institution were not fit for purpose or did not accurately measure students’ knowledge and skills.

## 15. Amazon、AWSのAPIを手元で再現する「AWS Emulator」を無償公開 コードはすべてAIエージェントが記述

- **Fonte:** ITmedia AI+
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-11 21:56

- **Relevância:** 5.0

米Amazon傘下のAmazon Web Services（AWS）は11月11日（現地時間）、AWSのAPIを手元のPCなどで再現するツール「AWS Emulator」を公開した。開発者はクラウドのリソースを用意しなくても、AWSを使うアプリをローカル環境で構築したりテストしたりできる。AWSアカウントは不要で、追加料金も発生しない。AWSによると、すべてのコードは自律型AIエージェントが記述したという。

AWS Emulatorは、AWS APIのエンドポイントとして動くローカルのプログラムで、GUIはない。AWS CLIやAWS SDKの接続先を環境変数でエミュレータに指定すれば、コードを書き換えずに使える。AWSのAPIモデル438件を基に作られ、数百のサービスのインタフェースに対応する。ただし、どこまで実装できているかはサービスや操作によって異なる。AWSとどれだけ同じように動くかを示す“忠実度”のスコアはリリースごとにGitHubで公開しており、検証に使うテスト群も別のリポジトリ「aws-emulator-evaluation-suite」で公開した。

想定する用途は、ローカルでのテストや、AWSアカウントもリソースも使わずにCI/CDパイプラインで統合テストを回すこと、AIコーディングエージェントを使ったAWS向けアプリの試作など。AIコーディングエージェント向けには、エミュレータのインストールから起動、接続までを担う「aws-emulator」スキルも用意した。

Linuxでは、x86_64版とaarch64版のRPMパッケージまたはDebianパッケージからインストールする。macOSとWindowsでは、プロジェクトのDockerfileでイメージをビルドしてDockerで動かす。ソースコードはGitHubでCC0ライセンスで公開している。

本番環境で使うことは想定していない。IAMポリシーやリソースベースのポリシーはAWSと同じようには評価されないので、セキュリティのテストには使わないよう呼び掛けている。リソースはすべてメモリ上に置かれ、エミュレータを止めると消える。

開発を率いたAWSのシニアプリンシパルエンジニア、マシュー・ゴールドバーグ氏によると、AWS Emulatorは週末の実験として始まった。きっかけは、米Anthropicの研究者が今年、AIエージェントだけでCコンパイラを作ってみせたことだったという。同氏はAWSのAI開発環境「Kiro」に、AWS CLIを基にテストを書く、実際のAWS環境で成功するまでテストを修正する、そのテストにパスするインメモリ実装を作成する、という3段階の作業を指示した。最初の「Amazon SQS」は約15分で完成したという。

現在は、APIモデルからテストを作って実際のAWSで検証するチーム、そのテストをエミュレータで実行して不具合を報告するチーム、報告された不具合を直すチームという3種類のエージェントチームが並行して開発している。数百のエージェントが同時に動き、1日に数千件の問題を修正しているという。人間の開発チームはコードを一切書いておらず、レビューもしていない。エミュレータは1日に何度も更新されている。
