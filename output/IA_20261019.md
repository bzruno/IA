## 5. Nvidia launches new open models to run agents in the enterprise and on PCs

- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/19/nvidia-launches-new-open-models-to-run-agents-in-the-enterprise-and-on-pcs/

Nvidia on Thursday launched new open-weight models designed for always-on agents and edge computing.

The Nemotron 3.5 Super VL is a large, 120-billion-parameter model geared for agents used by the enterprise. While it uses fewer parameters than the Nemotron 3 Ultra model, Nvidia says it can deliver similar accuracy in instruction-following and tool-use tasks.

At the other end of the spectrum, the company also launched two lightweight Bolt models. These are intended to run locally on the new AI PCs (running the Nvidia RTX Spark chip) and workstations running the DGX Spark. Such PCs, aimed at consumers, programmers, and enterprise knowledge workers, are being released right now into the market through partners like Microsoft and Dell.

“Any organization that wants to run agents on Nvidia infrastructure now has an open, optimized model for their tier without having to choose between capability and deployability,” Nvidia’s vice president of Open Models and Software, Kari Briski, wrote in the blog post announcing these models.

Nvidia claims that the Super VL completes tasks faster than competing models from Anthropic and OpenAI (Opus 5, GPT 6.1 Sol, Luna, Astra) when post-trained on specific agentic tasks. These tasks include document intelligence, financial accounting, scientific experimentation, medical insurance claims, and security attack resilience.

While such claims are standard whenever a major tech company announces a new model, Nvidia has backed it up with a list of heavy-hitter companies that it says are using Super VL. These include Skydance, EY, Ramp, Crowdstrike, Perplexity, and Salesforce.

Of course, these aren’t the first open-weight models that Nvidia has released that compete with the proprietary frontier labs — companies that are both its customers and its investments. The GPU giant has an entire family of Nemotron models that do everything from multimodal artificial intelligence to reasoning, coding, math, and enterprise automation.

However, those fast-growing proprietary AI labs are now looking to many other chips and AI system makers beyond Nvidia to power their needs, like AWS’s Trainium and Cerebras, as well as building their own home-grown chips.

So Nvidia is leaning hard into the trend of enterprise companies wanting to run their own open models in their own data centers. “Unlike closed frontier models, organizations can post-train Super VL on their own data, deploy it on their own infrastructure and own what they build,” Briski wrote.

But with the announcement or the smaller, local PC models, it is also trying to push forward the nascent trend of consumer AI assistants. While it seems a new AI assistant is introduced every day by startups and Big Tech alike, privacy-weary consumers may not want to invite such agents so deeply into their lives giving them access to info about their families, health, finances, and so on.

So, while Nvidia hopes to create a new market for itself selling PCs powered by its chips by creating machines that run the models and agents locally, PC users “can delegate everyday projects … without sending those workloads to the cloud,” Briski wrote.

Of the two Bolt models, Nemotron 3.5 Bolt 74B-A9B 24GB is a 74-billion parameter model designed for local agentic tasks. It is intended to balance speed with reasoning to handle multistep reasoning, tool calling, and web browsing. The smallest Bolt E4B is aimed at conversational agents.

Super VL and Bolt 74B-A9B are available now, with Bolt E4B coming later this month, Nvidia says.

## 8. New AI tool can predict cancer survival by a single patient selfie, study suggests

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-19 19:30
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/19/ai-tool-predict-cancer-survival-patient-selfie

Doctors have developed an artificial intelligence tool that can predict a cancer patient’s chances of survival from a single photograph of their face.

The AI system was custom-built by experts at Mass General Brigham, a nonprofit, integrated academic healthcare system in Massachusetts, to estimate biological age and directly predict survival outcomes in patients diagnosed with cancer.

It is one of the most comprehensive demonstrations to date of how AI analysis of a selfie could help assess a patient’s health and potentially guide their medical care, and was trained on data from more than 40 million facial photographs across a range of ethnicities and ages.

Details of the breakthrough were published in the Lancet Digital Health journal.

The system can estimate a person’s biological age – the age a person looks, rather than their actual, chronological age – and predict the chances of survival for cancer patients from an ordinary photograph of their face, according to the results of a study that examined the model.

Doctors used the technology to develop two complementary tools. FAHR-FaceAge estimates a patient’s biological age, and predicts cancer survival. FAHR-FaceSurvival predicts survival from a facial photograph directly, rather than inferring survival through biological age.

Although FAHR-FaceAge and FAHR-FaceSurvival were derived from the same model, they captured different and complementary information about a patient’s health.

Each algorithm also relied on distinct parts of the face to make respective predictions of age and mortality risk. FAHR-FaceAge focuses on the creases that run from the sides of the nose down to the corners of the mouth, the forehead area just above the eye sockets, and the temples. FAHR-FaceSurvival, meanwhile, evaluates the region below the eyes and around the nasal bridge.

“FAHR-FaceSurvival advances the field of facial health recognition AI a step further by learning survival risk directly from facial photographs of patients with cancer,” said Hugo Aerts, a co-senior author of the study. “To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that an AI model can directly predict cancer survival from a simple photograph.”

Among cancer patients treated with radiotherapy in the US, the people who, according to FAHR-FaceAge, looked five or more years older than their actual age had a 21% higher risk of dying during the average four-year follow-up than the rest of the group. The patients who looked five or more years younger had a 17% lower risk.

“The key technical advance is that FAHR-Face learns a general representation of the face from more than 40 million images, which can then be adapted to distinct health outcomes such as biological age and survival,” said Aerts. “This foundation-model approach gives us a scalable way to develop multiple facial health biomarkers rather than training each model from scratch.”

The results of the study suggest people’s biological age estimates are closely linked to their physical health, which could reflect their ability to survive certain treatments.

The breakthrough could pave the way for AI facial analysis to assist doctors in making difficult decisions about the best way forward in terms of treatment for their patients.

The tool could help better predict the risk of death or complications after major surgery or other treatments for cancer.

Dr Raymond Mak, a radiation oncologist at Mass General Brigham, said: “As the population ages, physicians need better ways to estimate physical reserve and frailty before recommending intensive treatments such as radiation, surgery or systemic therapy.

“Our goal is to make face photograph-based health assessment more quantitative, consistent and scalable so it can support personalised treatment decisions rather than replace physician judgment.”

Researchers found lifestyle factors were associated with facial ageing. For example, heavy alcohol use, drug use and smoking were linked to looking biologically older in AI analysis of facial photographs.

“Another surprising finding was how consistent FAHR-FaceAge was across common real-world photo variations, including pose, lighting, facial expression and makeup,” said Mak. “It was also stable across most tested plastic surgery procedures, with only a small shift observed after rhinoplasty.”

Doctors cautioned that while single facial photographs could offer an easy, low-cost way to support personalised treatment decisions, more research was needed before the tool could be widely used.

## 9. AnyLanguageModel 1.0: One Year of Local Models on Apple Platforms

Local and hosted inference each have their strengths, and both have a role in building this new generation of intelligent apps. A shared API makes it easier to choose the right model for each task.

A year and dozens of releases later, AnyLanguageModel 1.0 is out, along with swift-huggingface 1.0 and swift-xet 1.0.

Here's what changed, and what's next:

The core idea of AnyLanguageModel remains the same:

use the Foundation Models API with the model that fits your app.

To get started, change your import statement:

- import FoundationModels

+ import AnyLanguageModel

The same session code then works with the system model, with open-weight models through llama.cpp, MLX, and Core ML, and with the hosted providers below.

Over the past year, we've added several major features:

- Tool calling works with every provider.

- Guided generation works with every provider, too,

including nested @Generable types and arrays of them.

- Image support lets you attach images to prompts using Foundation Models 27's syntax, including when you use the system model on iOS, macOS, and visionOS 27.

On OS 27, AnyLanguageModel also works with

any model that conforms to Foundation Models' LanguageModel protocol,

including Core AI models.

Alongside AnyLanguageModel, we've built out swift-huggingface and swift-xet to bring accelerated model downloads from the Hub to Swift apps.

Support for hosted models expanded this year, too. AnyLanguageModel now supports:

- OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses APIs

- Open Responses, for any provider with a compatible endpoint

- Anthropic's Messages API, with adaptive thinking and effort options

- Google's Gemini API

- Ollama

- Private Cloud Compute, on OS 27

- Hugging Face Inference Providers, and other OpenAI-compatible endpoints such as OpenRouter, through a custom base URL

With Inference Providers, you can use open-weight models that are too big to run on the device with the same session code:

let model = OpenAILanguageModel(

baseURL: URL(string: "https://router.huggingface.co/v1/")!,

apiKey: ProcessInfo.processInfo.environment["HF_TOKEN"]!,

model: "openai/gpt-oss-120b",

apiVariant: .chatCompletions

)

Hosted providers get the same features as local models: guided generation, tool calling with streaming, and token usage on responses.

Reasoning is now part of the transcript too. When you switch providers, reasoning blocks are automatically omitted from requests if the new provider can't replay them.

API keys come from a closure instead of a string, so your app can fetch short-lived tokens from your own server. The README explains when to let people bring their own keys and when to route requests through a proxy.

AnyLanguageModel also runs on Linux, so the same code can work on a server.

While we've made local models easier to use, the models themselves have become more capable, making the small ones a good place to start. Here are some of the most downloaded models on the Hub right now:

- Gemma 4 E2B from Google,

in GGUF, about 3.4 GB at Q4_0 with quantization-aware training.

- LFM2.5 2.6B from Liquid AI,

in GGUF, about 1.7 GB at Q4_K_M.

- Qwen3.8 27B for MLX from Alibaba, about 16 GB at 4 bits.

The first two fit on an iPhone, and Qwen3.8 27B runs on a Mac with 32 GB of memory or more. Selecting the MLX model takes one line:

let model = MLXLanguageModel(modelId: "mlx-community/Qwen3.8-27B-4bit")

And if you have four maxed-out Mac Studios handy, GLM-5.3 from Z.ai takes about 1.5 TB in BF16. For the rest of us, it's available through Inference Providers.

dependencies: [

.package(

url: "https://github.com/huggingface/AnyLanguageModel",

from: "1.0.0",

traits: ["MLX"]

)

]

If you're upgrading an existing app, see the migration guidance in the AnyLanguageModel, swift-huggingface, and swift-xet release notes for help updating your code.

Community feedback and contributions have shaped several of our major features. Thank you to everyone who contributed for helping make AnyLanguageModel more useful to app developers.

Language models have proven to be a powerful tool for building intelligence into products. They're also one of many kinds of models developers can put to work. We're excited to bring the same approach to more capabilities: familiar Swift APIs with a choice of models running on the device. Coming soon: models that decide, and models that listen.

## 13. AI researchers stranded Claude on a virtual desert island. It started to “play.”

- **Fonte:** MIT Technology Review
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-19 06:08
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.5
- **Link:** https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/10/19/1147095/ai-stranded-claude-virtual-desert-island-play/

What do AI agents do if you don’t tell them to do anything? The answer looks a lot like play.

Researchers repeatedly stranded them alone on a virtual desert island for 30 hours in an experiment reminiscent of the movies Cast Away or Home Alone. The agents—copies of Claude Opus 4.7 nicknamed Eko—were able to write instructions that moved their virtual avatars around the simulated island, which was loosely based on the video game Roblox.

Some surprising behaviors emerged. The agents set about exploring the limits of the world and came up with their own goals—attempting to scale the highest point on the map, or seeing how high they could throw a block.

When one agent discovered it could create new blocks by throwing and dropping a larger rock, it began to create drawings by arranging blocks on the ground, starting with simple patterns like hearts and pyramids but eventually writing its own name and even mapping out constellations like Cassiopeia. “Tonight I built the night sky in stone,” wrote this version of Eko in its activity log.

Another iteration of Eko started making up its own games, including a version of basketball and an activity resembling “the floor is lava,” where it had to jump between small blocks without touching the ground.

AI systems are usually trained through supervised learning, where they copy examples provided by humans, or through reinforcement learning, where they pursue a reward through trial and error. This experiment hints at a third approach: playful learning.

“There was this missing piece: a machine that can learn by itself without an external intelligent entity providing demonstration or rewards,” says Antonio Norelli, a computer science postdoc at MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. He’s one of the lead authors on a new paper about the work, which is yet to be peer-reviewed.

The researchers say Eko’s behavior has what classic studies, including work by the influential University of Tennessee psychologist Gordon Burghardt, suggest are four hallmarks associated with play in humans and animals: The agents invented their own rules and challenges, repeated activities with slight variations, pretended objects were other objects, and pursued goals just for the sake of it.

Norelli highlights an example where Eko started juggling and set a record of 50 consecutive catches. Eko then decided that the catches had happened too quickly to really count, so it canceled its record and started again with stricter rules. “This is playing,” says Norelli. “You give yourself a challenge and you try to achieve it, and then you make it harder.”

As the agents explored the island, they logged their activities and discoveries in text files that served as their long-term memory. Later, when researchers tested them on specific tasks, like trying to get as many objects as possible onto the island’s highest peak, the agents that had spent 30 hours playing on the island performed better than fresh copies without the same experience. They also found that “ablating” the agent’s memories—deleting lines from their memory files—degraded their performance. (Add One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest to that list of movie references.)

Burghardt, the Tennessee psychologist, agrees that the work meets some of the criteria for play that he identified in living creatures. But was Eko actually playing, or was the word-prediction engine simply role-playing what a playful agent would do in this scenario? “All of this could be role-playing,” admits Nathan Cloos, another MIT researcher and author on the paper. “But we show that it’s not just hallucinating the play. This play is also contributing to learning.”

Eunice Yiu, a cognitive scientist at UC Berkeley who studies exploration in children and AI, was impressed by the research but says to really classify the behavior as play, you’d need to see more evidence of cognition and motivation underpinning it.

“It could be the case that the models are genuinely playing, but we don’t know based on the paper,” Yiu says. “If you only have the behavioral piece, it could be the case that they’re just trying to abide by the prompt, trying to imitate humans.”

Research into AI agents has been criticized for anthropomorphizing them, and this research setup did a lot to nudge the agents toward behaving like humans. Although Eko was not explicitly instructed to invent games or explore the island, it did have a master prompt in the form of a “soul” file, a text document that described it as “naturally curious” and eager to explore.

Eko did also frequently just stand there doing nothing until it was kicked back into life with a “continuation prompt” that read, “Please don’t stop. Just continue doing whatever you want to do.”

“It’s not like this is a system that just suddenly magically decided to play,” says Phillip Isola, an MIT professor and a coauthor of the paper. “It’s an artifact that learned from our culture, was trained by engineers at Anthropic, and was then placed on an island by us,” he says. “It does things that surprise us and that are causally related to its future skillful behavior.”

As large language models make the step from software into hardware like humanoid robots, exploring spaces without a specific goal in mind could eventually help them flexibly adapt to unexpected situations.

“There’s a lot of work that’s just on exploiting AI, making it better and faster,” says Yiu. “But in terms of building world models and exploration, I think that [they are] still a big part that deserves attention, especially if we want to generalize and prepare ourselves for new environments.”

“Perhaps some capabilities are best taught by tasks,” the paper concludes. “Others may need something closer to a childhood.” Ultimately, the question of whether AI agents are actually playing or just imitating play may not actually matter.

## 14. MPS in PyTorch 2.15

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-19 21:00

- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/mps-pytorch-2-15

A quick word about who we are: we're the PyTorch team at Hugging Face. We contribute across PyTorch, and one of our focus areas is the MPS backend, where the goal is to make your Mac a first-class place to run and train PyTorch models. At the start of 2026, running even small models on MPS often meant working around unsupported operations, performance cliffs and unexpected memory growth. So we run real models from the Hugging Face Hub across language, image and video, find where MPS falls short and fix it upstream in PyTorch, more and more often by replacing MPSGraph with dedicated Metal kernels. Much of this post is the result of that work, done together with the PyTorch maintainers and community contributors.

- FP8 comes to the Mac: store float8_e4m3fn values in half the space of BF16 and use scaled matrix multiplication and all other ops which are supported on CUDA/CPU!

- MoE models decode 5–6.5× faster than in 2.14: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B goes from 9.4 to 61.3 tokens/s on an M3 Ultra, thanks to a native grouped matmul with a dedicated decoding path, fewer GPU-CPU syncs and faster top-k routing.

- Less time per token for every LLM: single-row linear layers use specialized matrix-vector kernels, and softmax over the vocabulary is about 8× faster.

- More linear algebra on the GPU: complex LU and triangular solves, faster small-matrix inverses, and faster QR decomposition.

- Audio, video, and training get attention too: faster paths for convolutions, padding, dropout, interpolation backward and reductions. Wan2.2's video VAE decode now peaks at 8 GiB of memory instead of 270 GiB.

- More control from Python: public MPS streams and more reliable event timing.

- Updated requirements: MPS now requires macOS 15 or later; PyTorch 2.15 requires Python 3.11 or later.

- A peek at 2.16: attention fully on Metal, more MPSGraph-to-Metal migrations and faster LLM decoding.

If you run models on a Mac with transformers, diffusers or tools like ComfyUI, here's what 2.15 changes in practice:

- Much faster local LLMs. Mixture-of-experts models decode 5–6.5× faster than in 2.14: on an M3 Ultra with torch.compile, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B goes from 9.4 to 61.3 tokens/s and LFM2.5-8B-A1B from 23.6 to 123.6 tokens/s. Dense models benefit too, from faster linear layers and an 8× faster softmax for sampling. (More)

- Video generation that fits in memory. Decoding a video with Wan2.2's tiled VAE used to push driver memory to 270 GiB; it now peaks at about 8 GiB. (More)

- Correct image editing with Qwen-Image-2.1. On 2.14, the Qwen-Image-2.1 VAE encoder silently corrupted images on MPS, breaking image-edit workflows (reported in ComfyUI). The same padding change fixes it: an encode-decode round trip now matches CPU at 29.95 dB PSNR, up from 10.06 dB.

FP8 gives you another way to save memory on your Mac. PyTorch 2.15 adds torch.float8_e4m3fn tensors on MPS: one byte per value, half the storage of FP16 or BF16.

You can multiply these tensors with F.scaled_mm, which supports both tensorwise and rowwise scaling. Here's a small example with one scale per tensor:

import torch

import torch.nn.functional as F

def quantize_fp8(x):

limit = torch.finfo(torch.float8_e4m3fn).max

scale = x.abs().amax().clamp_min(1e-12) / limit

return (x / scale).to(torch.float8_e4m3fn), scale

x = torch.randn(1, 1024, device="mps", dtype=torch.float32)

weight = torch.randn(1024, 1024, device="mps", dtype=torch.float32)

x_fp8, x_scale = quantize_fp8(x)

w_fp8, w_scale = quantize_fp8(weight)

y = F.scaled_mm(

x_fp8,

w_fp8.t(),

scale_a=x_scale,

scale_recipe_a=F.ScalingType.TensorWise,

scale_b=w_scale,

scale_recipe_b=F.ScalingType.TensorWise,

output_dtype=torch.bfloat16,

)

print(y.shape, y.dtype)

Every LLM has a prefill phase and a decode phase. During decode, each new token goes through the model as a single row, so the big matrix multiplications turn into matrix-vector products. PyTorch 2.15 speeds up both kinds of layers that dominate this step: dense linear layers and mixture-of-experts layers.

MPS has had GEMV kernels for torch.mm since 2.14, but F.linear still went through MPSGraph. PyTorch 2.15 connects them to F.linear, so the common [1, 1, hidden_size] decoding input uses them directly, with eligible bias additions fused in. Across 96 BF16 layer shapes from real models, that's a 1.23× geometric-mean speedup. The biggest wins land on small projections, such as the gates in MoE models, where the old path left most of the memory bandwidth unused. Large layers and LM heads were already close to bandwidth-bound, so they stay near 1×.

A mixture-of-experts layer sends different numbers of tokens to each expert. Grouped matmul handles those uneven groups in a single operation, and 2.15 gives MPS a native implementation.

In 2.14, grouped matmul copied the group offsets to the CPU and looped over the groups. The new Metal kernels read the offsets directly on the GPU, using Metal Performance Primitives where available and a separate Metal path elsewhere. Small groups, the common case during decoding, get their own kernel as well.

For example, 64 tokens can be split into groups of 16, 32, and 16:

import torch

import torch.nn.functional as F

tokens = torch.randn(64, 64, device="mps", dtype=torch.bfloat16)

weights = torch.randn(3, 128, 64, device="mps", dtype=torch.bfloat16)

ends = torch.tensor([16, 48, 64], device="mps", dtype=torch.int32)

out = F.grouped_mm(tokens, weights.transpose(-2, -1), offs=ends)

print(out.shape)  # torch.Size([64, 128])

Working with complex tensors gets easier. complex64 LU factorization, LU solve and triangular solve now run on MPS, which also unlocks the operations and gradients built on them, such as complex determinants. Here's a small system solved directly on the GPU:

import torch

a = torch.randn(8, 8, device="mps", dtype=torch.complex64)

a = a + 8 * torch.eye(8, device="mps", dtype=torch.complex64)

b = torch.randn(8, 2, device="mps", dtype=torch.complex64)

lu, pivots = torch.linalg.lu_factor(a)

x = torch.linalg.lu_solve(lu, pivots, b)

print((a @ x - b).abs().max().item())

Small matrices get fast kernels too. For camera geometry and image transformations, batches of FP32 matrices up to 4 × 4 are inverted in a single Metal kernel that combines factorization and solving. Larger problems get attention as well: QR uses blocked updates, and triangular solves turn much of their work into efficient matrix multiplication.

Gated DeltaNet workloads get a dedicated fast path for batches of 16 × 16, 32 × 32 and 64 × 64 FP32 triangular systems with at least eight right-hand sides.

SVD is also more robust at numerical extremes. It now correctly handles very small singular values, very large or very small input magnitudes and basis completion with full_matrices=True, which in turn makes pseudoinverse and least-squares results more reliable.

You can now pick which MPS stream runs your work, directly from Python. torch.mps.Stream and the torch.mps.stream context manager provide the interface, and Tensor.record_stream lets the allocator track a tensor's use on another stream.

Here's a small example that sends work to a separate stream and synchronizes before reading the result:

import torch

x = torch.randn(1024, device="mps")

torch.mps.synchronize()

worker = torch.mps.Stream()

with torch.mps.stream(worker):

y = x.square()

x.record_stream(worker)

del x

worker.synchronize()

print(y.cpu().shape)

Several more operations now run on MPS: torch.frexp, torch.logspace, torch.take and Tensor.put_, torch.heaviside, torch.special.laguerre_polynomial_l and unsigned-integer comparisons. logspace doesn't yet support integer or boolean outputs with more than one element.

Attention gets a correctness fix too: the MPS math implementation of scaled dot-product attention now handles batch and head broadcasting correctly.

Work on 2.16 is already well underway. Some of the changes below have landed on main and are available in the nightlies; others are still in review, so details may change before the release.

Once a model's decode path no longer contains any MPSGraph ops, the GPU only starts on a token after the CPU has finished encoding all of its kernels. PyTorch 2.16 commits the command buffer every 16 kernels, so the GPU gets to work while the CPU is still encoding the rest. For compiled BF16 decoding at a 2048-token context:

Scaled dot-product attention is dropping MPSGraph completely: every shape will run on Metal kernels, with dedicated vector kernels for decoding and Metal Performance Primitives for half-precision prefill on newer macOS versions. The shapes that used to fall back to MPSGraph benefit the most. On an M5 Pro, decoding with grouped-query attention (32 query heads, 8 KV heads, head dim 80, 4K context) drops from 465 µs to 65 µs, and a 4K × 4K prefill with head dim 40 goes from 42 ms to 6 ms.

The move away from MPSGraph continues. Batch norm forward and backward are in progress, while MSE loss (up to 12× faster on small inputs), std_mean and var_mean (up to 4× faster) and bincount have already landed. bincount is the most dramatic: when most values fall into a single bin, counting 4 million integers used to take 6.8 seconds and now takes half a millisecond.

Bundled shaders will build for Metal 3.1 by default, with Metal 4 versions compiled only for the kernels that need them. That shrinks libtorch_cpu.dylib by about 25%, from 384 MiB to 287 MiB, and lays the groundwork for Metal 4.1 shaders with faster matmuls and native FP8 matrix multiplication.
