We're excited to share three updates to IBM's Granite Speech collection: a new speech-language model, granite-speech-5.0-2b, and revised versions of two models the community already uses, granite-speech-5.0-470m-turboctc and granite-speech-4.1-2b-plus. Together they cover the full range of enterprise speech work: transcription and translation with high accuracy, extremely fast transcription at the edge, and recordings where who spoke, and when, matter as much as what was said.

granite-speech-5.0-2b is the successor to granite-speech-4.1-2b at the same parameter count. It transcribes English, French, German, Spanish, Portuguese, and Japanese, and translates to and from English in those languages, plus English to Italian and Mandarin. It was trained on 390,000 hours of public audio, plus synthetic data built for Japanese ASR, keyword biasing, and translation.

What's new compared with 4.1 2B Speech:

- More accurate, more robust English ASR: Higher accuracy on English short-form benchmarks, and better handling of noise, reverberation, and distant-microphone speech.

- Language ID prompting: Name the input language in the prompt to steer multilingual transcription.

- Better long-form and conversational translation: Translation holds up for longer with more natural speech, not just short read sentences.

- Keyword lists up to 2,000 terms: Bias transcription and translation toward product names, people, and acronyms, in every supported language.

- Punctuation and capitalization on request: Ask for formatted output in the prompt; capitalization F1 ranges from 89.7 to 99.5 across our test sets.

The model ships under Apache 2.0 with support in Transformers, vLLM, llama.cpp, and mlx-audio for Apple Silicon.

When we introduced Turbo CTC in August, it was an English-only, 470M-parameter encoder built for speed. This revision keeps that footprint and adds the three things users asked for most:

- Multilingual transcription: English, Japanese, Spanish, German, French, Italian, and Portuguese.

- Native punctuation and capitalization: Transcripts come out ready to read, with no separate punctuation or truecasing step.

- More robust in difficult acoustics: Training that explicitly targets reverberant audio makes Turbo CTC more robust in echoey rooms and far-field recordings.

It's also faster. By compiling a single Conformer block and reusing its graph across the stack, we cut warmup time and pushed Turbo CTC to 19,870 RTFx on the Open ASR Leaderboard, up from roughly 12,600 RTFx at launch: about 1.6x faster, at the same accuracy level.

## 4. Anthropic、Claudeでソフトウェアのアタックサーフェスを洗い出し・縮小する研究を公開 OpenSSHの修正にも貢献

- **Fonte:** ITmedia AI+
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-21 23:48
- **Idioma:** ja
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://www.itmedia.co.jp/news/article/2610/22/2000002498/

米Anthropicは10月20日（現地時間）、AIモデル「Claude」を使ってソフトウェアの攻撃対象領域（アタックサーフェス）を分析し、縮小する研究の初期結果を公開した。同社でAIのリスク評価を担う「Frontier Red Team」のクリス・ロルフ氏が執筆した。

攻撃対象領域とは、外部の攻撃者が到達できるコードの範囲のことだ。同社によると、脆弱性を1件ずつ見つけて修正する方法では、重大な脆弱性を1つ直しても、残りの脆弱性については何も分からず、規模を広げにくい。一方、その環境で外部に見せる必要のない機能を認証の後ろに移したり、初期設定で無効にしたりして、攻撃者が届く範囲を狭める方法もある。この方法なら、まだ見つかっていない脆弱性があっても悪用されにくくなり、深刻度を下げられるという。家の弱点を1つずつ探すのではなく、使っていない窓や勝手口を先に閉めておく考え方だ。

研究では、C、C++、Java、PHP、Pythonなどで書かれたオープンソースプロジェクト25件（計500万行超）をClaudeで分析した。Claudeが各プロジェクトから洗い出した1万7000以上の設定項目のうち、3842項目がセキュリティに関わるものだった。分析の観点は、初期設定のまま不要なコードに外部から届かないか、組み合わせると問題を起こす設定がないか、認証などのチェックより前に複雑な処理が動いていないか、の3点だ。その結果、合計493件の問題を発見した。

認証を前提とする11件のプロジェクトでは、すべてで意図せず外部から届くコードが見つかった。また、エラーも警告も出ずに受け入れられる設定の組み合わせが162件あり、そのうち94件ではセキュリティ機能が意図せず無効化されていた。中には、クライアント証明書による認証を求める設定と別の設定を併用すると証明書のチェックが外れ、誰でも接続できてしまう例もあった。この設定の矛盾は21年間残っていたという。いずれもそれだけですぐに悪用できるものではないが、必要以上に攻撃の入り口を広げているとロルフ氏は説明している。

成果が最も大きかったのは、リモート接続に広く使われる「OpenSSH」だ。バージョン10.4では、耐量子計算機暗号の署名方式「ML-DSA-44」の実験的なコード約4500行に、認証前のクライアントが到達できる状態だった。この方式は初期設定では有効になっておらず、管理者が明示的に除外した場合でも同様だった。約4500行は、初期設定のOpenSSHで認証前に動くコードの約16％に当たる。

AnthropicはこれをOpenSSHの開発者に修正案とともに報告し、10.5で対策が入った。その後Claudeは、10.5の対策にも2つの抜け道を見つけた。開発者と修正を重ねた結果、認証前のクライアントが到達できたコードの約5分の1が削減され、この修正はバージョン10.6で反映された。

ロルフ氏は、OpenSSHが世界で最も入念に監査されているコードの1つであることに触れ、そこでも不要な到達経路が残っていた以上、どこにでも残り得るとしている。

Anthropicは今回の手法を再利用可能な「スキル」としてまとめ、オープンソース向けの脆弱性発見サービス「OSS Scanner」に組み込む作業を進めている。スキルは3つの観点で順に分析し、見つけた問題はプログラムを実際に動かして確認する。初期設定に関わる修正では、プロジェクトのテストを実行して既定の動作が変わらないことを確かめる。初期設定以外の動作に影響する変更は、人間の判断に委ねる設計という。

## 5. NVIDIA Kumo Relational Outperforms Supervised Machine Learning on Relational Data, No Training Required

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-21 15:26
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/nvidia/kumo-relational

NVIDIA Kumo Relational is a pre-trained relational foundation model that makes predictions directly on enterprise databases. Given a predictive query, it gathers up to 10,000 historical examples as context and predicts outcomes such as churn, demand, or fraud in a single forward pass. It needs no task-specific training, no feature engineering, and no flattening of tables. Kumo Relational is the first few-shot foundation model to outperform supervised machine learning on common relational benchmarks. Across 41 tasks on 15 databases, it beats supervised relational deep learning models and expert-engineered gradient-boosted tree pipelines, and on the SAP SALT enterprise benchmark it surpasses AutoGluon ensembles by 8% in mean reciprocal rank (MRR). It scales to databases with 500B+ rows and works for humans and agents alike, through NVIDIA's open-source, GPU-native structured-data-models library or as an NVIDIA NIM.

- Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12596

- Documentation: https://docs.nvidia.com/sdgm/rfm/overview

Foundation models have already rewritten the playbook for language and vision. Not long ago, production NLP meant a dedicated model per task, each fed by its own pipeline of tokenization, lemmatization, and n-grams. LLMs made that stack largely obsolete for three reasons: they were more accurate than the best task-specific models, far simpler to put into production, and flexible enough to open a new era of agentic applications.

The same shift is now underway for structured data. Relational databases power the world's most critical business decisions, yet predictive modeling on them still requires weeks of feature engineering, task-specific training, and tuned pipelines. Structured data foundation models change that for the same three reasons:

Better accuracy today: As drop-in replacements, these models already beat many production pipelines, especially XGBoost. On RelBench V1 classification tasks, Kumo Relational outperforms gradient-boosted trees on expert-engineered features by nearly 4 AUROC points on average. On SAP SALT, it beats XGBoost on engineered features by 7 MRR points out of the box and by 13 MRR points after fine-tuning. These results make a compelling case for replacing production XGBoost pipelines with Kumo Relational.

Simplicity and agility: One pre-trained model replaces separate pipelines for individual tasks. A new prediction becomes a query instead of a project, so ML teams can finally take on use cases that traditional ML made too laborious to build at every level of the organization.

Agents that can predict: LLMs let agents read, write, and reason. Structured data foundation models let them anticipate what happens next, such as which customer will churn, and act on it. Kumo Relational is already built to be called by agents, and its Predictive Query Language (PQL) gives them a simple interface for asking predictive questions.

Today, we are introducing Kumo Relational (paper, documentation), a pre-trained foundation model that brings this paradigm to relational data. Connect a database, describe what to predict, and get predictions in a single forward pass. No training required.

Most foundation models for structured data operate on a single flat table. Real-world data lives in relational databases instead: users connected to orders connected to products, all evolving over time. Flattening these relationships into one row through manual aggregation (e.g., "average order value in the last 30 days") loses fine-grained structural signals and takes significant human effort.

Kumo Relational is a Relational Foundation Model that operates directly on the database, respecting its tables, foreign keys, and timestamps. It is pre-trained once on synthetic relational data that reflects real-world datasets and adapts to each new task through in-context learning, reading up to 10,000 context examples at inference time. Each example pairs a local subgraph around an entity at a point in time with its ground-truth target (e.g., "this user churned" or "this item sold 142 units").

![Overview of the NVIDIA Kumo Relational system: (a) database integration, (b) a prompting interface that turns natural language into a predictive query, and (c) automatic context generation feeding the NVIDIA Kumo Relational model, which scales horizontally across tenants.]

Figure 1. Overview of the Kumo Relational system. Connect a database, specify a predictive query, and Kumo Relational handles the rest, from context generation to model inference.

To make a prediction, Kumo Relational has to do three things: (1) understand the values within each table, (2) relate records across tables, and (3) relate context examples with known targets to the entity being predicted. Here is how it works:

![Kumo Relational architecture: (a) context input with task, users, orders and items tables; (b) column and row attention within tables with injected context targets; (c) graph attention and cross-sample attention across tables.]

Figure 2. Kumo Relational architecture: (a) context input, (b) reasoning within tables, (c) reasoning across tables.

Automatic Context Generation: Tasks are written in the Predictive Query Language (PQL), a declarative interface where you state what to predict rather than how. For temporal tasks, Kumo Relational replays historical database states: it samples entity-centric subgraphs up to an anchor timestamp and computes each target from events after that timestamp, while serving the model only the data from before it. This prevents data leakage.

Reasoning Within Tables: A lightweight network processes each table independently, alternating column attention (relating values within a column) and row attention (relating features within a row). The model learns which columns and rows matter for the current prediction and filters out noise before it propagates.

Reasoning Across Tables: A larger network distributes the row embeddings across the relational structure via graph attention over primary-key/foreign-key relationships, interleaved with cross-sample attention across context examples. This is where relational reasoning happens, such as linking a user's profile to their transaction history. Because this stage works on row embeddings rather than on every cell in every table, the design avoids quadratic cost and scales to large contexts and deeper subgraphs.

Kumo Relational is pre-trained on entirely synthetic relational data simulated to reflect real-world relational databases with diverse predictive queries. Pre-training proceeds in stages, from single tables to increasingly complex relational structures.

The diverse relational databases needed for pre-training are rarely public, so synthetic data fills the gap. Kumo Relational's generator builds on the approach of PluRel (Kothapalli et al., ICML 2026), which extends single-table data generators to full relational databases. Members of the Kumo team helped develop PluRel and co-authored the original paper. PluRel generates each database in three stages: a schema, foreign-key links between rows that cluster as they do in real data, and column values from Structural Causal Models with temporal trends and cross-table dependencies. Generation is lightweight, so pre-training data is effectively unlimited, and the PluRel paper shows that pre-training loss follows a power law as synthetic databases are added, with gains that carry over to real databases.

Through this pre-training, the model learns to filter, correlate, and aggregate information across tables instead of relying on fixed aggregations. When maximum accuracy matters, Kumo Relational can also be fine-tuned on a specific dataset and task.

In this paper, we evaluated Kumo Relational on 41 benchmarks across 15 relational databases from RelBench V1 and V2, SAP SALT, and 4DBInfer, spanning e-commerce, medical, enterprise, social, and academic domains. None of these datasets were seen in pre-training. Kumo Relational outperforms the strongest supervised models in both classification and regression, including relational deep learning models and expert-engineered tabular ensembles. On the RelArena-α leaderboard, which compares relational models and systems on RelBench V1, it also sits on the accuracy-efficiency Pareto front out of the box:

![Scatter plot of the RelArena-α leaderboard: relative accuracy as Elo across tasks (y-axis) versus median inference time per 1,000 samples (x-axis, log scale). Kumo Relational sits on the Pareto front at about 17 seconds and Elo 1,842. The highest-scoring entry, rt-plurel, reaches Elo 1,878 at about 7,300 seconds, roughly 430 times slower. Every other foundation model, feature-engineering system, and supervised model scores lower and runs slower than Kumo Relational.]

Figure 3. RelArena-α leaderboard: Elo across tasks versus median inference time per 1,000 samples (log scale). Kumo Relational is about 430× faster than the highest-scoring entry at similar accuracy.

The paper also reports that on the SAP SALT enterprise benchmark, Kumo Relational surpasses large AutoGluon ensembles by 8% in mean reciprocal rank (MRR) and recent tabular foundation models by about 25%, and fine-tuning extends its lead over AutoGluon to 16%. It also holds up under hard conditions: it performs well with as little as 0.2% of the training data as context, stays stable when up to 75% of foreign-key links are removed, and barely degrades when many noisy columns are added.

Kumo Relational performs best on data with meaningful relationships and temporal dynamics, such as user behavior over time. On tasks with very large training sets, task-specific supervised models may still edge out in-context predictions, which used at most 10,000 context examples in our benchmarks. Fine-tuning of the foundation model is designed for exactly this case. Predictive signals must also be reachable within a limited number of hops, so start with a focused query over a few relevant tables and expand the graph as needed.

Kumo Relational runs in-context learning natively over databases: in memory, through database and warehouse connectors, or through a custom graph engine that handles 500B+ rows via memory-mapped I/O at up to 5 GB/s. It is available in NVIDIA's newly released, GPU-native structured-data-models library, the same open-source library that powers our sister model, Kumo Tabular. The library downloads the weights from the Hugging Face Hub on first use, so you can load the model in a GPU-backed notebook and predict directly from it. The code below passes context examples, prediction entities, and their related tables to the model:

\# pip install structured-data-models

model = sdm.models.KumoRelational(device="cuda")

pred = model(

\# In-context training table (fkeys/timestamps/targets):

x_context=...,

y_context=...,

\# Prediction examples (fkeys/timestamps):

x_query=...,

\# Multi-table information for context/prediction entities:

related_context_tables=...,

related_query_tables=...,

)

See the library's relational examples for complete RelBench and SAP SALT pipelines, including how to sample each entity's related tables.

\# Prediction task in Predictive Query Language (PQL):

\# Multi-table data as pandas DataFrames (keys/links inferred):

)

\# Context examples and related tables for the entities to predict:

## 15. OpenAI Invests in Startup That Uses Brain Implants to Detect Cancer

- **Fonte:** Wired AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-21 07:30
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://www.wired.com/story/openai-startup-brain-implants-detect-cancer/

OpenAI’s venture capital arm as well as Peter Thiel’s Founders Fund are investing in Coherence Neuro, a startup developing a brain implant to detect and treat cancer.

The investments are part of a $45 million seed funding round for Coherence and reflect Big Tech’s burgeoning interest in brain data. Coherence’s CEO and cofounder Ben Woodington says there is “growing synergy” between the big AI labs and companies building devices to decode the brain, known as brain-computer interfaces.

“The idea of capturing data to train better models and use it to optimize treatments is going to be super important,” he says. “The AI companies seem to be aligned with this thesis as well—that this novel data source is going to be valuable.”

Woodington says AI is a “perfect use case” for brain-computer interfaces because developers of these devices generate massive datasets that are too large for humans to analyze themselves. AI models can help read raw neural data and link it to actions, such as moving a computer cursor or producing synthesized speech.

In January, OpenAI announced that it was directly investing in a new brain-computer interface company, Merge Labs, cofounded by its billionaire CEO Sam Altman. Merge Labs came out of stealth with $252 million in initial funding to pursue ultrasound technology that can decode information from the brain. At the time, OpenAI said it was funding Merge Labs because of a belief that brain-computer interfaces “will create a natural, human-centered way for anyone to seamlessly interact with AI.” (The name Merge Labs comes from the idea popular in Silicon Valley that humans will eventually merge with machine intelligence.)

Thiel’s VC firm has also backed Elon Musk’s Neuralink as well as CTRL-labs, the creator of a wristband that translates neural signals from arm and hand movements that was acquired by Facebook in 2019.

Rather than focusing on restoring function for paralyzed individuals, San Francisco–based Coherence is developing AI models to decode subtle electrical signals associated with brain tumors. The company says it already knows how to detect these signals in animals to determine whether cancer is growing, shrinking, or recurring. The team is working to translate these findings for human research and, eventually, clinical trials.

This summer, Coherence briefly tested the recording ability of its implant in the brains of three people who were already undergoing brain tumor removal surgery at the Royal Melbourne Hospital in Australia. The implant was in place for roughly 30 minutes before being removed, providing an initial safety check. Next, Coherence is testing both the recording and stimulation capabilities of the device in an additional seven patients.

The implant is also designed to emit mild electrical stimulation to halt the growth of tumors. The idea is that over time, the device would learn to detect signals associated with cancer and provide real-time treatment. Coherence plans to use the new funding to launch a clinical trial to implant the device long-term in patients with glioblastoma, an aggressive type of brain cancer. The company also aims to develop implants to treat cancers in other parts of the body.

“Coherence's approach to developing a cancer therapy capable of improving from data is incredibly innovative,” Ian Hathaway, partner at the OpenAI Startup Fund, says in a statement. “We are proud to be backing them on this exciting mission.”

Woodington says that OpenAI will not have access to its data. “There's nothing in the contracts or arrangements that would mean that we would share, or they would have access to, any brain data or other health data that we're generating,” he says.

That is seemingly different from OpenAI’s relationship with Merge Labs. In its January announcement, OpenAI said it would “collaborate with Merge Labs on scientific foundation models and other frontier tools to accelerate progress.”
