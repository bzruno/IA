## 1. Trump threatens ban on US firms building AI data centres overseas

- **Fonte:** BBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-20 17:54
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 8.0
- **Link:** https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmde1lw9y54ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss

- Published

Donald Trump has said he will ban US companies from building AI data centres overseas, threatening a "tremendous tax" for tech firms who do so.

"We're going to keep it all in this country," the US president said, while providing little detail on the potential policy he said others in his cabinet were hearing about for the first time.

Data centres were a central issue in the midterm elections earlier in November, with many Democratic candidates promising tougher regulation on their construction.

Tech giants including Meta and Google have entered agreements to build data centres overseas. It remains unclear how such a policy would be enforced if it was brought in.

"We're not going to allow our companies to build data centres outside of our country," Trump told reporters in a press briefing following his return from his trip to Asia.

"Because China will take all of them, and we're not allowing that to happen."

But whether that idea will become policy remains unclear.

"I don't know if that's a breaking story, but I haven't even said that to my people. But I'm telling them now, they're here," he said.

Trump floated the policy after saying the US was leading on data centre construction, and he would not allow China to beat the US when it came to AI - which he calls super intelligence, or SI.

It also follows an announcement earlier this week that would restrict the export of advanced computer chips and who can access the computing power provided by them.

Washington on Monday announced firms would require a licence to buy advanced chips, so US officials can monitor whether they are being used to serve Chinese companies or they are being smuggled into China.

The move will impact South East Asian countries that have invested heavily in data centres, as Chinese companies seek to rent computing power from overseas.

Data centres are crucial to the AI boom. The vast warehouse-style buildings house the advanced computer chips and servers that are needed to run AI programmes such as Claude and ChatGPT.

As of April there were about 3,000 data centres across the US, with another 1,500 in various stages of approval and construction, according to the Pew Research Centre.

Many of them are built in rural areas, and concerns about issues including noise pollution and energy consumption helped drive Democratic candidates promising tougher rules on data centre construction to wins on 3 November.

"You ought to take advantage of the data centres. Make them pay you, make them pay your community, but take advantage don't shut them off," Trump said.

"We have a lot of places that want them badly, and they're great places. They have energy and they have everything. They're tremendous assets."

Large tech firms - often called AI hyperscalers due to the size of their investments in AI - have also been branching out, and Canada has made moves to step in to offer itself as an alternative location for these sites, with less local pushback against projects so far.

Meta has already broken ground on a data centre, external in the province of Alberta, and Anthropic has signed a long-term agreement to lease a proposed data centre in Nova Scotia.

The BBC has reached out to Meta for comment on Trump's remarks.

In September Google announced a €13bn ($15bn: £11bn) investment in AI infrastructure in Finland, which included funding for three data centres.

Last month, building work for two of those centres was halted due to concerns over the clearance of more than 300 hectares of forest.

Trump called out the build in Finland specifically, saying his administration was "not allowing that to happen".

Google has been contacted for comment.

## 2. TikTok lança ferramentas para identificar conteúdos de IA e deepfakes; veja como funcionam

- **Fonte:** G1 Tecnologia
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-20 18:30
- **Idioma:** pt
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://g1.globo.com/tecnologia/noticia/2026/11/20/tiktok-ferramentas-para-identificar-ia-e-deepfakes.ghtml

As medidas buscam ampliar a transparência sobre o uso da tecnologia e facilitar a identificação de deepfakes, como são conhecidos os vídeos, imagens e áudios manipulados por IA para simular situações reais.

Uma das novidades permitirá que qualquer pessoa verifique se uma imagem ou vídeo foi produzido ou editado com ferramentas de inteligência artificial do próprio TikTok, mesmo sem ter uma conta na rede social.

A outra, chamada Likeness Detection (detecção de semelhança), será voltada a criadores de conteúdo e figuras públicas, incluindo políticos. O recurso permitirá localizar e denunciar publicações que reproduzam sua aparência por meio de inteligência artificial, possivelmente sem consentimento.

Segundo a empresa, a primeira ferramenta já está disponível, enquanto a segunda começou a ser liberada gradualmente em diferentes países.

Os anúncios foram apresentados durante um evento sobre segurança e inovação promovido pelo TikTok em Londres, no Reino Unido.

A iniciativa também faz parte dos esforços da plataforma para combater conteúdos enganosos antes das eleições previstas para 2027 em países europeus.

Como vai funcionar a identificação de deepfakes?

A ferramenta Likeness Detection permitirá que pessoas elegíveis encontrem vídeos e contas do TikTok que possam estar utilizando sua aparência por meio de inteligência artificial.

O objetivo é facilitar a identificação de conteúdos que simulam a identidade de alguém, especialmente quando produzidos sem autorização.

Segundo a empresa, o funcionamento terá três etapas:

- Verificação de identidade: o usuário deverá se cadastrar na ferramenta e confirmar sua identidade por meio de um parceiro externo de confiança.

- Identificação de conteúdos: depois do cadastro, receberá notificações quando a plataforma identificar vídeos ou contas que possam estar utilizando sua aparência. O material poderá ser analisado diretamente na ferramenta.

- Denúncia: caso encontre conteúdos suspeitos, o usuário poderá denunciá-los às equipes de segurança do TikTok. As publicações que violarem as regras da plataforma serão removidas.

A empresa afirma que o sistema foi desenvolvido para reduzir a coleta de dados pessoais e preservar a privacidade dos usuários. Os documentos apresentados durante a verificação de identidade não serão armazenados pelo TikTok.

O recurso não estará disponível para todos os usuários. Segundo a plataforma, poderão utilizá-lo criadores de conteúdo e figuras públicas que atendam aos critérios de elegibilidade, incluindo contas de governos, políticos e partidos.

O TikTok não detalhou, nos comunicados, um calendário específico para a disponibilização da ferramenta no Brasil.

Como verificar se uma imagem ou vídeo foi criado por IA?

A segunda ferramenta anunciada pelo TikTok será aberta a qualquer pessoa, inclusive quem não possui uma conta na rede social.

O recurso permite verificar se uma imagem ou vídeo foi criado ou editado com as ferramentas de inteligência artificial da própria plataforma.

Para utilizar a ferramenta, o usuário poderá acessar uma página específica disponibilizada pelo TikTok e enviar a imagem ou o vídeo que deseja verificar. O sistema analisará o arquivo em busca de informações que indiquem o uso de recursos de inteligência artificial da plataforma.

Segundo a empresa, a verificação utiliza duas tecnologias:

- Content Credentials: sistema que registra informações sobre a origem e o processo de criação de conteúdos digitais.

- VeraDura: marca-d'água invisível desenvolvida pelo TikTok, projetada para resistir melhor a alterações e tentativas de remoção.

O TikTok explica que informações sobre a origem de um conteúdo podem desaparecer quando arquivos são baixados, compartilhados ou editados. A nova ferramenta busca facilitar essa identificação.

Segundo a empresa, a ferramenta identifica conteúdos criados ou editados com seus próprios recursos de IA, sem especificar se é capaz de reconhecer materiais produzidos por outras plataformas.

## 3. Anthropic defied the odds in its record IPO. Now it has to reckon with AI market turbulence

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-20 18:06
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/anthropic-defies-odds-holding-ipo-despite-massively-disruptive-market.html

The moment felt impossible.

Between Jensen Huang's shocking exit from chip giant Nvidia, voters' rebuke of artificial intelligence in this month's midterm elections, and rising interest rates that threatened to stymie plans for a multitrillion-dollar infrastructure buildout, there seemed to be no way Anthropic could carry out an initial public offering.

Anthropic's response? Let's go!

"Very few companies would've attempted it in this environment," said Byron Deeter, a partner at Bessemer Venture Partners, which counts Anthropic as its biggest investment ever, in an interview. "These things collectively have been massively disruptive, yet the team stuck to its true north and got the largest financing in IPO history done, and it's just remarkable."

Anthropic's stock jumped 17% in its Nasdaq debut on Thursday, closing at $97.17 per share and giving the Claude creator a market cap of roughly $1.8 trillion. That makes it the seventh-most valuable U.S. company, despite being less than six years old.

The shares sold off on Friday, closing at $88.30, just a few dollars above the IPO price. It's a reminder of the uncertainty richly valued companies face in their early days on the public market. Facebook had a tough first year after its IPO in 2012 due to concerns surrounding its mobile prospects. And after Uber's IPO in May 2019, shares of the ride-hailing company trended lower the rest of that year.

Earlier on Thursday, the second floor of the Nasdaq was decorated in orange, Anthropic's signature color. Founders and backers were given Claude lapel pins that served as their ticket to the bell ringing, which was followed by a champagne toast ahead of the opening. With the first trade, the stock shot up to $111.50.

The previous afternoon Anthropic raised $81.3 billion, topping Elon Musk's SpaceX for the largest IPO on record.

It was a wild feat, and Anthropic backers breathed a sigh of relief. Days earlier, Altimeter Capital's Brad Gerstner, whose firm put in an order for $5 billion of stock in the IPO, acknowledged that the election results, the semiconductor sell-off and the Huang scandal created "a very bad backdrop" for the offering. Nvidia shares fell for a seventh straight weekly, losing 46% of their value over that stretch.

Malcolm Ethridge, managing partner at Capital Area Planning Group and a CNBC contributor, pointed to the high levels of volatility in the market as a real risk to going public. The Cboe Volatility Index (VIX) is up 46% this month, including a 16% surge on Thursday, to over 28.4.

Ethridge said that with the VIX above 25, most companies would consider "shelving" an IPO. But Anthropic decided, "this is our window," Ethridge said.

In the face of stout headwinds, investors told CNBC that the combination of Anthropic's historic growth rate (1,200% year over year), its adjusted operating profit and its openness to constructive feedback from Wall Street contributed to this week's gravity-defying moment.

"Just truly, truly market redefining," Iconiq partner Will Griffith, an Anthropic investor, said in an interview. "The ability to achieve this growth, at this scale, in this short of a time frame, this efficiently — the world has never seen that before."

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei co-founded Anthropic in 2021, after he and a handful of other researchers defected from OpenAI, now its chief rival, to pursue what they viewed as a safer approach to AI. The company captivated the corporate world with its Claude AI models and products, which fueled its unprecedented rise.

With tens of billions of dollars flooding in over the past few years from private investors and tech giants, Anthropic was able to secure the computing power needed to train frontier AI models and serve customers with an expanding portfolio of services.

Anthropic executives, including CFO Krishna Rao, used those funding rounds as opportunities to build goodwill with the market, particularly when it came to pricing. The company opted not to squeeze every last dollar out of the ecosystem, preferring, in Amodei's words, to "underpromise and overdeliver."

"This isn't a company that's focused on optimizing price," Deeter said. "They could have priced the IPO higher. At every financing they have chosen to leave some money on the table."

Anthropic has also worked to prove itself as a company that can deploy capital responsibly. Of the more than $140 billion that the company has raised, it's only spent around $10 billion, which helped to reassure investors that it could weather a potential storm.

The company confidentially filed its prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission in June, and OpenAI, followed days later. But by August, OpenAI executives signaled that the ChatGPT maker was targeting a 2027 debut, as a series of security incidents rattled the industry and opposition to data centers swept the nation.

The market, stung by rising oil prices and the Federal Reserve's plan to raise interest rates, soured on the AI trade and lost its appetite for IPOs. Biometric ring maker Oura, Australian AI data center operator Firmus and SK Hynix's U.S.-based Solidigm subsidiary all postponed or abandoned plans to go public.

Gerstner said Altimeter reduced its exposure to the broader AI trade to the lowest in three years. Meanwhile, the firm aimed to put five times as much into Anthropic's IPO as it had invested in any prior initial share sale, Gerstner told CNBC.

Anthropic began testing the waters with investors in August, before kicking off its official roadshow last week. It was all taking place while many AI stocks were melting down.

Having successfully reached the public market, Anthropic now has a boatload of much-needed cash on hand as it continues to spend aggressively on compute capacity.

Anthropic's IPO fundraise, including shares sold to underwriters, totaled $93.5 billion, topping the $85.7 billion raised by SpaceX. Add to that another $26 billion from a parallel share sale to a handful of tech companies, and the more than $130 billion in cash and equivalents on Anthropic's balance sheet at the end of September.

It's only been one day for Anthropic as a public company, but the AI lab's financial standing is about as solid as anyone could have hoped.

"Today puts the company in a stronger financial position, and in doing so, only strengthens our resolve to do the right thing," Amodei said in a speech at the Nasdaq on Thursday. "If we can show it's possible to make the right choices on safety and still build an incredibly successful company, others in our industry will be inspired to do the same."

— CNBC's Leslie Picker and Jordan Novet contributed to this report.

Anthropic said it sold an aggregate of 1,127,000,000 shares of its Class A common stock at $83 per share in the offering. Anthropic dodged an unforgiving series of obstacles on its road to the Nasdaq on Thursday, as investors brooded over Jensen Huang's shocking ouster at Nvidia, voters' stinging rebuke of the tech during the midterms and whether rising interest rates would stymie industry plans for a multitrillion-dollar AI infrastructure buildout.

## 5. Anthropic IPO 'exactly what the markets need,' but analysts warn of volatility

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-20 01:51
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 7.5
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/anthropic-ipo-stocks-market.html

Anthropic's blockbuster IPO on Thursday gave markets a much-needed boost after weeks of weakness, though analysts warned that the stock could remain volatile in the coming weeks.

Share of the Claude maker opened at $111.50 and closed at $97.17 per share, 17% above the IPO price. The offering raised $81.3 billion through the sale of 980 million shares — surpassing SpaceX's June listing to become the largest IPO on record.

Nomura strategist Charlie McElligott described investors as "foaming at the mouth" for the offering, with allocations "impossibly low," underscoring the strength of demand.

"Anthropic's IPO is exactly what the markets need to revive overall sentiment," said Rebecca Walser, chief investment officer at Walser Wealth Management, adding that markets have been "depressed with all of the negativity in the AI complex over the past several weeks."

Still, U.S. equities fell sharply on Thursday, with the tech heavy Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.25% to end at 23,879.20, S&P 500 down 2.02% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average down 2.19%.

Artificial intelligence and technology stocks initially rose thanks to the IPO, but the gains faded as "profit-taking spread across AI-related stocks," said James Ooi, chief market strategist at Tiger Brokers.

While the initial pop was not surprising for such a highly anticipated listing, investors should avoid "chasing the IPO," Walser said.

She expects the stock to come under pressure in the coming weeks as former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang testifies before the U.S. Senate, potentially reviving concerns around the AI sector.

"The shares remain highly volatile," said Morningstar senior equity analyst Malik Ahmed Khan.

Walser said that any weakness around the time of Huang's testimony could offer an entry point for investors interested in Anthropic, adding that she remains bullish on the OpenAI rival over the long term.

"For investors, it's going to become a blue-chip stock like Google," Ross Gerber, president and CEO of Gerber Kawasaki Wealth & Investment Management told CNBC's "Squawk Box Asia."

He added that U.S. technology valuations are at ridiculously low levels relative to the "crazy amazing innovation" coming out of California.

Morningstar's Khan struck a more cautious tone, warning investors against getting swept up in the enthusiasm around Anthropic, advising them instead to step back and "carefully" assess the company as a long-term business.

That will depend on Anthropic's ability to scale enterprise adoption, continue improving its models and turn those capabilities into economically useful products and services in the AI ecosystem, he said.

"We remain confident that the firm will be able to execute on all three fronts," Khan added, setting a $92 fair value estimate for the stock.

Walser stated that Anthropic is "extremely important for the overall AI story" as the first non-Chinese large language model developer to go public.

Its listing gives public-market investors their first direct test of how a major standalone AI model developer will be valued and perform outside the chip sector. Meanwhile, rival OpenAI could follow with an IPO sometime in 2027.

"This IPO is the show me moment for the AI story," Walser said, as much of the stock market's AI narrative has so far centered on chipmakers.

AIを用いたサイバー攻撃は完全に自動化されつつあるため、発見から攻撃までの時間や攻撃そのものにかかる時間は劇的に短縮されています。

今回提供開始が発表された「Microsoft Perception」は、こうしたAIのサイバー攻撃に対抗するためのAIエージェント群により構成される新サービスです。

3つのエージェントチームがサイバー攻撃に対抗

## 7. Ações da Anthropic caem 8,5% um dia após estreia histórica na bolsa dos EUA

- **Idioma:** pt
- **Relevância:** 7.0

Placa da Anthropic , PBC (ANT) no One Times Square no dia em que a Anthropic realiza sua oferta pública inicial (IPO) na NASDAQ Market, em Nova York, EUA, em 19 de novembro de 2026 — Foto: REUTERS/Shannon Stapleton

As ações da Anthropic, criadora do chatbot de inteligência artificial (IA) Claude, fecharam em queda de 8,5% nesta sexta-feira (20), a US$ 88,91, um dia após a estreia histórica da empresa na bolsa de valores dos Estados Unidos.

O resultado aproximou os papéis dos US$ 83 fixados na oferta pública inicial de ações (IPO, na sigla em inglês), em meio ao crescente pessimismo dos investidores em relação ao setor de IA.

Na quinta-feira (19), primeiro dia de negociação, as ações da empresa encerraram o pregão a US$ 97,17. A abertura de capital movimentou US$ 93,5 bilhões, superando o recorde anterior de US$ 85,7 bilhões da SpaceX, de Elon Musk.

A estreia da Anthropic era vista como uma oportunidade de recuperar o entusiasmo dos investidores com as empresas de inteligência artificial. A queda das ações, porém, mostrou que nem mesmo o maior IPO da história foi suficiente para reverter a cautela que domina o setor.

O setor de IA tem enfrentado pressão no último mês, diante das crescentes preocupações de que os investimentos na tecnologia, que podem ultrapassar US$ 1 trilhão neste ano, segundo algumas estimativas, não estejam gerando retornos com rapidez suficiente.

O receio de obstáculos políticos à construção de centros de dados e a turbulência na liderança da fabricante de chips Nvidia também contribuíram para essas preocupações.

"A Anthropic perdeu força relativamente rápido. Não acho que os investidores acreditem que exista algo fundamentalmente errado com a empresa, sua administração ou seus negócios. Acho que eles estão apenas céticos em relação a esse mercado", afirmou Robert Pavlik, gestor sênior de portfólio da Dakota Wealth, à Reuters.

David Miller, diretor de investimentos da Catalyst Funds, disse à agência que os investidores "ainda acreditam na inteligência artificial, mas estão se tornando mais seletivos em relação ao preço que estão dispostos a pagar por esse crescimento".

O movimento também atingiu outras gigantes de tecnologia. Entre as chamadas Sete Magníficas, a Nvidia caiu cerca de 14%, enquanto a Meta recuou quase 6%, a Amazon perdeu aproximadamente 4% e a Microsoft encerrou o pregão em queda de 3%.

As fabricantes de semicondutores também registraram perdas. As ações da AMD e da Marvell Technology caíram cerca de 13% e 12%, respectivamente, enquanto a Intel recuou aproximadamente 8%.

Valor de mercado

Considerando o preço mais recente das ações, a Anthropic tinha um valor de mercado de US$ 1,2 trilhão nesta sexta-feira, calculado com base nos papéis atualmente em circulação.

Já a avaliação totalmente diluída, que considera também ações que poderão ser emitidas no futuro, como as vinculadas a opções e outros direitos, chegava a aproximadamente US$ 1,6 trilhão.

## 12. ‘We’re the most arrogant of all apes!’ A delightfully mind-expanding encounter with Peter Gabriel

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-20 02:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/music/2026/nov/20/peter-gabriel-interview-ai-music-bonobo-ape-duets-jurassic-park-plants

Peter Gabriel asks me to fetch my laptop: he wants to show me a video of himself playing a keyboard duet with a bonobo called Kanzi. In fairness, said footage is, as Gabriel suggests, “extraordinary”. Working with primatologist Sue Savage-Rumbaugh, who developed a means of communicating with bonobos involving sign language and a speech-synthesis keyboard, Gabriel plays droning chords on a synthesiser while Kanzi picks out complementary notes on a piano. “Look, she likes that note, which is a ninth, and then she finds the octave,” he enthuses as the video plays. “There’s a real musicality there.”

It is all deeply unlikely, but it seems the perfect illustration of how easily a conversation with Gabriel can go wildly – if fascinatingly – off-piste. I’ve only been in his company for 10 minutes, long enough to ask a solitary question about his epic new album o\i: its 13 tracks last nearly 90 minutes, the result, he suggests, of a longstanding interest in African music “which made me realise that a song could last as long as a song could last”, and of “being old enough to think ‘fuck it’, really”. For anyone who feels that an hour and a half of exquisitely arranged, beautifully produced, occasionally deeply strange art rock – its sound shapeshifting from the folk-flecked A Hard Lesson to the glowing pop hues of Till Your Mind Is Shining – is too meagre an offering, it arrives in two versions: a “dark” mix by US producer Tchad Blake and a “bright” version that’s the handiwork of Mike “Spike” Stent, famed for working with everyone from U2 and Madonna to Frank Ocean and Harry Styles.

I had mentioned the two versions, a routine enough inquiry, but it quickly turns into a discussion about the potential replacement of car stereos with immersive sound systems, which then unexpectedly shifts into an explanation of the work of Michael Levin, an American professor of bioengineering who specialises in morphogenesis. “He’s found some bio-electric instructions that can make an eye grow on the leg of a frog, for example,” says Gabriel, and has apparently “created life forms that never existed before, which really challenges evolution.” Gabriel is interested in “sonifying” some of his data and using it in a subsequent mix of the album.

From there, we move on to software engineer Blaise Agüera y Arcas, whose experiments with an esoteric 90s programming language called Brainfuck have led him to “the radical idea that some constants in maths might actually have some form of life”. This leads us, via the “re-evaluation of what consciousness is and what intelligence is”, to the belief of internet pioneer Vint Cerf that attempting to communicate with other intelligent species might prepare us for the possibility of encountering aliens, and thus to Savage-Rumbaugh and Kanzi the bonobo.

“She used to allow the bonobos to watch videos, and to choose the videos they watched,” Gabriel says, laughing, “and their favourite, perhaps obviously, was Planet of the Apes, then Harry and the Hendersons, which is a film about a sasquatch who came to live with a family. The whole thing blew me away. I think we’re the most arrogant of all apes, and there’s just so much intelligence we’ve chosen to ignore and instead see the world as something that’s entirely available for our exploitation, and I think that’s cost us, as we are now learning.”

Morphogenisis, inter-species communication, the re-evaluation of consciousness: it should go without saying that you don’t usually end up discussing these things when you ask an august rock legend about his latest album. But, as it turns out, that’s what inevitably happens when you ask Gabriel a question. We meet in a pub not far from his home in Notting Hill. He says he’s here “to sell the new album”, but almost every query about it seems to lead him somewhere completely different: science, activism, the nature of consciousness, how language is “a fairly crude tool” for communication, the controversial 1970s self-improvement courses of Werner Erhard.

You can’t fault his diversity of interests, even if that diversity of interests is why it takes so long for him to make albums. o\i is essentially the second part of a project that began with 2023’s i/o, which was his first album of new material in 21 years. Like its predecessor, it contains songs that Gabriel has spent decades tinkering with: he started work on A Hard Lesson in 1989, when he was still a huge mainstream pop star thanks to 1986’s So, an album that shifted more than 5m copies in the US alone.

Yes, he concedes, it is a bit weird to find yourself, at the age of 76, still working on songs you started writing in your 30s: “I recognise that guy, but you’re bound to make different decisions having lived a little longer.” But that’s just the way it is. His friend Laurie Anderson once suggested that Gabriel’s problem was that he was more interested in the process of making something than in finishing it. He thinks the real issue is that he’s easily distracted.

“If you have some measure of success, you end up meeting all sorts of people and having fascinating conversations, which lead to detours, and you think, ‘Well, life is for living,’” he says. “If you get on that conveyor belt of album-tour-album, there’s not time to breathe or have any kind of life, and that always seems somewhat sad to me. I think the diversions feed the music. You know, if you want a better class of shit, you’ve got to eat a more varied diet.”

Hence the digressive nature of our interview. Gabriel seems to have developed a genuinely intriguing theory about virtually everything, from theme parks to funerals: he thinks people should “plant a tree over their dead body, or the dead body of their loved ones, so you’d get woods where there wouldn’t be any signage, but if you go and sit under your dad’s tree, your phone would have the GPS position and you would start seeing his videos, hearing his voice, all the digital data would be locked into that GPS point”.

Moreover, he has an incredibly engaging way of talking about all this stuff, evincing such enthusiasm and curiosity that you’re carried along with him: on the off-chance that you wanted to pitch the idea of growing eyeballs on the legs of frogs, you could do substantially worse than get Gabriel to act as your salesman. It probably helps his cause that Gabriel has a hugely impressive track record of thinking ahead of the curve.

At the height of his fame as the frontman of Genesis, he was sharp enough to realise that the genre of progressive rock they had helped invent was becoming too grandiloquent and remote for its own good – “prancing around in fairyland” as he once put it – and that the band he had formed might be more successful without him. The latter opinion wasn’t very popular with the rest of Genesis, but nevertheless proved to be spot on.

A few years later, he was the first British artist to realise that the future of pop lay in sampling: for some time in the early 80s, if you wanted to use a sampler on your new track you had to borrow Gabriel’s, because he was the first person in the country to own one; he then set up a company to import and distribute the new technology. Twenty years later, he cottoned on very quickly to the idea that music was going to primarily exist online, co-founding a streaming platform that might have become the industry standard had it not launched at exactly the same time as a competitor called Spotify.

If he was right about everything from sampling to streaming to the latter-day career of Genesis, perhaps he’s also right about AI. In a musical world filled with people decrying its malign influence, Gabriel is one of its few high-profile boosters: he recently suggested that ultimately “everything will be done better” by AI than by humans.

He says it again today, albeit with caveats. “The application of AI and robots in warfare is really scary. There’s the sense that we’re creating a species that is way, way more intelligent than we are. If we look around, there are not many good examples of intelligent species looking after less-intelligent species, except as pets.

“You can see the worry on [computer scientist and ‘Godfather of AI’] Geoffrey Hinton’s face,” he continues. “I think we need to slow it down a bit, we need some international agreement, for which there are precedents, like in air safety or the Start treaty for nuclear weapons. We need guard rails, like a seed of wisdom in every algorithm before it’s allowed to be released that could allow for monitoring and a measure of control and an ‘off’ button. But it interests me in the moment as a whole new set of tools that could encourage people to consider themselves film-makers, painters, musicians, to explore themselves in that way.”

With or without AI, Gabriel says he thinks that’s true anyway: “Being those things is just a matter of walking around and saying, ‘This is what I am’, then committing to learning about it. You can get accelerated learning through AI – even though you haven’t learned all the skills to get there, you can quickly access some of the joy of making really nice-sounding things or have the AI eliminate some of your worst moments. So in the long run, as a creative tool: absolutely extraordinary. It’s like you’ve been painting in black and white and someone suddenly throws a colour palette at you, saying: ‘What can you do with this?’”

Contrary to most, Gabriel genuinely seems to believe that AI is capable of creating great art. “It’s just down to better algorithms. So how do we deal with this as a species? I think long-term, if you can’t defeat it, you have to join it. I think we will become cyborgs, because we’ll have these brain-computer interfaces, where you insert something in your brain that allows you then to communicate to the computer … I don’t think there’s any reason why we shouldn’t use our brains to become more AI.”
