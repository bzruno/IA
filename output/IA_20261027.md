## 2. Can we trust LLMs to judge whether a research idea is novel?

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-27 13:12
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 9.5
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/novelty-bench

Today we’re releasing Old Ideas, Novel Problems, a new study of how reliably large language models can evaluate the novelty of scientific research ideas. Alongside the paper, we’re publishing a benchmark, data, and code for testing novelty judges, plus an automated pipeline researchers can use to build new evaluation sets.

At Ai2, we’ve been developing AI systems that support different parts of scientific discovery. DataVoyager helps researchers explore and analyze structured data, AutoDiscovery generates and tests hypotheses, and Theorizer synthesizes evidence across large bodies of literature into candidate scientific theories. Together with Asta, these projects point toward systems that can help researchers move from understanding prior work to proposing, testing, and refining new ideas.

But generating research ideas is only part of the challenge of automating scientific discovery. Those ideas also need to contribute something genuinely new, which means these systems need a way to distinguish a novel direction from one that mostly repackages existing work.

That judgment is often handed to another LLM—what we call a novelty judge. Novelty judges assess whether a research idea is new relative to prior work, either on its own or in comparison with another idea. There’s no standard way to build one; research teams choose a model, prompt, evaluation format, reasoning budget, and sometimes a literature-retrieval system. Those choices can materially affect the verdict. Yet these judges are often validated only on human-written papers rather than the AI-generated ideas they are actually meant to evaluate.

Our new benchmark results suggest novelty evaluation itself is far from reliable. Across six LLM judges – GPT-5.1, GPT-5.2, GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Opus 4.5, and Claude Opus 4.6 – seemingly small changes to the evaluation setup produce dramatically different judgments of the same ideas. In one experiment, changing just a few words in a prompt shifts a model’s accuracy in identifying which of two ideas is more novel by more than 50 percentage points. Retrieval and additional reasoning do not consistently improve judgments, and two purpose-built novelty evaluation systems perform worse than a simple prompted Claude Opus 4.6 baseline.

If AI is going to play a larger role in scientific discovery, we need more dependable ways to tell whether it is actually proposing something new. Our work provides tools for studying that problem, while showing how much remains unresolved.

To test how reliably novelty judges can distinguish more novel research ideas from less novel ones, we first needed examples where there was strong evidence about the originality of the underlying scientific contribution.

So we built an automated pipeline around ICLR 2026 peer reviews. Rather than treating paper acceptance as a proxy for novelty, we mined the reviews themselves for passages where reviewers explicitly discussed a contribution’s originality. That distinction is key; a paper can be accepted without being particularly novel as long as it is strong enough in other aspects. Likewise, a rejected paper with an original contribution can have other weaknesses.

We created a high-novelty pool of 154 human-authored research ideas by selecting accepted papers in the top-rated 10% of their ICLR research area whose reviewers positively identified novelty and where no reviewer disputed it. We applied the reverse criteria to build a lower-novelty pool: 145 ideas from rejected papers in the bottom 10% whose reviewers raised originality concerns without any reviewer positively identifying novelty. In a separate check of the review-mining process, an annotator agreed with the extracted novelty-signal polarity in 47 of 50 cases; none of the disagreements would have changed whether a reviewer was counted as praising or disputing the paper’s novelty.

Together, these two pools form our first evaluation setting, Human-Only, which tests whether novelty judges can tell apart human-authored ideas with strong evidence of higher or lower novelty. But in practice, novelty judges are increasingly being used to evaluate AI-generated ideas. So we created a second setting, Human+Generated.

For this pool, we paired the same 154 validated human ideas with 154 ideas produced by a simple LLM generator. We assumed the generated ideas were less novel than their human-authored counterparts; these are weak labels, not verified judgments for every idea. The generator received only a research area and an instruction to propose a novel idea, with no literature search, tools, iteration, or feedback. This gave us a conservative comparison between ideas from a basic generator and contributions from top-10% ICLR papers whose reviewers had explicitly endorsed their originality.

As additional checks, an expert independently reviewed 30 Human+Generated pairs, heavily weighted toward cases our strongest judge got wrong, and we rewrote the human- and AI-generated ideas into a common structure to test whether judges were simply reacting to differences in writing style. The expert preferred the human idea in all 30 pairs and judged none of the generated ideas novel.

Importantly, many of our core findings don’t depend on the labels at all. If two reasonable evaluation setups give opposite answers for the same pair of ideas, that disagreement itself reveals instability.

Using our collections of human-authored and AI-generated research ideas, we tested how well six LLM judges – GPT-5.1, GPT-5.2, GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Opus 4.5, and Claude Opus 4.6 – could evaluate novelty through both pointwise (i.e., absolute novelty ratings) and pairwise (i.e., comparing two ideas to find the most novel) comparisons. We varied one factor at a time: the prompt, reasoning budget, whether the judge could see related papers, how the ideas were formatted, how many judgments were combined into a final verdict, and – for AI-generated ideas – which model generated them.

The largest shifts in performance came from how we prompted the model to judge novelty.

For example, we showed GPT-5.4 the same Human+Generated pairs – each containing one validated human-authored idea and one AI-generated idea – with two prompts. One prompt told the model that reviewers had found exactly one idea in each pair to be novel and asked it to identify which one; the other simply asked which idea was novel. When we switched to the simpler prompt, accuracy dropped from 93.5% to 40.9% – below chance – and the model changed its verdict on more than half of the same pairs.

By contrast, the same prompt change shifted accuracy by only about seven percentage points on the Human-Only dataset, which pairs human-authored ideas with stronger and weaker evidence of novelty. That suggests a novelty judge can appear relatively stable on human-authored research but behave very differently on AI-generated ideas.

That said, human-authored ideas weren’t immune. We saw substantial shifts on the Human-Only dataset as well; the same change moved Claude Opus 4.6 accuracy by 21 points. The shifts were just less severe than on the AI-generated ideas.

There was also no universally better prompt. For example, when judging ideas individually, dropping an instruction that warned judges not to treat trivial or incremental contributions as novel improved accuracy for some judges while reducing it for others. A prompt that works well for one model may not transfer to another, in other words.

We also tested a common use of novelty judges: comparing ideas from two AI generators. We paired ideas from Claude Opus 4.5 with Claude Sonnet 4.5, and GPT-5.4 with GPT-5.1. Across the tested prompt changes, roughly a quarter to a third of verdicts changed on average across six judges. This disagreement can be measured without assuming which generator produces more novel ideas, showing how the choice of judge prompt can affect comparisons between ideation systems.

More search and reasoning didn’t necessarily produce better novelty judgments. We gave GPT-5.6 Sol an extra-high reasoning budget and live, iterative search restricted to arXiv, then compared it with standard-retrieval judges that each saw five pre-retrieved abstracts. (All judges in this comparison ran once per idea on Human+Generated.) The agentic judge scored 0.78 macro-F1 – 11 points behind Claude Opus 4.6 at 0.89, and in roughly the same range as simpler Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.4 setups – while costing roughly 10× more per decision at current API prices. (In this context, macro-F1 measures how well a judge identifies novel and non-novel ideas; higher is better.)

Purpose-built evaluators weren’t more accurate either, despite costing substantially more. On the Human+Generated set, a simple Claude Opus 4.6 prompt using low reasoning and majority voting across three calls achieved 0.854 macro-F1 at a total evaluation cost of $2.59. By comparison, Idea Novelty Checker, a literature-grounded retrieval and reranking pipeline, scored 0.642 at $30.66, while Sakana AI’s AI Scientist, whose novelty evaluator iteratively searches Semantic Scholar, scored 0.772 at $82.05. (Both dedicated evaluators used Claude Opus 4.6, with one call per decision.)

In other words, the simple prompted baseline was both more accurate and considerably cheaper than either purpose-built system.

Taken together, these results suggest that novelty evaluation is particularly brittle on the AI-generated ideas these systems are increasingly being asked to judge.

Our results suggest that LLM-based novelty evaluation remains a major open problem. Judges that appear reliable on human-authored research may behave differently on AI-generated ideas; prompts that help one model can hurt another; and more search or compute does not necessarily produce more consistent judgments.

Future work should test judges on the kinds of ideas they will actually evaluate, measure sensitivity across prompts and models, improve how prior literature is incorporated, and build benchmarks that can evolve as the scientific literature grows and AI systems improve. Our benchmark and reusable data pipeline are a starting point—researchers can apply them to new peer reviews, other scientific communities, and future generations of novelty judges.

Automated ideation depends on more than generating plausible research directions; we also need ways to tell when those ideas are genuinely new. By making the weaknesses of current novelty judges easier to measure, we hope this release helps researchers build more robust ways to evaluate AI-generated research ideas.

米Google傘下のGoogle DeepMindは10月27日（現地時間）、インタラクティブな仮想世界をリアルタイムで生成するAIモデル「Genie 4」を発表した。「Genie 3」よりも映像のリアリティや物理現象の再現能力を高め、環境音や音楽、登場人物の会話も同時に生成できるようになった。まずは限定されたテスター向けに研究プレビューとして提供し、今後、「Google AI Ultra」加入者や開発者にも提供する計画だ。

Genieは、テキストや画像から仮想世界を生成し、ユーザーの操作に応じて世界を変化させる「ワールドモデル」。あらかじめ完成した映像を再生する一般的な動画生成AIとは異なり、ユーザーが仮想空間を移動したり、環境に働きかけたりすると、その結果をリアルタイムで生成する。

Genie 4では、自然言語で天候を変えたり、人物や物体を追加したり、カメラを移動させたりできる。空気力学や衝突などの物理現象をシミュレートできるとしている。最も大きな進化として挙げるのは人物の表現で、自然に動いて環境に反応する人物を生成でき、その行動をリアルタイムで指示することもできるという。

新たに音声のネイティブ生成に対応し、風や波などの環境音だけでなく、登場人物が状況やユーザーの入力に応じて会話することも可能になった。

技術面では、GoogleのAIモデル「Gemini」ファミリーとの連携を強化した。「Gemini Omni」が現実世界に即した外観や動作の生成を支え、「Gemini 3.8 Flash」が生成中の場面を監視し、複数の手順からなる行動を計画・修正する。さらに「Gemini 4 Argon」と組み合わせ、ロボットが複雑な作業を実行する様子をシミュレーションする実験も進めている。

Google DeepMindは、こうした技術を映画制作やゲーム開発、教育、ロボットやAIエージェントの訓練などに応用できるとしている。ただし、現段階では連続して操作できる時間は数分程度で、長時間の利用では物体の外観や位置の一貫性が失われることがある。複雑な物理的相互作用や音声の正確性などにも課題が残る。

Genie 4は今後、仮想世界の生成・探索サービス「Project Genie」を通じてGoogle AI Ultra加入者に提供するほか、「Gemini API」と「Google AI Studio」でも早期アクセスパートナーに提供する予定だ。Project Genie向けにはウェイトリストへの登録を受け付けている。生成される映像と音声には、AI生成コンテンツを識別する電子透かし「SynthID」を埋め込む。

Google DeepMindは2025年8月にGenie 3を発表し、2026年1月には同モデルを利用したProject Genieの試験提供を開始した。5月にはGoogleストリートビューとの連携も発表している。

From the end of humanity to the company's current clash with the Trump administration, Anthropic's IPO document underscores how one of the most well-capitalized startups in history foresees the future of its operations.

The Claude maker's risks include a laundry list of ways its models can cause harm, including potentially "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity."

It's imperative for Anthropic to emphasize AI safety because failure to do so could adversely affect its "brand, business, financial condition, and results of operations," the filing said. The public's souring position on data centers poses a major risk for Anthropic, which relies on an ever-expanding portfolio of state-of-the-art computing facilities to train and maintain its prized foundation models.

And with the midterm elections approaching, the tech industry's massive data center build-out has turned political, with lawmakers in 16 states considering moratoriums, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures.

"Opposition to data center development has also become more organized and coordinated across jurisdictions and our public profile may draw particular attention to projects associated with us," the filing said.

Anthropic's future success depends not only on building giant data centers, but also on the electrical and related power infrastructure needed to juice those computing facilities.

Anthropic knows it has work to do protecting children "as adoption of our models grows, including by minors and other vulnerable users, notwithstanding our efforts to restrict such access."

The company calling to pace the frontier also says it's a risk to move too slowly.

Anthropic needs to keep racing and needs the rest of the business world to keep up. If companies curb their AI spending, Anthropic could feel the burn.

"If the market for our models, platform, and applications develops more slowly than we expect or declines, our growth may slow or stall, and our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected," the filing says.

The prospectus also cites reduced demand for frontier models as a risk. Customers might find that lower-cost models work fine for them and that "incremental performance gains at the frontier do not justify the additional cost," instead choosing cheaper, or open-source alternatives.

## 9. PFNの国産フラグシップモデル「PLaMo 3.1 Prime」発表 オンプレでDeep Research対応 API無料プランも開始

- **Fonte:** ITmedia AI+
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-27 23:32
- **Idioma:** ja
- **Relevância:** 6.5
- **Link:** https://www.itmedia.co.jp/aiplus/article/2610/28/2000002652/

PFNの国産フラグシップモデル「PLaMo 3.1 Prime」発表 オンプレでDeep Research対応 API無料プランも開始（1/2 ページ）

Preferred Networks（以下、PFN）は10月27日、国内でフルスクラッチ開発するAIモデル「PLaMo」のフラグシップモデル「PLaMo 3.1 Prime」（以下、3.1 Prime）の提供を始めた。Web検索機能と、オンプレミス環境でも使える「Deep Research」機能を搭載し、企業の調査業務を支援するとしている。同モデルを試せる「PLaMo API」の無料プランの提供も開始した。

Deep Researchは、調査テーマを入力すると、調査計画の立案から出典付きレポートの作成までを数分～数十分で自律的に実行するという。オンプレミスで社内文書の検索システムと連携させ、社内文書とWebの情報を組み合わせた調査や、社内文書に限った調査が可能としている。

3.1 Primeは、情報通信研究機構（NICT）との共同研究で得られた事前学習モデルをベースに開発。事後学習により、知らないことは「知らない」と答える事実性と、与えられたコンテキストを正しく読んで答える忠実性を高めたという。「AA Omniscience」など8つのハルシネーション関連ベンチマークで、前モデル「PLaMo 3.0 Prime」（以下、3.0 Prime）からスコアが平均約15％改善したと説明している。

犯罪などの不適切な行為に関する質問には、回答を拒否していた3.0 Primeと異なり、質問の前提の誤りを説明するなどしてユーザーの適切な判断を助けるとしている。

## 11. How AI Is Changing the Pace of Discovery for Millions Living With Dementia

- **Fonte:** Nvidia
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-27 12:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.5
- **Link:** https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/open-source-ai-tools-cbrain-dementia/

There are 57 million people worldwide living with dementia today. For the researchers working to help them, the obstacle isn’t a lack of effort — it’s too much data, too few experts to synthesize it and a pace of discovery that can’t keep up with the disease.

The Consortium for Biomedical Research and AI in Neurodegeneration (C-BRAIN), working with NVIDIA, is using AI to change that.

Alzheimer’s disease is one of today’s leading global health challenges, with over 10 million new dementia cases diagnosed each year. Despite a 7x increase in National Institutes of Health funding in the last 15 years — reaching $3.9 billion annually — no treatment has been found to stop, prevent or reverse the disease yet.

The bottlenecks are not enough high impact scientific findings, too much data living in institutional silos and too slow a pace of synthesis to keep up with the research that is published.

C-BRAIN, led by Dr. Randall Bateman and a consortium of 18 member institutions across academic, philanthropic, pharma and research organizations, launched in November 2025 to help scientists make better, faster discoveries by accelerating the research that makes new treatments possible.

“We have a window of opportunity with artificial intelligence to do research fundamentally different than we’ve ever done before, in a way that accelerates discoveries to help patients,” said Bateman, founder and director of C-BRAIN and the Charles F. and Joanne Knight Distinguished Professor of Neurology at the Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis. He described their work as “AI tools built by scientists, for scientists.”

The consortium is building a system that harnesses previously untapped Alzheimer’s research data to support scientists’ work.

Speeding Research From Weeks to Hours

Essential assets in Alzheimer’s research are datasets that have never been publicly accessible to AI tools or human researchers. This includes unpublished findings, negative results and governed proprietary data from member institutions.

“A lot of life sciences data doesn’t make its way into the frontier models,” said Dr. Ade Ojewole, chief technology officer of C-BRAIN. “The consequence of that is we’re leaving a lot of intelligence on the table.”

C-BRAIN refers to this as “dark data,” which slows down Alzheimer’s research.

Some dark data is directly deposited into secure computer systems for AI tools to access. To use this data without compromising patient privacy or institutional governance, C-BRAIN uses a hybrid centralized and federated approach — where AI models learn from data at each institution without that data ever having to leave its walls. This kind of infrastructure is technically demanding to build and scale, which is part of what NVIDIA brings to the collaboration.

C-BRAIN currently runs 64 NVIDIA data center GPUs on premises alongside cloud-based nodes on Microsoft Azure, with additional NVIDIA GPU capacity, including NVIDIA Blackwell systems, on the horizon as the consortium’s ambitions scale.

To explore how AI and accelerated computing can advance Alzheimer’s research while navigating its technical demands, C-BRAIN is working with NVIDIA to incorporate technologies including the NVIDIA BioNeMo open developer platform and the MONAI framework for C-BRAIN’s research pipelines.

“The C-BRAIN collaboration with NVIDIA aims to unlock those models in a way that they can deliver something quite impactful and important,” said Bateman.

“A great deal of optimization is going to be needed as we push the boundaries of what today’s spatial omics machine learning architectures can model,” Ojewole said. “NVIDIA software is definitely going to come into play there.”

Researchers at C-BRAIN have already built two tools: OpenScientist — built with Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory in an effort led by research scientist Justin Reese — and InsightEngine, built with 387 Labs.

OpenScientist is an open source agentic AI co-scientist that takes a scientist’s research question and dataset, then semi-autonomously generates hypotheses, runs analyses and produces verifiable, auditable insights. InsightEngine is an open source research platform that uses agentic AI to test hypotheses against the scientific literature, analyzing user-supplied datasets, exploring a biomedical knowledge graph and checking every claim against evidence to catch hallucinations before researchers commit to costly experiments.

In published case studies, OpenScientist completed analyses in minutes that domain scientists estimated would take weeks to months, at a cost of under $10 per run. At C-BRAIN’s current reach of over 200 research groups, this capability could release substantial research capacity, potentially exceeding one million hours annually at scale.

From Correlation to Cause

Looking ahead, C-BRAIN aims to build a multimodal biological foundation model trained on the full breadth of its data — neuroimaging, multi-omics, fluid biomarkers and longitudinal clinical measures.

By year three, it aims to discover new causal mechanisms of brain aging and Alzheimer’s disease, moving to testable scientific principles using the full scope of available research and data.

Bateman said, “AI that can think and reason has the potential to accelerate discoveries that will lead to powerful and implementable preventions and treatments for the entire population.”

## 14. Who’s really in charge at Anthropic after the IPO?

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI

Anthropic was founded in 2021 to ensure humanity can control AI and benefit from it in the long-term. As the frontier lab approaches its initial public offering, however, there’s one class of humanity that is being shut out of control: anyone who buys its stock on public markets.

Complex structures that allow founders like Elon Musk or Mark Zuckerberg to control their public companies without owning most of them are nothing new in Silicon Valley. But Anthropic’s obsession with AI safety has resulted in a truly baroque style of corporate governance that will leave common shareholders with little say over most major decisions at the AI company, including who sits on its board.

First, Anthropic is a public benefit company — a for-profit corporation legally allowed to weigh a public mission alongside profit — with the goal of promoting AI safety. That status protects the board’s ability to make decisions about AI safety that aren’t driven entirely by shareholder value without being sued by angry investors.

Right now, the board is chaired by Anthropic president Daniela Amodei and includes CEO Dario Amodei (her brother), Netflix cofounder Reed Hastings, former Microsoft and GM CFO Christopher Liddell, Novartis CEO Vas Narasimhan, and Spark Capital partner Yasmin Razavi. There is one vacancy on the board.

Second, Anthropic has allocated significant control of its board to a Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT), an independent panel meant to keep the company focused on mission to benefit humanity. The trust owns a class of stock that gives it the ability to elect and remove a majority of the company’s board, known as Class T directors. The LTBT is chaired by Neil Shah, a global health expert who was managing director at GiveWell, a nonprofit that ranks charities, and includes former Federal Reserve chair Ben Bernanke, national security expert Richard Fontaine, and Harvard physicist Christopher Stubbs. There is one vacancy on the LTBT right now. The existing members will themselves choose who fills it.

Third, Anthropic’s seven founders also jointly own a special class of stock that gives a majority of them 50.1% of shareholder voting power, allowing them to elect three members of the board of directors. If, for whatever reason, there’s no majority among the founders, only then are their shares voted in proportion with holders of common stock.

Finally, the board can put a check on the LTBT. When the LTBT nominates a “Specified” one of its four directors, or removes that director without cause (that is, without alleging misconduct), the board’s nominating committee can decline to approve the move and throw the decision to the company’s employees for a vote.

The dynamics of such a complex structure are hard to predict. We’ve already seen one notable instance where a similar structure was essentially overthrown: OpenAI’s so-called blip, when OpenAI’s board briefly fired CEO Sam Altman over concerns that he was not being candid with them. Thanks to the loyalty of the company’s employees to Altman, the board backed down, reinstated him, and a new governance structure was put in place. Anthropic’s structure seems to formalize that kind of employee power.

At Anthropic, cracks in the founders’ unified front could lead to common shareholders having more say than expected, while employees can overrule the LTBT on board seats if they back the board over the assembled luminaries.

The founders’ control won’t last forever: Ten years after the IPO, the founders can choose to end their control of the company, or their control will end automatically when fewer than three of them remain.
