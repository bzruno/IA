Open science is critical to advancing medical research. To accelerate this progress, we’ve expanded our academic partnerships, giving researchers funding and access to our AI models like Muse Glimmer, SAM3, DINOv3, and Brain2Qwertyv2. These researchers are independently adapting our models for their own investigational research. Here are a few of the projects that are benefitting from our partnership:

Training Doctors in Virtual Reality

At Louisiana State University, Dr. Peter DeBlieux and his team are using Muse Glimmer, SAM 3, DINOv3, and other models from Meta, along with Meta Quest 3 headsets to develop COPES, an experimental coaching platform that supports healthcare professionals with medical procedural training in virtual reality. The system will aim to provide adaptive guidance, real-time performance assessment, and personalized feedback across technical execution, patient communication, clinical reasoning, and decision-making. Working virtually means medical students can learn complicated procedures in situations where practicing on real patients may be risky, and virtual instruction means more medical professionals can receive high quality technical and communications training — no matter where they are.

Helping Doctors Find the Source of Epilepsy

Dr. Pierre Bourdillon at Rothschild Hospital Foundation is using AI research and models from Meta including NeuralSet, Brain2Qwertyv2, Tribev2, and DINOv3, in research projects aimed at better understanding the source of epilepsy in the brain. Currently, epilepsy can’t be directly visualized in brain images, making it difficult for clinicians to identify its source. Dr. Bourdillon is using AI to develop a tool that can enable simulations of epileptic seizures originating in different regions of the brain. This approach could one day enable doctors to select the simulation that best matches their patient’s case, allowing them to form a strong hypothesis about where the patient’s epilepsy originates. This early-stage research could improve accuracy in a range of epilepsy treatments, including surgical removal of affected brain tissue and device implantation designed to disrupt epileptic activity. The simulations also have the potential to help researchers address speech and motor restoration in patients with brain injuries.

Improving Our Understanding of Rare Diseases

At Columbia University, Dr. Wolfgang Pernice has partnered with Meta to adapt the DINO family of models to improve analysis of microscopy images of cells, helping doctors understand how rare diseases appear in cells. In conventional image analysis methods, researchers have to know what they are looking for in advance, limiting their analysis. But the adapted Cell-DINO, DINO-BoC, and FINO models generate unbiased representations of the inner workings of cells, allowing scientists to decode the complex patterns by which rare diseases manifest in human cells. This can help improve our understanding of rare diseases and accelerate drug discovery.

Each of these investigational projects highlights AI’s unique ability to fill critical research gaps. While all of the work featured here is early-stage, we look forward to the potential breakthroughs enabled by our AI models. Amazon is scooping up much of the technology and talent behind artificial intelligence startup Decart, marking the latest reverse acquihire deal by a major tech company.

In a Monday blog post, Amazon said it agreed to purchase Decart's "proprietary technology" and hire the startup's three founders, as well as their AI researchers and engineers.

The Israeli startup, founded in 2023, is best known for its software that lets developers sync their AI models across different chips, allowing them to switch hardware quickly, known as the Decart Optimization Stack.

"Every leap forward in AI starts with getting more out of every chip," Peter DeSantis, a senior vice president at Amazon who oversees foundation models, custom silicon and quantum computing development, said in a statement. "More efficient compute means better training, better reinforcement learning, faster inference, and better models for customers at a lower cost."

DeSantis added, "From our earliest work with Decart, we knew they were the kind of technologically fearless team who would help us push it even further."

Amazon has moved deeper into building custom AI chips amid insatiable demand for the technology, and as companies seek alternatives to Nvidia, the dominant provider. Amazon has positioned its Trainium and Inferentia chips as lower-cost options for AI developers.

Last month, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy said the company plans to make its Trainium chips externally available for the first time in "late in 2027."

Several outlets reported Amazon will pay $7.5 billion for Decart's assets, citing people familiar with the matter. Amazon declined to comment on the terms of the deal.

Anthropic had considered acquiring Decart and conducted due diligence, but ultimately walked away from a deal, Bloomberg reported.

Tech companies including Microsoft, Meta and Google have announced a series of deals with buzzy AI startups, which involve licensing their technology or nabbing their top talent and founders. The moves, often referred to as reverse acquihires, stop short of buying the companies outright. Amazon made two similar deals in 2024 when it brought on talent from Adept and Covariant.

The arrangements have attracted regulatory scrutiny in some cases. Amazon said Monday the Decart deal is subject to regulatory approval.

## 4. SpaceX sued by Oregon and Virginia over AI deepfake porn, Grok safety claims

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology

- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 9.5
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/oregon-virginia-spacex-grok-deepfake-lawsuits.html

Lawsuits against Elon Musk's SpaceX are stacking up, with Virginia and Oregon becoming the first states to sue the company over issues with its Grok AI image generator.

Versions of Grok and SpaceX's social network X have enabled users to generate and distribute explicit deepfakes based on real photos or videos of children and other people who did not consent, according to research by the Internet Watch Foundation, the Center for Countering Digital Hate and others.

In separate complaints submitted to state courts as of Monday morning, Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield and Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones argued that SpaceX failed to put safeguards in place to stop Grok and X users from making and sharing harmful deepfake images, especially alleged AI-generated child sexual abuse material.

The complaints also allege that SpaceX has falsely represented its AI apps and social network (Grok and X) as generally safe, which the states see as a violation of Virginia's Consumer Protection Act or Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act. The matter is particularly concerning because X allows minors 13 and older to access its content.

Rayfield and Jones both noted in their complaints that harms caused by non-consensual, sexualized images go beyond the victims depicted in the images.

"This content can cause fear, distress, and anxiety in those who are unwillingly exposed to the content," Jones wrote. Rayfield wrote that such conduct "erodes important norms regarding consent and privacy and trivializes sexual imagery, abuse, and exploitation."

In a statement emailed to CNBC by a spokesperson for SpaceXSI, and posted on the company's @Safety account on X, the company said, "When someone tries to break our safeguards and generate this content, we take appropriate measures, including actioning accounts and notifying law enforcement. Attempting to create this material is a serious crime, and the individuals who do it should be prosecuted to the fullest extent of the law. We will continue to work with the authorities via all available means to pursue these bad actors."

At an NYC Council hearing in October, a SpaceX AI policy leader, Gabriela Castillo Madrid, said the company had appropriate "safeguards" and "strict controls" in place around its AI products.

SpaceX was already facing multiple Grok- and X-related lawsuits, and government probes around the world, including by authorities in the U.K., Spain, Ireland, France and the state of California.

In private-sector lawsuits filed against SpaceX in the U.S. concerning Grok and AI-generated, explicit images, litigation is still in early stages and is tracked by The Center for Internet Technology Addiction.

When the city of Baltimore sued xAI, now owned and operated by SpaceX, the company argued that under Section 230, they were not liable for unwanted images. Section 230 is a federal law that largely shields online platforms from liability for users' content.

SpaceX also sued a user in a federal court in the Northern District of Texas for breach of contract after he allegedly used Grok to create and distribute deepfake explicit images, including depictions of minors. Lawyers for Musk's company argued that Grok should be seen as "a neutral tool, subject to user control," and that any image generation "is the result of the user's prompts and directions."

The new state suits add to legal pressure on SpaceX. Both Virginia and Oregon are seeking injunctions, civil penalties, disgorgement and legal costs, while Virginia is also seeking restitution for consumers from Musk's company.

Oregon submitted its complaint to the Multnomah County Circuit Court on Sunday evening, and Virginia submitted its complaint to the City of Richmond Circuit Court Monday morning. As of early Monday, the suits were pending the courts' acceptance.

State attorneys general have recently succeeded in compelling U.S. social media companies to change their products and pay for alleged past harms.

In August, for example, Meta settled a major federal case centered on allegations that the social media giant misrepresented the level of child mental health problems caused by its apps, Facebook and Instagram. Without admitting to wrongdoing, Meta agreed to pay states around $17 billion over a 10-year period and to make app changes that experts believe will help keep children safer online.

対象は「Claude」の「Team」「Enterprise」プランのユーザーで、GitHubのリポジトリで利用できる。エージェントをどこで稼働させるのかや、どの変更に人間の承認を必要とするかは、チーム側で設定できる。

既に一般提供しているCode Reviewは、複数の観点からPRを詳しくレビューする機能だ。見つけた問題を検証・分類した上で、PRにコメントとして投稿する。レビューの深さは、リポジトリごとに「Quick」「Standard」「Deep」の3段階から選べる。Anthropicによると、Claude Codeの開発チームでは、6月から9月にかけてマージしたPRの数が2倍以上に増え、マージ後に変更が取り消されたPRの割合は40％以上減少したという。ただし、同チームではCode Reviewを他のレビュー手段と併用している。

PR Stewardは、継続的インテグレーション（CI）の失敗やマージコンフリクト、レビューコメントに対応し、PRをマージ可能な状態に保つエージェントだ。承認やマージ自体は行わない。変更をプッシュするたびに、何を変更したかと、その変更を元に戻す方法をコメントに残す。開発者は「@claude」と書いて作業を指示でき、「@claude stop」で停止できる。

Claude Approvalsは、チームがリポジトリ内のファイルに記述した承認ポリシーに従って、PRを承認するかどうかを判断する。コードの担当者を指定する「CODEOWNERS」ファイルを拡張したような仕組みだ。ポリシーで許可すれば定型的な変更を自動承認でき、特定の変更には人間の承認を必須にできる。判断材料にはCode Reviewの指摘や、自然言語で定義した分類器を利用できる。

Anthropicは、まず承認せずに判断結果を確認する「観察専用モード」で試すことを推奨している。十分に信頼できると判断した段階で、ブランチ保護の設定によってClaude Approvalsの承認を必須にできる。

開発者がClaude Codeで新コマンド「/ship」を実行すると、Claudeがコードのプッシュ、PRの作成、レビュー、指摘事項や失敗したチェックへの対応まで進める。マージできるかどうかは、開発者の権限とリポジトリのブランチルールによって決まる。/shipは同日から、Claude Codeとデスクトップアプリで順次提供する。

管理者がリポジトリ単位でClaude Shipを有効にすれば、開発者が/shipを実行しなくても、チームのPRに自動で適用される。管理者はレビューの深さや実行タイミング、月額の利用上限を設定できる。Claude Approvalsは、接続済みリポジトリのデフォルトブランチにポリシーファイルを配置すると動作を開始し、管理者が無効化することもできる。既存のブランチ保護や必須チェックとも併用可能だ。

料金はトークン使用量に応じた従量課金で、PRの数や規模、レビューの深さや頻度、PR Stewardの作業量によって変わる。Claude Approvals自体に基本料金はかからないが、ポリシー内の分類器にはトークン使用量に応じた料金が発生する。Claudeのレビューを呼び出すポリシーでは、観察専用モードでもCode Reviewの料金が発生する。/shipの実行は通常のClaude Codeの利用として扱う。

OpenAI and Cisco are launching Shirudo, an open-source runtime that helps teams protect Linux systems while they roll out patches. In a controlled test(opens in a new window), a Codex-generated shield blocked a Java4Shell exploit attempt against a running Elasticsearch service without requiring a restart.

We’re inviting developers, security researchers, and infrastructure operators across the industry to help develop the project further. The project brings together OpenAI’s frontier AI, security and infrastructure research and Cisco’s infrastructure and security expertise to help protect the systems people rely on every day.

Testing and deploying a patch can still take days or weeks. Protecting a service in the meantime can be challenging because an exploit and a legitimate request may rely on the same Linux operation. Blocking that operation entirely could take the service offline.

Shirudo (Japanese for “shield”) is designed to help security teams navigate this tradeoff. Teams can test whether a temporary safeguard, called a shield, blocks the behavior that exposes a vulnerability while allowing legitimate requests through. Once an operator approves the shield, it can be deployed while the service continues running.

Shirudo uses eBPF, a Linux technology that allows small programs to run at specific points inside the operating system. This makes it possible to observe suspicious activity and apply targeted security protections to running systems.

OpenAI and Cisco are designing Shirudo around four core capabilities:

- Live protection: eBPF shields can block vulnerable code paths without restarting the system. Security teams can monitor how a proposed shield would affect applications before enforcing it.

- Defense at machine speed: OpenAI and Cisco are designing Shirudo to give agents programmable controls to investigate vulnerabilities, develop targeted shields, and validate their effectiveness against exploits and real workloads. Security teams control what gets deployed.

- Trusted deployment: Shields are cryptographically signed and verified before loading. Linux's eBPF verifier checks eBPF programs against its safety rules, while kernel security controls restrict unauthorized changes.

- Hardware-backed verification: Shirudo uses a Trusted Platform Module (TPM) to support a verifiable record of protections being loaded or removed. Operators can use this evidence to check whether systems meet their security requirements.

The Shirudo technical walkthrough(opens in a new window) from the Linux Plumbers Conference shows how to apply a shield and verify it on the target host(opens in a new window). The Java4Shell vulnerability is another example illustrating how a targeted shield can protect a running application while a permanent patch is being prepared. Using OpenAI's Codex, the team generated a shield that added an input check to Java's Base64 decoder and attached it directly to a running Elasticsearch process. When attempting to exploit the vulnerability, the shield rejected the malicious input, blocking the attack while Elasticsearch continued running. You can read more here(opens in a new window).

Shirudo is a first step toward infrastructure that can adapt to emerging threats while remaining reliable. We want to strengthen the operating system foundations needed to deploy these protections at scale, including hardening the kernel and core services, limiting workload permissions, and making updates and recovery safer.

Our long-term goal is to enable defenders and AI agents to quickly identify affected systems, validate protections, and deploy eBPF shields that block exploits without disrupting workloads. Applications should run with only the permissions they need, governed by policies that are automatically generated and maintained.

We will combine frontier AI with security and infrastructure research to discover vulnerabilities and test defenses against realistic attacks. We aim to make it easier for operators to detect and contain compromises, prevent attackers from moving across systems, and restore services to a verified, known-good state. Hardware-backed attestation and independent network controls provide additional assurance even if individual systems are compromised.

OpenAI and Cisco are open-sourcing Shirudo(opens in a new window) so developers, security researchers, and infrastructure operators can inspect the code, test protections, and contribute to its development. Shirudo is licensed under BSD 2-Clause, with its BPF programs dual-licensed under BSD and GPL.

Our goal is to make tested shields available alongside vulnerability disclosures, giving operators a way to evaluate and deploy temporary protections while preparing permanent patches.

## 12. Supertake raises $20M to let AI agents turn your market predictions into stock portfolios

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI

AI investing startup Supertake has an interesting premise: What if your “takes” on the market could make you money without you having to pick stocks yourself? The service lets people turn their ideas into real investment portfolios, using frontier AI models and trading agents.

Now, the company has raised $20 million in seed funding to grow its business. The round was led by venture firm Adjacent (Nico Wittenborn), with participation from Coinbase Ventures and Union Square Ventures (USV). Numerous angel investors also joined, including Nat Friedman, who’s currently head of product at Meta Superintelligence Labs; Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch; renowned product manager Nikita Bier; investor Scott Belsky; and Nemil Dalal, a former Coinbase exec who is now a visiting partner at Y Combinator.

Michael Mignano originally incubated the startup at USV. He is the co-founder and former CEO of the podcasting platform Anchor, which Spotify acquired in 2019 for over $150 million. After leading Spotify’s podcasting business as “Head of Talk” for a few years, he joined the team at Lightspeed as a venture partner, betting on AI companies like Suno and Granola. He also co-founded Oboe, an AI education startup, and joined USV as a general partner in April.

“We really believed there was an opportunity to make investing more democratized…and give everyone and anyone the ability to invest in their own insights and beliefs and ideas,” Mignano said. While he will continue in his role as founder and executive chairman, he’s now brought in a team to run the company day to day.

The team includes co-founders Lincoln Murr (CEO) and John Peterson (CTO). Both joined from Coinbase, where, Mignano notes, they were the heads of AI product and engineering, leading launches like Coinbase advisor, Coinbase for agents, agentic wallets, and others.

The startup arrives as AI continues to make inroads into financial services, but allowing AI to take over people’s actual investments requires a new level of trust. Supertake is betting that people will ultimately be willing to take that leap, given recent improvements in frontier AI models.

Currently, Supertake works with Coinbase and Robinhood, and the company intends to expand to more brokerages over time. The idea is that you can explain, in broad, natural language, what you believe about how the world is going to change, and Supertake’s AI will iterate with you back and forth to turn that idea into a “take” that’s investable.

Under the hood, the startup is leveraging frontier AI technology and tools, combined with its own proprietary software that directs and limits what the AI can do.

Supertake’s users’ ideas are tracked on a leaderboard on the site, so others who agree can invest alongside them. For instance, there’s currently an idea on the leaderboard about looksmaxxing and longevity becoming more of a trend, where the portfolio includes certain pharma and cosmetic companies that could potentially benefit.

For now, these portfolios consist of stock picks, but the team wants to expand the service over time to handle other types of transactions, like shorts, derivatives, perpetuals, long exposure, or even prediction markets — the latter handled through partnerships with companies in that space.

Despite these “takes” seeming like bets on what could happen next in a given market, Murr stresses that this doesn’t make Supertake itself a prediction market.

“We think it’s a novel and very much improved way to create what was previously considered the robo-advisor experience,” he says. “When that came out, it had this amazing vision and direction of allowing people to have digital asset management. We think now is actually the time for that, with AI agents being so widely proliferated.”

“I don’t think this is necessarily a prediction market by any means. The idea of natural language translation into an underlying set of assets feels different in that regard,” he says.

Still, the startup could wade into short-term predictions if a user’s take involves something they believe could happen in the near future, like who might win an election.

“We’re primarily focused on the longer, medium-term holdings today. But that being said, we don’t want to preclude people from building out very hyper short-term strategies that might exist,” Murr adds.

To address people’s concerns over AI managing their money, Supertake’s team says it’s implementing certain guardrails and spend limits. For instance, the AI has no ability to deposit or withdraw funds on a user’s behalf without their explicit consent. There are also drift limits in place so the portfolio stays balanced, plus allow and deny lists (meaning which assets the AI may and may not trade), real-time monitoring for anomalous activity, tools to set trade size limits, and a restriction that keeps agents from seeing the user’s brokerage credentials.

“We view it as somewhat analogous to self-driving cars, actually, where there is a spectrum here of both trust and technological innovations that are happening,” says Murr. “Where a few years ago you may not feel comfortable getting in a car that has lane assist, but today you get in a Waymo, and it’s like nothing. And we think that there’s a very similar progression happening with the agentic finance space, and we’re far further along in the technological spectrum than people think.”

Access to Supertake is still behind a waitlist while in beta, but the founders said they’ve put their money where their mouths are. Murr and Peterson are letting it manage “tens of thousands” and “thousands” of dollars, respectively.

Mignano, meanwhile, says he has Supertake managing “hundreds of thousands” of dollars.

With the new funding, which follows a $2 million pre-seed round, Supertake aims to expand its team in areas like engineering, design, growth marketing, and others. Mignano and Murr are in New York, while Peterson is in the Austin area but commutes to New York at times. The team says it hopes to figure out a hybrid work model as it grows.

## 15. States Sue SpaceX Over Grok Deepfakes

- **Fonte:** Wired AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-23 08:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.5
- **Link:** https://www.wired.com/story/states-sue-spacex-over-grok-deepfakes/

The attorneys general of Oregon and Virginia are suing Elon Musk’s SpaceX, alleging that the company violated consumer protection laws by lying to users about both the safety of its generative AI tools and how seriously the company took stopping child sexual abuse material (CSAM) and nonconsensual intimate imagery from proliferating on its platforms. The suits follow a slew of other lawsuits against Musk’s companies around similar issues, including from the city of Baltimore and teens in Tennessee.

The attorneys general are each filing their own complaints in their respective state courts—Virginia in the circuit court for the city of Richmond, and Oregon in Multnomah County circuit court. Both suits name SpaceX and X Corp., the SpaceX subsidiary responsible for the social media platform X. SpaceX acquired xAI, the Musk startup and parent company of X Corp., in February. In doing so it also took ownership of Grok, which is at the center of the allegations in the suit.

Last winter, the Grok chatbot made headlines after users on X started prompting it en masse to generate sexualized photos of women and minors. Even more disturbing AI-generated videos were later found on Grok’s website, including those showing female celebrities engaging in sexual activities.

The states allege that Grok’s creators ignored industry safety standards and intentionally implemented weak safeguards in an attempt to entice more people to use its “lagging product.” In all, as Virginia alleged in its complaint, the defendants saw “a market opportunity for more profits” in the generation of sexualized images of real people, including children, and “intentionally released a product that inundated their platforms with CSAM.”

For example, the states say that Grok wasn’t subjected to enough third-party evaluations before releasing new models and that Grok’s safety prompts included instructions to “assume good intent” and that the terms “teenage” and “girl” do “not necessarily imply underage.” They also allege that tens of thousands of reports were submitted without key information from xAI to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children’s CyberTipline, preventing the Center from being able to act on them 90 percent of the time.

In the complaints, the states say that Musk leaned into Grok’s “spicy mode” as a way to differentiate the model from its competitors and that this worked. They note, for example, that during the same time period that Grok’s ability to “undress” people was sparking international outrage, Musk posted about how Grok was “hitting #1 on the App Store in one country after another!” and discussing the chatbot’s “heavy usage growth.”

“It’s no surprise that Grok and X became a haven for perpetrators using AI to create and share child sexual abuse material, because the lack of needed safety measures was considered a feature, not a bug,” says Oregon attorney general Dan Rayfield in a statement.

Oregon and Virginia each filed their own “separate but similar actions” on Monday, but say they’ve been working in collaboration with each other. Both states retained Platkin LLP, a private law firm run by former New Jersey attorney general Matthew Platkin, to help litigate their cases.

Virginia attorney general Jay Jones says that both the collaboration and the decision to work with an outside firm came from trying to make the most of what they had. “Our offices are strapped. I wish I had all the resources in the world,” says Jones. “And so we oftentimes do partner with folks on the outside to make sure that we’re pursuing the matters that are in the best interest of our constituencies.”

“I have a deep worry now into the future with this case and many others that we actually don’t have the appropriate tools to deal with trillion-dollar corporations,” says Jones. “This is just a line item on a budget as the cost of doing business, right? Dealing with, you know, AG Jones and AG Rayfield. I think it’s going to be real interesting to see how these cases go, because it’s not just this, there’s a lot of other things that we’re looking at. How is this going to be grappled with as a society into the future?”

In its complaint, Virginia says that shortly before filing, its investigators tested Grok and found its image-generation safeguards were not only “inconsistent and ineffective” but also taught users how to bypass moderation measures by explaining which words triggered its filters. When asked, the chatbot also helped state investigators draft prompts designed to avoid moderation and then carried out those prompts on request.

“At no point,” Virginia’s complaint says, “did Grok ask whether (a) the image uploaded was of a real person, or (b) whether the user had the person’s consent to use or modify the photo.”

Both Virginia and Oregon say that this contradicts assurances made by a SpaceX official during a New York City Council hearing this October.

The attorneys general are seeking civil penalties and attorneys’ fees, but also pointed to Meta’s settling—without admitting wrongdoing—this summer with 51 US states and territories over alleged failures to protect minors as an example of how enforcers can demand that companies make sweeping product changes to resolve consumer protection cases.

“They profited off the backs of children with these types of images to get a strategic advantage, and it worked. You don’t get to benefit from that type of stuff; there has to be an associated fee and penalty just like anything else,” says Dan Rayfield.

Adds Jones, “Your size and your scope shouldn’t excuse the fact that you have to play by the rules.”

In response to a request for comment, SpaceX directed WIRED to a post on X’s official safety account. “Protecting children is non-negotiable,” the account wrote. “It has no place on X or in Grok, and neither do explicit images of real people made without their consent.”

The post further went on to say that Grok’s designers continue “testing and adjusting” protections designed to refuse requests to generate explicit images of real people or minors. SpaceX’s subsidiary, xAI, has previously argued in court that it takes violations of its terms of service seriously, touting a lawsuit it filed against a South Carolina user for alleged breach of contract after he was arrested for possessing CSAM, including some allegedly generated using Grok—one of several similar lawsuits the company has filed. Both xAI’s lawsuit against the individual and his criminal case are still ongoing.
