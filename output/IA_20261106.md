“The result is a model that is both stronger at coding and easier to use inside real agent loops,” the company said.

The open-weight race

Thinking Machines said it will release the model’s weights next week, a practice that is becoming customary. Given the heightened security concerns around AI in general, AI companies spend a few weeks testing the to-be-open model with cybersecurity teams and safety reviewers before releasing the weights, rather than leading with the weights out of the gate.

French AI lab Mistral recently released its Le Chonk model via the same staggered process — it now keeps pace with OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Terra in coding abilities, which is rare for western-made open models.

## 4. ToyBlocks: A controlled world for robot learning and evaluation

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-06 11:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.5
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/toyblocks

💻 Code | 📊 Data | 🤖 Models | 📖 Documentation

Robots are learning to fold laundry, sort parts, and handle unfamiliar objects. But those demonstrations are often rather narrow and don't explain what a robot has learned or why it fails.

To study a complex system, scientists often begin with a small, controlled environment, such as a petri dish or terrarium, where they can vary conditions and observe the effects. Robotics needs environments that make this kind of investigation possible—researchers should be able to change the training data or model design, repeat a test, and trace the cause of a failure.

Today we're introducing a framework for creating these environments; we call them FoundationWorlds. Each pairs a controlled, simulated world targeted at studying robotics in a specific environment with tools for automatically generating tasks and training demonstrations, as well as metrics for measuring a robot's behavior over time. Together, they provide a setting for studying questions such as how models improve as more training data becomes available, and how to best combine high-level reasoning with precise physical control.

Our first foundation world is ToyBlocks: an open simulation framework built around a robot arm and blocks on a tabletop. The setting is deliberately simple, but the robot must still reason about how the blocks interact and how to arrange them while pushing, flipping, and stacking.

ToyBlocks automatically generates demonstrations, which are recordings of a robot completing tasks in simulation, without requiring time-consuming teleoperation, in which a person remotely controls the robot. Researchers can easily vary these training experiences. For example, a team could generate more pushing or flipping demonstrations, then evaluate performance on tasks kept out of training.

Starting with ToyBlocks as our first FoundationWorld, we aim to extend the approach to increasingly complex environments.

Building on our earlier work in open robot learning, including MolmoAct 2, MolmoBot, and MolmoSpaces, ToyBlocks gives researchers a way to study robotics questions in a deliberately constrained domain.

As a FoundationWorld, ToyBlocks is designed around four requirements: (1) realistic physics and appearance, (2) automatic generation of tasks and demonstrations at scale, (3) challenges that combine reasoning with dexterity, and (4) broad coverage of the tasks possible within a domain.

Open-ended domains require modeling a wide range of objects and interactions, making it infeasible to fulfill all these requirements. ToyBlocks’ narrow scope, on the other hand, makes it possible to fulfill them all.

Within ToyBlocks' smaller setting, researchers can work toward investigating how a robot can learn to perform any reasonable task while being confident that the simulated arm and blocks behave very similarly to their physical counterparts.

A physical robot gripper above wooden blocks in the ToyBlocks setup.

ToyBlocks is not just about picking and stacking blocks: pictures and letters on the blocks' faces allow for searching, sorting, and arranging tasks. A robot may need to turn a block to see a hidden face before deciding where it belongs. Domain randomization – varying block size, table height, and other physical properties from scene to scene – tests how well models generalize as the geometry changes.

ToyBlocks automatically generates skills (behaviors for short tasks) and task demonstrations that can be used to train models or adapt previously trained models through additional training (fine-tuning). High-level reasoning models decide what to do; low-level action models control the robot’s movements. The framework can also be used to easily design experiments and validate hypotheses.

ToyBlocks uses reinforcement learning (RL) and motion planning, which computes how the robot should move, to generate skills for short-horizon tasks, which involve brief sequences of actions. Skill training takes place in Isaac Lab, Nvidia's open-source framework for robot learning in simulation. Through trial and error, these policies – models that choose the robot’s actions – learn non-prehensile skills such as pushing a block across the table or flipping it onto another face without picking it up. Skill generation can draw on privileged information – values the simulator can supply directly but a real robot cannot, such as a block's exact position and orientation – instead of estimating it from camera images. This enables the automatic generation of skills that would require teleoperation in the real world.

To generate demonstration data, a task-and-motion planner (TAMP) automatically selects and sequences the skills needed to solve a task and plans the robot’s movements between them. Running each plan in simulation produces a dataset of recorded demonstrations along with labels describing which task or skill the robot performs. ToyBlocks scales data generation through generating and executing hundreds of such plans in parallel on a single graphics processing unit (GPU), reaching up to 24 times real-time performance.

Acting from sensor observations adds a perception problem: a model has to estimate a block’s pose – its position and orientation – which the simulator could supply directly. To reflect real-world conditions, ToyBlocks trains models that map raw sensor observations to actions.

Execution of Molmo 2 (a high-level model) and ManiFlow (a low-level model) trained in ToyBlocks. Molmo 2 selects skills such as rotation and flipping, indicating the relevant block via a pointing interface, while ManiFlow controls the simulated arm to execute the commanded skills.

ToyBlocks' controllable data generator lets users create training mixtures that emphasize particular skills or fill gaps in existing datasets. Researchers can study how much data a model needs and which experiences help it generalize to unseen tasks. For example, they could test whether adding rotation and pick-and-place demonstrations improves flipping, or vary the balance of examples inside and outside a defined workspace region—evaluating their choices on tasks kept out of training. Importantly, evaluation of trained models generates metrics beyond success rates; it provides information such as execution speeds, forces exerted on objects, and task progress measured via the same specifications used by the planning module.

A webpage interface lets users connect Claude – or another frontier model – and use natural language to instruct the robot or try tasks, offering a way to explore how a model plans and acts without needing robotics expertise.

The evaluations below use ToyBlocks to study how training data and methods affect performance, spatial reasoning, generalization to new letters and block appearances, multi-step planning, transfer to physical robots, and frontier models that act through tools. Users can easily design their own tasks and measures to investigate other questions—for example, whether a model performs better when it selects objects by pointing or by issuing text commands such as "pick the yellow block."

Low-level action models, such as vision-language-action models (VLAs), map language instructions and visual observations to robot actions.

The premise of VLAs is that, by pretraining on large-scale datasets, they can rapidly generalize to different scenarios or a specific workspace. However, evidence on how well these action models generalize remains conflicting. Some studies report that these action models are brittle to changes in inputs, such as different object layouts and lighting, whereas others show that VLAs generalize well. We argue that these studies often rely on real-world datasets with limited flexibility in spatial variations. ToyBlocks enables us to test how well action models generalize in a simple, easily reproducible setting with meaningful task-space diversity.

We design generalization tests that vary the skill sequences needed to complete an instruction, the letters or colors on the blocks, and the number of blocks in a scene. A stacking study can withhold four-block stacks from training, then test whether a model can combine familiar movements to build them. Workspace studies distinguish performance at new positions within a training region from performance beyond its boundaries, where the model has to extrapolate. We illustrate the study setup and findings below.

We consider four tasks: Stack, Unstack, Flip, and Rotate. For these tasks, we study interpolation (new configurations within the training range) and extrapolation (configurations beyond that range). For example, we evaluate generalization by training on scenarios where objects spawn only in the left region (green) and testing on the right region (orange), or by training on stack heights of 2 and 5 blocks and testing on heights 3 and 4.

Spatial generalization studies for VLA models (results based on ManiFlow, π0.5, and MolmoAct 2). Despite large-scale pretraining, these popular action models generalize poorly in the initial results. We improve generalization and analyze the contribution of each technique. The chart’s LoRA label stands for low-rank adaptation, a method for fine-tuning a model with a small set of trainable weight updates. We believe action model generalization remains far from solved; ToyBlocks provides a valuable testbed for systematically exploring generalization techniques.

Long-horizon tasks, which require many steps, allow us to measure how well models can plan a sequence of actions to solve them. Our framework enables us to create such long-horizon tasks, and create oracles – reference components that provide correct plans or actions – at both the high and low levels. This allows us to isolate whether the high-level planning or low-level execution failed when a model fails at a task. An example long-horizon task might be to spell “Ai2” with a block stack; because the letters may not be immediately visible, the agent must first choose actions that reveal information, and then plan and execute a sequence of actions to build the stack.

We evaluate hierarchical models with explicit high-level planning and low-level execution models. In this instance, we choose Molmo 2, a vision-language model (VLM), as the high-level planner, and ManiFlow as the low-level policy. Molmo 2 chooses the skill to execute and the block to execute it on, while the low-level model executes the instruction. We train low-level models such as ManiFlow to pause after completing the execution and query the high-level model for the next instruction. We evaluate the model’s planning abilities on three scenarios of increasing difficulty. The easiest scenario initializes with each character upright and facing the camera. The medium scenario has characters that may not be upright, and the hardest scenario contains characters that are not visible at the start of the episode.

Success rates on three-letter block-spelling tasks using Molmo 2 as the high-level reasoning model and either ManiFlow or a motion planner for low-level manipulation. An example task is “Stack Ai2”. Molmo 2 and ManiFlow are trained on ToyBlocks and evaluated across three difficulty settings and two generalization conditions.

Our experiments with agentic systems test how frontier models plan and act through tool calls. We study three settings using Claude Opus 5.5. In the search-and-spell setting, we expose ToyBlocks skills through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), a standard for connecting AI applications to tools. The agent sees the scene through camera images, calls skills such as pick, flip, or rotate by pointing at a pixel, and decides when to search, build, or declare the task complete or impossible. The same MCP tools can execute either the scripted skills that generate ToyBlocks demonstrations (motion-planned pick and place, plus RL-based rotate and flip) or learned ManiFlow skills. ToyBlocks includes the framework behind these experiments, which also powers the natural-language web interface described earlier.

A frontier model (Claude Fable 5.1) controls a robot in a ToyBlocks world to search for and stack blocks into a tower spelling “Ai2.” During the episode, the agent muses, “I need to figure out whether pick-and-place naturally straightens orientation or if I need to correct it manually… I have 63 calls left, plenty to run a controlled test” (0:06). The agent later hypothesizes: “…maybe the gripper’s yaw is radial—pointing from the robot base toward the block” (0:15) and eventually confirms this hypothesis (0:26) before constructing the three-block tower, but the ‘A’ is sideways (1:11). Surprisingly, current frontier models often make similar perceptual errors (see the next figure), despite their impressive planning, spatial reasoning, and search capabilities.

For classical planning, the agent (our system using Claude Opus 5.5 to plan and choose actions) solves all 30 of a group of block-stacking tasks similar to ones that frontier models struggled with a few years ago, using text descriptions. It also solves 30 out of 30 while repeatedly observing camera images and choosing its next action in a closed loop.

In the search-and-spell setting, the agent had to generate stacks spelling out three-letter words. It had to find the blocks with the corresponding letters among up to eight blocks in the scene. Importantly, 50% of the scenes did not have blocks with all the required letters, rendering the task infeasible. In this case, we required the agent to state that the task is infeasible. We found that in this experiment, the agent was able to successfully solve or correctly declare infeasible 63% of the tasks. Errors were mostly caused by the agent confusing letters with look-alike letters (13%) or choosing an incorrect orientation for a letter in a stack (15%).

Our results also show that the agent was able to solve the task more efficiently when the blocks had semantic regularities, or patterns, taking advantage of such patterns to reason about occluded faces. For instance, in most of the infeasible scenes, when the blocks had no patterns, the agent correctly looked at all the blocks’ faces before declaring infeasibility, whereas it was able to declare infeasibility significantly faster when the blocks had semantic patterns.

Our third experimental setting asks whether an agent can learn how its skills behave, using experiments as a scientist would. When a skill behaves unexpectedly, the agent forms a hypothesis, designs a small test, and revises its understanding from the result. We expand on this idea by having an agent run its own experiments and write its own skill descriptions based on what it finds. These learned descriptions raise success rates from 24% with the default descriptions to 63%, outperforming the 36% achieved with descriptions written by a human expert.

Share of letter-block faces the agent had exposed by the end of each “search and spell” episode (bars averaged over 20 episodes; one marker per episode, styled by outcome). Action-conditioned world models are trained to predict how a scene changes in response to a robot’s actions. Using them as an alternative to model-based simulators for training and evaluating robot policies is an active area of research. How well they serve this purpose depends on how accurately they predict physical interactions, which is hard to measure without knowing the true state of the scene. ToyBlocks provides this ground truth – the actual simulated scene state – enabling highly controlled tests of how recently developed world models perform in low-level object manipulation. Here, the arm’s end effector is its gripper.

Specifically, we tested whether action-conditioned world models are capable of predicting the motions of blocks or the outcomes of skill executions after being trained on example executions. To do so, we chose four different skills (pick-and-place, flip, rotate, and a mixed task) and generated 900 trajectories – sequences of robot states and actions over time – for each skill. 50% of these trajectories were successful demonstrations of the skill, and 50% were unsuccessful because of small changes to spatial configurations. With equal numbers of successful and unsuccessful executions, a model that always predicts success would be correct only half the time. The models were fine-tuned on these demonstrations and then tested on their ability to predict the outcome of action sequences of the same skills.

We find that Wan 2.2 predicts block motion and task outcomes most accurately. Astra tracks the gripper well but struggles to simulate contact. LeWM is fast and more accurate over short interaction segments, though it drifts over full trajectories. In additional experiments, we also showed that models that score higher on pose and success prediction also produce better training data: adding 100 model-generated trajectories (rollouts) from the best video-generation model raises a policy’s success in an unseen region from 19% to 78%.

We tested three types of models: a video prediction model with 5 billion parameters (Wan 2.2), an agentic model that created its own physics simulator tuned to match the demonstration data (Astra), and a latent world model (LeWM). During testing, each trained model has to predict future images, simulator states, or latent states (learned internal representations), respectively, based on an initial image and a sequence of actions. For full trajectories, models use the initial image as the only observed image, along with the action sequence. For short interaction segments – a single pick, place, flip, or rotate – models start from the real image immediately before that interaction. Full trajectories show how errors accumulate over long horizons, while interaction segments isolate a single interaction, such as the gripper making contact with a block, and show how well the model predicts it. To compare models fairly, we probe each model’s predictions, reading out where it expects the blocks and gripper to be at different points in the trajectory and whether it expects the task to succeed at the end.

Sim-to-real evaluations test policies trained in simulation on physical robots performing block manipulation tasks. By comparing simulation and real-world execution, we assess whether behaviors and failure patterns observed in simulation provide meaningful indications of real-world performance. For example, researchers can evaluate individual skills and their composition in longer-horizon tasks to identify where failures arise and whether these patterns carry over to physical deployments.

## 7. Ramp data shows AI spending stalling just as Anthropic nears its IPO

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-06 12:54
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.5
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/ramp-data-shows-ai-spending-stalling-just-as-anthropic-nears-its-ipo/

Companies are using more AI than ever, but they’re not spending more on it.

A new report from Ramp shows AI spending flatlined over late summer even as token volumes rose, a sign that price competition and savvier customers may be capping revenue for OpenAI and Anthropic. According to Ramp’s data, token spend only grew 1.5% across August and September. To compare, in July, it grew 42% from the previous month.

As Anthropic gears up for its IPO, the data suggests investors might be wrong to assume AI companies will keep growing at breakneck speed.

“Almost the entire spend on AI is from venture-backed, high-growth tech companies,” Ramp’s lead economist Ara Kharazian told TechCrunch. “That’s fine as long as AI spend continues to rise, but if it starts to slow down because of something in the market or some other factor, they will all start to slow down simultaneously, and that could lead to other cascading effects.”

Kharazian said he’s already seeing evidence that the top 1% of firms — the most “AI-pilled” — have stuttered slightly in their spend in August and September, coming up just below the $10,000 per-employee they were hitting at their summer peaks. A slowdown among that cohort is “sufficient to slow down revenue entirely, despite the fact that we’re seeing rising volumes,” he said.

Ramp data shows growth in spending among median and top 10%, but it’s not enough to make up the difference. Median firms spend $12-$15 per employee per month, and the top 10% spend $600-$1,000.

“If you look at the use cases today, it’s not necessarily clear they will get up to that $10,000 per employee per month mark that the 1% is at,” Kharazian said. While there’s plenty of demand for AI coding agents in industries like software and finance, “it’s not necessarily true that a doctor’s office or a car dealership will need to spend that much on AI.”

“The era of hypergrowth may be over, and now that we know there may not be as much juice to squeeze out of the top 1%,” he continued.

October data did see a re-acceleration, with token spend up 8.9% from September 23 to October 23, but Kharazian said the headwinds on spend growth are strong.

Kharazian (who has published a series of blogs called “Cracks in the AI Thesis”) said the slowdown isn’t due to a demand problem. Again, AI volumes are up. Instead he points to the competitive nature of the marketplace.

“OpenAI and Anthropic price cuts have…just made it cheaper to use AI,” he said. In September, OpenAI cut API prices in half for GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna. Answering the price war, Anthropic has also made significant reductions to pricing for Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 — a decision that the company said in a recent filing slashed revenue growth by about $600 million. Both companies have also been offering enterprise discounts that are intended to lock-in spend, but in reality are hitting their revenue targets.

Kharazian said this dynamic means it has very much become a buyer’s market.

Subsidizing use of expensive technology in order to lock in customers has always been a part of the Big Tech playbook. Just recall how cheap it used to be to take an Uber or Lyft ride, and now that there are few other ride-hail players, the cost of going from A to B has risen significantly. Kharazian says those dynamics don’t work in the AI world because there’s just too much competition.

“Meta and Nvidia are also introducing enterprise offerings with pretty cheap models or open source models,” Kharazian said. He also pointed to the range of other open model companies from China and the United States, as well as a new company called Jev — a lightweight tool for choosing between a few predetermined outputs — that is increasingly among the top performers on Ramp.

“For a lot of AI use cases, you just need a simple Yes/No,” Kharazian said. “So you take your already cheap models, you shift them over to Jev, and now your already cheap stuff gets even cheaper.”

The ability to complete workloads at a much cheaper cost is another factor driving the spending slowdown. That’s partly due to the discounts from AI companies, but also because companies are just getting better at being thrifty.

“As you get really advanced in using AI, you get better at figuring out how to control it,” Kharazian said. “You’re more likely to use defaults that prevent usage of very expensive models for long-running tasks. You’re more likely to impose some budgets on employees. You’re more likely to offload some of the spend from standard and light models to even cheaper light models via routers.”

Notion co-founder Akshay Kothari recently posted internal data that matched Ramp’s insights. As he succinctly put it: “tokens go brrr. costs go ahhh.”

“A lot of the assumptions that we had about the trajectory of AI spend are starting to break down,” Kharazian said. “You can use more AI, but use it for less. That’s great for the customers…but it’s not necessarily great for the model companies. And it does introduce new questions around their revenue trajectories.”

Bloomberg reported Friday that Anthropic’s potential valuation sank from the $2 trillion estimated in early October to just $1.2 to $1.3 trillion. The company is also reportedly expected to receive a high “junk” rating from credit agencies. OpenAI also lowered its proposed valuation to $1.25 trillion from $1.4 trillion and slashed its latest fundraising goals by about $10 billion.

Kharazian says Ramp’s dataset likely informs many investor concerns. There is, however, one potential brightside for spending trajectories, albeit a speculative one.

Much of the thesis for hypergrowth spending is that AI will undoubtedly introduce new use cases that will accelerate things. AI coding emerged just a year and a half ago as the most commercially viable product that has led Anthropic and OpenAI to becoming trillion-dollar companies. The hope among investors is that there will be another revolution that will drive further spend.

Some think personal agents will be the next coding agents. OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Meta have all released their own personal agents, alongside startups like Instinct. Kharazian is skeptical, though, that personal agents, which are “not as economically productive as coding,” will provide the boost the industry needs for nonstop growth.

“The projection is that there will be new products being introduced that will drive further growth,” Kharazian said. “But based on what we can observe today and the products available and what is commercially advanced, AI spend has started to slow down.”

Queda atinge centros de dados, energia e big techs

## 14. Chinese AI lab Moonshot turned to Europe for Nvidia Blackwell access amid U.S. China curbs, sources tell CNBC

- **Fonte:** CNBC Technology
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-06 07:28
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/05/moonshot-ai-nvidia-chips-europe-china.html

Moonshot AI, a Chinese artificial intelligence lab that the White House accused of accessing Nvidia advanced chips remotely in East Asia, has sought access to the chips through data centers in Europe, sources have told CNBC.

Deals have been discussed this year for Moonshot to rent capacity from neoclouds hosting Nvidia graphics processing units, or GPUs, at facilities in Europe, four people familiar with the matter said. They asked not to be named because they were discussing private information.

Nvidia's effective monopoly over the most powerful chips has made U.S. export controls a key tool in Washington's effort to preserve its advantage over China in the AI race. American AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic are locked in a training and pricing battle with Chinese developers, including Moonshot AI, which makes the Kimi family of models.

Moonshot was looking to lease capacity powered by Nvidia's Blackwell systems, advanced chips currently subject to those export controls which effectively ban their import into China, three of the sources told CNBC.

In July, the White House said the Chinese AI lab had accessed Nvidia's Blackwell chips via a facility in Thailand.

U.S. export controls restrict the shipment of Nvidia's most advanced chips to China, but remote access via overseas data centers offers a loophole. Current law does not give regulators broad authority to control companies' access to those chips through cloud services hosted overseas.

Moonshot, Nvidia, the White House, the U.S. Department of Commerce, China's embassy in the U.K. and its Ministry of Foreign Affairs did not respond to a request for comment.

Chinese developers have made notable capability gains in the past six months and greater access to Nvidia chips could put even more pressure on their American rivals.

To lease Nvidia computing power in Europe, a Chinese AI company would typically rent capacity from a non-Chinese neocloud or data center operator that can purchase advanced chips.

Moonshot was interested in leasing capacity on clusters for inference, which refers to serving models to end users, in Europe, two of the sources said. One of the sources said they were also aware of interest in clusters for training.

Talks for arrangements that would see Moonshot rent access to clusters of 8,000 or more Blackwell GPUs hosted in the region have taken place, one source said. They described the Chinese AI lab as "very aggressive" in its pursuit of more computing power.

One of the sources told CNBC that talks on securing Moonshot compute in Europe had taken place since the White House said it had done so via Thailand. Moonshot did not comment at the time.

Founded in 2023, Beijing-based Moonshot AI is one of China's leading model builders. It released a new model in July that Moonshot said closed the gap with leading systems built by Anthropic and OpenAI.

The model saw significant uptake as a cheaper alternative to U.S. offerings. Chinese AI models have surged in global adoption in 2026, amid capability gains and lower costs than U.S. rivals.

Around the time Kimi K3 was released, Moonshot said demand had stretched its capacity, and it temporarily paused new subscriptions to prioritize compute for current members. "We're adding capacity as fast as we can and will reopen new subscription spots in batches," the company said in a LinkedIn post.

Leasing AI compute is a booming industry, with customers, or offtakers, typically taking capacity for a fee and paying an upfront cost to secure the deal, which is a percentage of the lifetime contract value.

One source said it had been proposed that Moonshot pay 30% to 50% more upfront than a non-Chinese offtaker would.

Arrangements with Chinese customers can be more lucrative upfront, as neoclouds and financing firms look to hedge against a potential future ban of remote access agreements.

In Washington, lawmakers have proposed the Remote Access Security Act (RASA) to expand U.S. export controls to include the cloud-based access of critical hardware and software. It passed the House of Representatives in January but has not passed the Senate.

The Financial Times reported on Tuesday that the chip giant told cloud providers selling access to their chips that they must sell at least 51% of services to non-Chinese customers.

Asked about the reports earlier this week, an Nvidia spokesperson said: "America cannot win the AI race unless America's AI infrastructure wins worldwide — if the world's clouds rely on foreign-made chips, the model and application layers will be foreign-built as well."

Nvidia said in August that Washington had licensed some sales of its H200 AI chips, an older generation, but that "such sales were restricted" by the Chinese government, which has sought to boost domestic chipmakers by limiting access to Nvidia chips.
