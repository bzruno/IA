## 5. A Closer Look at Phonon TTS. Launching High-Quality On-Device Speech That Tops the Benchmarks.

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-04 21:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 8.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/phonon-tts

Two weeks ago, we opened up Phonon, our small text-to-speech model that runs on your own device. It needs no server and no network, and what you type stays with you.

We've been very happy with the reception so far. So here's the story of the launch: why we built Phonon, what it can do, the demos that came with it, and how it did on Hugging Face's Open TTS Leaderboard.

Most voice AI lives in the cloud: every sentence makes a round trip to a server. Phonon runs on your own device instead. Your text stays there and the voice works without a network. We’ve also opened the weights. Phonon 31m is Apache 2.0, so you can use it for free, even in a commercial product. The other models are CC BY-NC, so they’re free for non-commercial projects. If you’re building something commercial with those, pricing is based on your number of users, not how much speech you generate.

Getting a good voice to run on a small device has always been hard. You could make the model small, but then it didn’t sound great. Or you could make it sound great, but then it wouldn’t fit. We addressed both.

We announced Phonon in April and spent the next six months building on it with partners and early users. Their feedback helped us to shape this release. The models got smaller, and we added more languages and support for custom voices through voice cloning and voice design.

Phonon is based on the Pocket TTS lineage and as such uses flow matching to generate the audio which is very different from the modeling techniques used in other TTS systems. This is what allowed us to bring this level of quality to such a small model but it created new challenges while training the models. The training was initially numerically unstable and required several tricks to produce quality results on all five languages.

Phonon comes in two sizes, at about 65 MB and 75 MB. Together they speak English, French, German, Spanish and Portuguese. There are more than 100 built-in voices, and you can create your own in Gradium Studio. Phonon also handles the important things that most other TTS systems often stumble on, like phone numbers, dates and codes.

It runs on phones, laptops, single-board computers and in a browser tab, so you can hear it right now. Phonon 31m speaks English and is Apache 2.0, so you can build on it freely, including for commercial products. Phonon 40m covers all five languages under a non-commercial license.

The launch came with demos from friends and from our own team. On an Apple Watch with Wi-Fi off, Phonon talks you through a coffee brew step by step. On an iPhone with no signal, it counts your reps and keeps you going. A robot called Lamp, from Autonomous, wears a sorting hat and talks in character. In a browser, an offline dino runner becomes a radio show hosted by Rex. There's also a sci-fi game voiced on the player's own machine, and Proust read aloud page by page.

The Hugging Face team built the Transformers integration, so developers could use Phonon in Transformers from day one.

Developers have started building on it too. An ExecuTorch conversion runs Phonon on a visitor's GPU in the browser, Micdrop brings it to Node.js apps, and Spaces like smallest-s2s build on it.

Phonon was downloaded more than 200,000 times in its first two weeks, and it’s still trending on Hugging Face.

The Open TTS Leaderboard is a very new project from Hugging Face, and we love it. With more than 9,000 TTS models on the Hugging Face Hub, the leaderboard gives developers one place to compare open models and hear them side by side. Across 23 languages, models are evaluated on intelligibility (word error rate of the outputs), speed (inverse real-time factor and time-to-first audio), and naturalness (UTMOS for English and KOMOS for Korean). Fixed-voice and voice cloning are evaluated, with an additional speaker similarity metric for the latter. The leaderboard also has a Listen tab, where you can conveniently compare TTS outputs yourself and vote for the ones you like the most.

On intelligibility, Phonon ranks first in German and Spanish, and in the top three in every language we evaluated, next to models up to 100 times its size.

Speed is where we see Phonon truly shine, as the models top the CPU streaming benchmark on time-to-first audio.

We built a new inference framework, ptts, specifically for Phonon. The goal of ptts is to get the best performance on a wide variety of devices and as such supports more than 10 backends: CPU (Intel, Arm, WebAssembly), GPU (CUDA, Vulkan, Metal, WebGPU), and NPU (Apple Core ML, Snapdragon®).

Phonon is also available in 🤗 Transformers, from v5.21, as its reference PyTorch implementation. Where ptts runs a single stream on device, Transformers targets GPU throughput: batched generation, full-graph torch.compile, e.g., for synthesizing speech datasets at scale. Being native PyTorch, it is a base to build upon, whether to integrate Phonon into your own stack or to study the model itself.

It belongs to the Pocket TTS lineage, so we first added Pocket TTS to Transformers. Phonon sits on top as a modular model: about 200 lines describing only what differs.

Phonon generates audio latents autoregressively: each step outputs a latent that is fed back as the next input, and the training loss is computed on them. Latents are the model's inputs, outputs and targets, as text tokens are for a language model, so the codec mapping audio to latents plays the role of a tokenizer. Transformers ships it as such: an audio tokenizer, held by the processor next to the text tokenizer:

from transformers import AutoProcessor, GradiumPhononTTSForConditionalGeneration

model_id = "gradium/phonon-31m-en"

processor = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained(model_id, device_map="auto")

model = GradiumPhononTTSForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained(model_id, device_map="auto")

print(f"{model.num_parameters():,}")  # 30,788,354

print(f"{processor.audio_tokenizer.num_parameters():,}")  # 10,305,313

A voice is not a recording but a tensor of speaker latents. Phonon never encodes audio directly with its audio tokenizer, which ships decoder-only, and voices come precomputed: gradium/phonon-voices.

from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download

from safetensors.torch import load_file

path = hf_hub_download("gradium/phonon-voices", "voices/LMDIYE0Oa377Qh6Z.safetensors")

voice = load_file(path)["speaker_wavs"][0].T  # (num_frames, 512)

The prompt layout (voice positions, text, then a token opening the audio) ships with the checkpoint as a chat template, as for other multimodal models in Transformers. You write a standard conversation, with an empty audio item where the voice goes, and your code stays the same across checkpoints. The processor then expands the voice placeholder to one position per frame, normalizes and tokenizes the text, and batches the inputs:

conversation = [

{"role": "user", "content": [{"type": "audio"}, {"type": "text", "text": "Hello from Phonon."}]}

]

inputs = processor.apply_chat_template(

conversation,

tokenize=True,

return_dict=True,

add_generation_prompt=True,

processor_kwargs={"audio_prompt_latents": [voice]},

).to(model.device)

\# prompt: <|audio_prompt|> × num_frames, "Hello from Phonon.", <|audio_bos|>

generate returns latents, which the processor decodes to audio:

outputs = model.generate(**inputs)  # audio_latents: (batch_size, num_frames, 32)

audio = processor.decode(outputs.audio_latents, outputs.audio_latents_mask)

processor.save_audio(audio, "phonon.wav")

See the docs 🤗 for more!

Phonon can speak with any voice. Clone one from a short recording with the speaker’s consent, or describe a new one in plain text in Gradium Studio. Then download the voice file, load it in Phonon, and it runs fully on the device.

We also train custom Phonon models with design partners. If your memory budget is tight, we can make the model smaller. If you need Korean, Arabic or Japanese, we can train for that too. And if your product has its own vocabulary, we can fine-tune on it. Get in touch and tell us what you need.

Thank you to the Hugging Face team, and to everyone who tried Phonon in its first two weeks.

Phonon is our first open release. Try it out and tell us what you build.

## 8. OpenAI、「Images 2.6」発表 生成速度50％向上、GIFアニメの生成も可能に

- **Fonte:** ITmedia AI+
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-04 22:59
- **Idioma:** ja
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.itmedia.co.jp/news/article/2611/05/2000002894/

米OpenAIは11月4日（現地時間）、画像生成AIの新版「ChatGPT Images 2.6」を発表した。9月に公開した「Images 2.5」の後継モデルで、画像生成速度を50％向上させたほか、編集精度を改善した。新機能としてGIFアニメーションの作成や、保存した写真を別のチャットでも再利用できる機能も追加する。

Images 2.6は同日から、世界中の「ChatGPT」と「Codex」の全プランで利用可能になった。無料プランも対象で、Web、デスクトップアプリ、モバイルアプリに対応する。ただし、GIF生成機能は順次展開中としている。

従来のImages 2.5では、画像の特定部分だけを変更し、それ以外の部分を維持する編集機能を強化していた。Images 2.6ではこれをさらに改善し、例えばキャラクターの背景を変更しても衣装などの細部を忠実に維持できるようにした。キャラクターや商品、ブランド固有の特徴を、編集を繰り返しても一貫して保ちやすくなったという。

新たに対応するGIFアニメーション生成では、ChatGPTにループアニメーションの作成を依頼したり、手描きのスケッチをアップロードしてキャラクターが手を振るアニメーションに変換したりできる。再生速度の調整やGIFの共有も可能だ。企業のマスコットや商品のアニメーション、SNS向けのリアクション画像などへの利用を想定している。

もう1つの新機能「保存した参考画像」は、写真を名前付きで保存し、別のチャットでも再利用できるというものだ。例えば、愛犬の写真を「Pepper」という名前で保存しておけば、次回以降は写真を再アップロードすることなく、「@Pepperの水彩画を描いて」と指示できる。

開発者向けAPIでは、速度重視の「GPT-Image-2.6 Flare」と、高品質な画像生成を重視する「GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst」を提供する。Flareは低画質設定で最短6秒で画像を生成でき、ポスターや商品画像などのレイアウト品質も改善した。Sunburstは中～高画質設定で、より精細な画像生成と編集時の一貫性を実現する。

APIではさらに、画像の特定箇所に注釈を加えて修正を指示する機能や、Webサイトや印刷物などに使えるSVG形式での書き出しに対応。画像生成前にモデルがどの程度検討するかを調整する「complexity」パラメータも追加した。

API料金はImages 2.5から据え置きで、両モデルとも画像入力が100万トークン当たり8ドル、画像出力が同30ドル、テキスト入力が同5ドルとなる。

同日公開したシステムカードでは、安全対策についても説明している。新たなGIF生成機能には、静止画では検出しにくい有害な動きなどを判定する安全対策を追加した。また、生成画像の出所を確認できるようにするため、従来のC2PAメタデータに加え、米Google DeepMindの電子透かし技術「SynthID」をChatGPT、Codex、APIに導入することも明らかにした。

OpenAIによると、現在ChatGPT、Codex、APIを通じて生成される画像は世界で週約40億枚、1日平均5億枚以上に達しているという。

## 9. AI PACs spent millions trying to shape Congress — here’s how they fared

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-04 19:45
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/04/ai-pacs-spent-millions-trying-to-shape-congress-heres-how-they-fared/

Political groups both supporting and opposing the AI industry spent millions of dollars trying to influence Tuesday’s midterm elections and shape the future of AI regulation. That spending produced mixed results, with some of the most heavily backed candidates losing their races.

On the industry side, two AI-focused political action committees (PACs) that raised and spent money to influence the elections — American Mission and Building the Future — collectively spent $4.8 million to back South Carolina Senate candidate Darline Graham, who lost. American Mission is the Republican spending arm of Leading the Future (LTF), a broader political group backed by OpenAI president Greg Brockman and the VC firm Andreessen Horowitz.

American Mission also spent more than $500,000 apiece supporting Republican House candidates Aaron Flint in Montana and Laurie Buckhout in North Carolina, both of whom lost. LTF’s Democratic spending arm, Think Big, had better luck backing Florida Rep. Jared Moskowitz, who won reelection.

Groups advocating for stronger AI safeguards also saw mixed results. Public First, which is backed by Anthropic, supported several Republicans who lost through its affiliated PAC but had more success backing Democratic candidates. It also contributed $3 million to a PAC supporting California state Sen. Scott Wiener, who was leading his race at the time of publication.

Meanwhile, Guardrails Alliance, a group that has sought to make taking money from AI companies a political liability, spent more than $1 million apiece opposing two candidates who lost: California Rep. Jimmy Gomez and Montana’s Aaron Flint. It also spent $300,000 supporting Angela Gonzales-Torres, who defeated Gomez.

It’s difficult to separate the impact of AI-related spending from the broader Democratic wave that swept Tuesday’s elections. Elon Musk, who reportedly poured half a billion dollars into supporting Republicans in the midterms, also saw many of his candidates lose. Indeed, LTF also backed some candidates it believed were likely to win, only to see them lose to the Dems.

For LTF, though, the general election wasn’t the main focus. The group told TechCrunch ahead of Election Day that it thinks primaries (the contests that choose each party’s nominee) are a more effective use of its money, because spending there can build name recognition and help candidates set up AI policy agendas.

LTF spent $27.38 million in the primaries, including $8 million alone on opposing New York Assembly member Alex Bores, who lost his bid for Congress back in June. When deciding whether it’s worth jumping into a general election race, LTF assesses how competitive the race is, whether it thinks spending could make a difference, and whether it should keep its powder dry for future elections.

Charlie Blaettler, political director of Guardrails Alliance, had a different theory for why LTF spent comparatively little in the general election.

“They don’t want their money to become an issue in these elections,” he said. He pointed to the Michigan Senate primary election, in which a pro-Israel lobbying group called AIPAC spent close to $30 million backing Democratic candidate Haley Stevens against Abdul El-Sayed. Stevens conceded the primary to El-Sayed, who went on to win the Senate seat.

“AIPAC became the story in that race,” Blaettler said. “We know people hate AI billionaires. They hate the people running these companies. They’re very distrustful and disgusted by the political spending and lobbying that the tech industry does. If they spend in a competitive race, they would just become an enormous liability for their candidates.”

The main fight among AI-related PACs, whether they are pro-industry or pro-regulation, is whether Congress should establish a national AI framework that overrides state laws, or whether states will maintain their own ability to impose safeguards.

Neither side seems poised to back down. LTF, which favors a single national AI framework, told TechCrunch it plans to keep spending in the 2028 elections, while Guardrails Alliance says it wants to keep opposing candidates backed by the AI industry.

Blaettler said that while LTF’s ads rarely touch on AI, instead focusing on popular issues for each candidate, Guardrail Alliance’s ads are specifically about who is taking money from the AI industry.

“Our goal is to make AI money politically toxic,” Blaettler said.

## 11. GitHub、「Copilot Fusion」正式リリース タスクに応じて最適なAIモデル選択、高性能・低コスト両立

- **Fonte:** ITmedia AI+
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-04 01:13
- **Idioma:** ja
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.itmedia.co.jp/news/article/2611/04/2000002869/

米GitHubは、米サンフランシスコで10月28日と29日の2日間開催した年次イベント「GitHub Universe 2026」で、新機能「GitHub Copilot Fusion」の正式リリースを発表しました。

Copilot Fusionは、与えられたタスクの特性に応じて、単一または複数のAIモデルを組み合わせて実行するマルチモデル対応の機能です。

推論、コード生成、デバッグ、ツールの利用など、タスクごとに必要な能力を評価し、AIモデルをどのように組み合わせて利用するかを判断します。

これにより、開発者がAIモデルの選択を個別に管理する負担を軽減し、性能とコストのバランスを考慮した開発を進められます。

複数のパターンでAIモデルを使い分ける

Copilot Fusionは、与えられたタスクに応じて以下の3つのパターンのいずれかを選択します。

- Single（シングル）

選択したモデルで課題を解決する

- Cascade（カスケード）

効率的なモデルとして選択されたAIモデルが解決策の案を作成し、品質確認用のAIモデルがそれを検証して、より高い能力のAIモデルにエスカレーションするかどうかを判断する

- Critique（批評）

まず1つのAIモデルが解決案のドラフトを作り、別のモデルファミリーのAIモデルが独立してレビューする。レビューが指摘を返すと、その指摘をもとに、ドラフトを作ったAIモデルが解決案を1回だけ修正する。

上記のパターンを選択するうえで、品質、コストをどのようにバランスさせるかを調整するための負荷レベルが用意されています。

- Fusion Standard

コスト、品質、待機時間のバランスを取る。日常業務向け。

- Fusion Hight

中程度のトークン節約によるバランスの取れたオーケストレーション。複雑なバグの修正や複数のファイルにまたがる変更など、スコープが明確な大規模なコーディングタスク向け。

- Fusion Max

トークンの効率を考慮しながら、最先端の性能を実現。可能な限り深い推論と最も徹底的な分析を必要とするタスク向け。

- Fusion Fast

高速なモデルが必要なシナリオで選択される。ユーザーは指定できない。

## 13. Dharma Field Extractor: Any Document In, Structured Fields in JSON Out

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-04 14:50
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 4.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/Dharma-AI/dharma-field-extractor

A 2-billion-parameter model that extracts exactly the fields you ask for, from any document type, outperforming every frontier LLM we tested, at a fraction of the cost.

In April, we released DharmaOCR-LITE, a model specialized for OCR across a wide range of documents, from contracts and court filings to invoices, IDs and forms. The paper behind it made a narrow, well-measured case for domain specialization: a smaller model trained closer to its task beating general-purpose frontier APIs on that task.

OCR gives you the text on a page, faithful to the original formatting. That's exactly what you want when the job is the whole document, like a contract you'll read end to end or a manuscript you're digitizing.

However, there's a different use case: you don't want the page, you want specific fields out of it, in a shape a system downstream can consume. Every line item on an invoice, as a list. The patient name, exam type and date from a medical requisition, ready to schedule. The fields off an ID, mapped to the columns of an employee record.

Run that document through OCR and you get back everything printed on it, line by line, with no indication of which string is the exam type, which is the date or which number is the total. Someone still has to write the code that maps each value to the right field before it reaches the database.

A general-purpose LLM can do this work today, with careful prompting. At frontier API pricing and latency, on a task many businesses need to run at high volume, that quickly becomes the expensive way to solve a well-defined problem.

So we built a model for it instead. The Field Extractor takes a document image and either a request for everything it can find, or an exact schema to fill, and returns structured JSON: typed, field by field, nested where the document nests. An invoice comes back with every line item as an ordered list, each with its own quantity and value, alongside the totals. An ID comes back as the fields you'll store, under the names your system already uses for them. The same checkpoint handles all of it, with no per-type fine-tuning.

You can try it yourself here.

Say a batch of supplier invoices lands every morning, and you need four things off each one to push into your system: the supplier, the invoice number, the due date, the total. Same story for medical requisitions going into a scheduler, or contracts feeding a compliance system, where what matters is the parties, the term, the penalties and the fine amounts. You don't need the page, you need certain fields. With the Field Extractor there are two ways to ask for them:

- Open: no schema supplied. The model reads the page and works out which parts of it are structured fields (a label followed by a value, a table with a header row over its columns) from context and layout, not from a fixed list of field names, and returns everything it finds as key-value pairs.

- Schema-directed: you specify the exact shape wanted, field by field, with a type for each one (string, number, or boolean), under the key names your system already uses. Ask for "service_provider" and "client_company", and those are the exact keys that come back. Ask for a table and it fills it row by row, column by column, matching each cell to the key it belongs under. Ask for a field that isn't on the document, and it returns null instead of inventing a value.

The choice comes down to what you already know. Send an open request when you don't know what the document holds. Send a schema when the output has to match a shape that already exists somewhere else.

Either way, what comes back is JSON: the fields as keys, the values typed as requested, and null where the document didn't have what you asked for. When you send a schema, the output follows it exactly, with the same keys and no extra fields, so it can go straight into the next system.

The base model is DharmaOCR-LITE v1.1, a new 2-billion-parameter version optimized from the one in the paper and already specialized for OCR through supervised fine-tuning and DPO. On top of that checkpoint we trained a LoRA adapter on 43,384 training examples covering both request modes.

The data is organized into three streams:

- English multi-domain data: diverse documents collected from the public web, spanning many document types.

- Brazilian domain data: real Brazilian documents sourced internally, in Portuguese.

- Multilingual data: a smaller share covering other European and Asian languages, for broader generalization beyond English and Portuguese.

All three streams are annotated at the field level. The mix is deliberately multi-domain: receipts, commercial invoices, forms, bank statements, ID documents and synthetic documents, rather than one filing type.

The full benchmark covers 546 documents across three request scenarios, for 1,638 total cases: extracting every field with no schema, a partial schema naming only real fields, and a schema that also includes deliberately nonexistent fields. The score is field-level F1 (matched by path, including position inside a table), multiplied by the Levenshtein similarity on the values that matched and by the share of outputs that come back as valid JSON.

We ran the same benchmark against four general-purpose frontier configurations.

Against general-purpose LLMs, the quality gap runs from 1.8 points (Claude Opus 5, the closest) to 9.3 points (GPT-5.6 Luna, the widest).

Cost runs from roughly 6 times lower (GPT-5.6 Luna) to roughly 147 times lower (GPT-5.6 Sol). The two sides are priced differently. The frontier figures come from measured average input and output tokens per page, reasoning tokens included, against each provider's published list pricing. Every frontier model ran with reasoning set to low; Claude Opus 5 used adaptive effort, which can skip reasoning on simpler pages while staying at the low level. Ours isn't billed per token: the model runs self-hosted, so the figure is GPU time on a single L40S at $2.24 per hour.

This is not a sales price. Self-hosted cost depends on the hardware, the volume and the latency each deployment needs. The comparison is here to show that a sovereign SSLM, deployed on your own infrastructure, is a real alternative to frontier APIs.

SROIE and FUNSD are two public datasets in our benchmark, not used in training. Below is one example from each.

On a SROIE receipt, we asked for all 18 fields printed on it, with no phantom fields this time, and every one comes back correct: the multi-line, unlabeled billing address captured as a single field, the two blank fields (salesperson and ref) correctly returned as null, and every number on the receipt matching to the cent.

On a FUNSD photocopying variance form, a checklist of twelve boolean fields with one nested group of five sub-options, the model marks exactly the one box that's checked (poor quality original, with a handwritten "faded" note beside it) as true, and the other eleven as false, matching the form exactly. It also reads the handwritten Bates ID off the page correctly, and returns null for the two fields left blank.

A 2-billion-parameter model scored 0.834, above every frontier configuration we tested, from Claude Opus 5 at 0.816 to GPT-5.6 Luna at 0.741, while running on a single L40S at a lower cost per extraction than every API in the comparison. Higher quality and lower cost, measured on the same benchmark, in the same run.

This isn't the first time we've measured it. Our OCR paper put the first DharmaOCR-LITE, a 3-billion-parameter model, above every commercial frontier API tested on Brazilian Portuguese; three months later, newer and better-resourced models arrived, and the gap held. Now a 2-billion-parameter model does it on field extraction. Each result adds evidence for the thesis we started Dharma-AI with: on a bounded task, a small model trained close to that task beats a frontier model asked to do the same thing in general. In this benchmark, it also cost 6 to 147 times less to run.

Parameter count sets the ceiling; training decides how much of it a model reaches on your task. Your business doesn't need the biggest model available. It needs one trained for your problem.
