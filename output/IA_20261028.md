## 2. Welcome Le Chonk: Mistral Large 4 on Hugging Face

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-28 21:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 10.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/mistral-large4

Mistral Large 4 (aka Le Chonk) is a multimodal, multilingual, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model from Mistral. It takes text and images as input and generates text. It has about 1T total, 49B active parameter, and a context window of 1M tokens.

The model is released in two official variants:

There are also GGUFs created by Unsloth and the llama.cpp team. It’s distributed under the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License (an MIT derivative that establishes limitations for model-as-a-service offerings).

Mistral Large 4 is particularly strong for cybersecurity, coding agents, and multimodality. In this post, we look at how the model works, its capabilities, architecture and more!

Mistral Large 4 combines a large pool of experts with sparse attention and a Pixtral vision encoder. The language model selects a small group of experts for each token, while its attention indexer selects which earlier token positions to attend to. These are two different forms of sparsity, where one selects feed-forward networks, the other selects context positions.

You can find all the checkpoints in the Mistral Large 4 collection.

Mistral Large 4 scores 82% on the Artificial Analysis Cyber Index test that asks a model to reproduce a real vulnerability in open-source software and then patch the software. Mistral reports that several closed frontier models score near zero on the same test because they refuse to attempt the work. This is super important for defenders, because fixing a vulnerability usually requires reproducing the vulnerability. In Mistral’s internal testing, Mistral Large 4 was also useful for malware analysis, vulnerability prioritisation and writing detection rules, which the model was not explicitly trained for.

Mistral mainly positions the model for defensive security work. Vulnerability research and incident response often appear like attacks in model input, so closed source filters can block them. A team that depends on an API can also lose access in the middle of an incident. Crucially, open weight models put both the usage policy and the deployment under the user’s control. That said, the model still declines harmful requests: on cyber prompts from JailbreakBench, StrongREJECT and AgentHarm, its average refusal rate is higher than any other open weight model.

For community builders, an open weight model like Mistral Large 4 unlocks a load of downstream use cases. You could build your own cyber system around your code base, crash logs, and malware samples. For example, with TRL you could fine-tune smaller models from Mistral Large 4 traces patching your code base. In fact, reproduce-then-patch tasks use a verifiable reward, so it fits with RL environments that could be used to further RL smaller security models. Open weights also let researchers measure the model’s refusal behaviour directly instead of relying on the provider’s description.

In short, cyber capabilities like ML4’s in the open are a huge boost for the community.

We have interviewed Pierre Stock about the cyber capabilities of the model, you can watch it here.

Mistral Large 4’s text decoder has 72 layers, a hidden dimension of 6,144, and 64 attention heads. Its attention blocks combine Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) with a DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) indexer (a direction in other models such as GLM 5.3). MLA keeps a compressed KV representation, and DSA selects up to 2,048 key positions for each query.

For each decoder block, there’s a 3:1 RoPE to NoPE pattern, i.e. 3 decoder layers employ RoPE and every fourth layer uses NoPE. The model also shares KV state across layers.

The first decoder layer uses a dense feed-forward network. The other 71 layers use MoE blocks, in which a softmax router selects four of the 128 experts for each token. One shared expert always runs alongside the selected experts, for a total of 5 active experts per token.

Regarding RoPE, Mistral uses LeRope, a YaRN-scaled RoPE with one learned multiplier per frequency.

Mistral Large 4 employs a 1.6B Pixtral encoder. Pixtral has 30 layers, a hidden dimension of 2,048, and 16 attention heads. It uses 14 × 14 patches and a patch-merge projector with spatial merge size 2. You can see the encoder pipeline below.

Mistral Large 4 delivers strong benchmark performance across coding, cybersecurity, multimodal understanding, and agentic workflows, positioning it as a competitive addition to the open-weight ecosystem. As of October 29, 2026, the model is the fifth best open weights model on Artificial Analysis Intelligence index.

On coding, ML4 achieves 61.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, 59.4% on SWE-Atlas-QnA, and 28.3% on Terminal-Bench 4. Its combined Coding Agent Index score of 49.8% places it ahead of DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 and Qwen3.8 Max.

Coding capabilities of ML4 extend to knowledge work, with 59.9% on AutomationBench and 1,393 Elo on AA-Briefcase, which evaluate business workflows and long-horizon professional tasks.

Cybersecurity is one of Mistral Large 4’s standout capabilities, with strong results across real-world vulnerability assessment and security competition tasks. Three benchmarks highlight the model’s cyber performance:

- Artificial Analysis Cyber Index: ML4 ranks among the top five models globally on this independent evaluation of models’ ability to find and fix software vulnerabilities.

- Vulnerability reproduction and patching: It achieves 82%, the highest reported score on this test within the index. The task requires reproducing a real vulnerability in open-source software and then patching it, testing both investigation and remediation.

- Cybench: ML4 solves 93% of a suite of 40 challenges drawn from security competitions, demonstrating strong performance on hands-on security problems.

Mistral Large 4 also demonstrates 93.3% attack resistance on Lakera’s B3 benchmark, adding evidence of robustness against prompt-injection attacks. Beyond benchmarks, internal testing showed promising results in malware analysis, vulnerability prioritization, and detection-rule generation.

Mistral Large 4 is shipped with day-0 support for transformers, vLLM and llama.cpp. GGUF quants are available from GGUF and the llama.cpp team. We provide options to deploy remotely with Inference Endpoints, and use serverless inference through Inference Providers.

The Transformers implementation provides AutoModelForMultimodalLM, with AutoProcessor preparing the conversation and image inputs. Mistral Large 4 can take low, medium, high as reasoning effort. The default is high, and note that reasoning cannot be disabled, so using reasoning_effort: none would still use high.

model_id,

distributed_config=distributed_config,

\# only text

}

]

\# text + image

{

"role": "user",

"content": [

In the following example we train the model to perform multilingual thinking. The fine tuned model would be able to reason in the language the system prompt asks for!

The script runs on the FP8 variant on a node of 8xB300s.

\# torchrun --nproc-per-node 8 sft.py

import os

from datasets import load_dataset

from peft import LoraConfig

from transformers import AutoModelForImageTextToText, DistributedConfig

from trl import SFTConfig, SFTTrainer

world_size = int(os.environ["WORLD_SIZE"])

model_id = "mistralai/Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B"

model = AutoModelForImageTextToText.from_pretrained(

model_id,

distributed_config=DistributedConfig(fsdp_size=world_size, ep_size=world_size),

dtype="auto",

)

dataset = load_dataset("HuggingFaceH4/Multilingual-Thinking", split="train")

\# the assistant turns carry reasoning, so render them in reasoning mode

dataset = dataset.map(lambda x: {"chat_template_kwargs": {"reasoning_effort": "high"}})

trainer = SFTTrainer(

model=model,

args=SFTConfig(

packing=True,

max_length=4096,

assistant_only_loss=True,

learning_rate=1e-4,

lr_scheduler_type="cosine",

warmup_steps=10,

),

train_dataset=dataset,

peft_config=LoraConfig(

r=16,

lora_alpha=32,

target_modules=r".*language_model.*\.(q_proj|kv_a_proj_with_mqa|kv_b_proj|o_proj|gate_proj|up_proj|down_proj)",

),

)

trainer.train()

Mistral Large 4 is also available on Inference Endpoints, with a ready-to-deploy recipe on 8 x NVIDIA H200 GPUs. The recipe runs vLLM with the FP8 weights split across all eight GPUs using tensor and expert parallelism, and uses the model's built-in DSpark draft head for faster generation through speculative decoding. It serves up to 131K tokens of context and processes up to 16 requests at a time, and we observed the following results:

- Single request: time to first token of about 0.6 seconds for a 1K-token prompt, and a generation speed of ~65 tok/s, reaching 120–138 tok/s on chat prompts where DSpark's drafts are accepted.

- Long prompts: 16K-token prompts, four at a time, are prefilled at about 4,300 tok/s in total, with a time to first token of 8–9 seconds.

- Under load: about 480 tok/s of total generation across 1,000 ShareGPT conversations.

## 4. Introducing AnySpeechRecognizer and AnyDecisionModel: Open Models for Speech and Decisions on Apple Platforms

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-28 21:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 9.0
- **Link:** https://huggingface.co/blog/anydecisionmodel

Language models can do a lot. They write, summarize, translate, and call tools. But they're only a small part of what machine learning can do.

Say a customer calls your support line. First, you want to know what they said (speech recognition). Then, you want to decide where to send them (decision). You could hand both to a language model, but there are models built for each that are better, faster, and less expensive.

In our AnyLanguageModel 1.0 recap, we looked at a year of local and hosted models in Swift. With AnyLanguageModel and swift-huggingface now on their 1.x releases, we're extending the same approach to speech and decisions: familiar Swift APIs with a choice of model.

Today we're announcing two packages:

- AnySpeechRecognizer implements Apple's new speech-to-text API with a choice of recognition engine, including NVIDIA's Parakeet and Alibaba's Qwen3-ASR running locally on Core ML, and adds speaker diarization to track who speaks when.

- AnyDecisionModel answers yes-or-no, multiple-choice, and rating-scale questions with typed results and probabilities. It derives each answer from the model's probabilities instead of generating text, using a model on your device with MLX, Core AI, or llama.cpp, or a decision model from Jev or Ollama.

Both packages can download open models from the Hugging Face Hub and run them on device.

Computers have been transcribing speech for decades,

and in the past few years, they've gotten very good at it.

Apple platforms already have an API for it.

macOS 26 and iOS 26 introduced SpeechAnalyzer,

Apple's new speech-to-text API.

You add modules such as SpeechTranscriber and SpeechDetector to an analyzer,

give it a file or a stream of audio buffers,

and read results from async sequences as they arrive.

AssetInventory installs the models,

so your app decides when downloads happen.

It's a well-designed API, but the only engine behind it is Apple's. AnySpeechRecognizer keeps the API and lets you choose the engine:

- import Speech

+ import AnySpeechRecognizer

The package mirrors SpeechAnalyzer, SpeechTranscriber, DictationTranscriber,

SpeechDetector, and AssetInventory,

with Apple's Speech framework as the default engine.

If your code uses that shared API, changing the import is enough.

To use Parakeet instead,

change only how you create the transcriber:

let transcriber = SpeechTranscriber(

model: ParakeetSpeechModel(variant: .v3),

transcriptionOptions: [],

reportingOptions: [.volatileResults],

attributeOptions: [.audioTimeRange]

)

The steps for installing assets, running the analyzer, and reading results stay the same. The README has a complete example and lists the differences from Apple's framework.

Parakeet v3 is NVIDIA's Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3. It's a polyglot: it recognizes 25 European languages and works out which one it's hearing, so you don't pass a locale.

Parakeet Ultra is Moondream's post-trained take on v3. It was more accurate in most of our measurements.

Parakeet Unified is NVIDIA's English-only Parakeet.

All three Parakeets run on the package's own Core ML runtime, which supports file and live input, volatile results, word timing, and word confidence.

Qwen3-ASR is Alibaba's speech model, in 0.6B and 1.7B sizes. It recognizes 30 languages and 22 Chinese dialects, including Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, and Korean, which Parakeet doesn't cover. It detects the language automatically, or you can set one:

let transcriber = SpeechTranscriber(

model: QwenSpeechModel(variant: .large, language: Locale.Language(identifier: "yue")),

transcriptionOptions: [],

reportingOptions: [],

attributeOptions: []

)

Qwen3-ASR gives one final result for each segment of up to 40 seconds, without volatile results, word timing, or confidence. It runs on the GPU and is slower than Parakeet, so it's better suited to transcribing files than live input.

To add another engine, implement SpeechRecognitionModel for its configuration

and SpeechRecognitionExecutor for one module's session.

The README includes a small provider

that reports placeholder text to help you get started.

To run these models in apps, we converted them to Core ML and published them on the Hub:

Each repository records its source checkpoint and a checksum for every file, and the package pins it to a fixed revision. The Parakeet repositories add an 8-second encoder for live input, which cuts live analysis time by about a third at the cost of a somewhat higher word error rate. The repository also has the conversion workflows, most of which follow FluidInference's mobius scripts, and checks each conversion against the original model.

Downloads happen only when your app asks for them: about 600 MB for each Parakeet, plus about 590 MB for the live encoder, 0.9 to 2.4 GB for Qwen3-ASR, and about 250 MB for Sortformer. Files go in the standard Hugging Face cache, so a Mac app outside the sandbox shares them with Python and other Hugging Face tools.

When you transcribe a conversation,

you often want to know who said what.

That's speaker diarization,

and Apple's Speech framework doesn't have a module for it,

so AnySpeechRecognizer adds one.

SpeakerDiarizer runs NVIDIA's

Streaming Sortformer

on Core ML alongside an Apple or Parakeet transcriber,

on the same timeline:

let diarizer = SpeakerDiarizer(model: SortformerDiarizationModel())

if let request = try await AssetInventory.assetInstallationRequest(

supporting: [transcriber, diarizer]

) {

try await request.downloadAndInstall()

}

let analyzer = SpeechAnalyzer(modules: [transcriber, diarizer])

The diarizer tracks up to four speakers,

even when they talk over each other.

A SpeakerTimeline collects its results

and attributes each timed word to a speaker,

so you can print a transcript by speaker turn.

Like image support in AnyLanguageModel,

this goes beyond what Apple's API offers today.

The diarizer uses Sortformer v2 by default. You can also choose v2.1, which NVIDIA trained with more meeting data and which was more accurate on meetings in our measurements, too.

AnyDecisionModel is a different story. There's no Apple framework to map onto, and decision models aren't new, exactly.

It's only a decision model if it comes from the Décision region of France; otherwise, it's sparkling zero-shot classification.

What's new is the name, a lot of hype, and a crop of models trained to make decisions. We think that's where things are going, so we wanted a Swift API that's ready for them.

Apps often ask language models to make decisions. Is this message urgent? Which team should handle this ticket? How frustrated is this customer? The usual approach is to prompt a language model and parse the text that it generates. That gives you one answer, but doesn't show the probabilities it assigned to the alternatives.

Here's what a decision looks like in Swift:

import AnyDecisionModel

enum Department: String, Choosable {

case billing, technical, sales

}

let ticket = "I was charged twice for my order, and now the app logs me out when I try to ask for a refund."

let model = MLXDecisionModel(modelID: "mlx-community/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507-4bit")

let session = DecisionSession(model: model, state: .text(ticket))

let urgent = try await session.probability(of: "Does this convey urgency?")

let team = try await session.choice("Which team?", from: Department.self)

let anger = try await session.score(

"How frustrated?",

levels: ["Calm", "Frustrated", "Very angry"]

)

urgent is a Double.

team is a Choice<Department> containing the selected case,

a probability for each option, and a confidence value.

anger is a Score containing the expected level,

a probability for each level, and a confidence value.

Because Department conforms to Choosable,

team.value is a Department that you can use in an exhaustive switch.

If you've used Foundation Models or AnyLanguageModel,

the model-and-session pattern will look familiar.

A DecisionSession holds a single state, which can be text or JSON,

and every question is about that state.

Questions are independent:

an answer never becomes input to a later question.

To ask several questions at once,

pass them to decide(_:) as a batch.

For each question, the MLX backend builds a prompt with a one-token label for each allowed answer, then reads the probability of each label from one forward pass.

Emoji Magnet is a macOS app with a text field above a pile of 343 emoji. Type a phrase, such as "things you wear", and the emoji that match rise out of the pile. Refine it to "things you wear in winter", and the emoji that no longer match fall back.

The app has no built-in keyword rules; the model decides everything at inference time. When you pause typing, it asks the model one yes-or-no question for each emoji, for example:

Statement: a heavy winter coat fits the description "things you wear". Is this statement true?

The session's state holds the phrase.

The demo above uses the hosted Jev backend,

which answers all 343 questions in one request,

so the emoji settle in about half a second for each phrase.

Without a TYPESAFE_AI_API_KEY, the app runs Qwen3 4B on your Mac with the MLX backend.

That backend uses prefix caching to evaluate the state once,

then answers 16 questions per forward pass.

On an M4 Max, a group of 16 takes about 0.45 seconds after the model loads.

A full pass over all 343 emoji takes about 10 seconds,

and each group's results appear as soon as they arrive.

Emoji Magnet is in the AnyDecisionModel-Examples repository.

Reading next-token probabilities works with general-purpose language models, but those models weren't trained to make decisions. Cloudflare's Clef models are an example of a newer approach: a model with a trained decision head that turns a state and a schema of typed questions into decisions, returning probabilities for all allowed options across the questions in one forward pass. Clef-Flash is the 9B variant.

AnyDecisionModel runs Clef-Flash on a Mac with MLX:

let model = MLXHeadDecisionModel(

modelID: "mlx-community/clef-flash-4bit",

head: .jointSchema

)

You use the same sessions and questions,

but MLXHeadDecisionModel reads each decision from Clef's trained decision head

instead of from next-token probabilities.

The 4-bit model is a 6.2 GB download,

and its model card recommends a Mac with at least 16 GB of memory.

Clef can read images and video,

but this backend accepts only text and JSON states.

Raw probabilities from language models are often overconfident.

The local backends accept a Calibration,

which applies a temperature to every answer and a bias to yes-or-no answers.

Use separate datasets to fit the calibration and evaluate the results.

Language models can also favor an option because of its position in a list.

On the MLX, Core AI, and llama.cpp next-token backends,

turn on rotationDebiasing to ask a choice question

once for each rotation of the options and average the probabilities.

That costs one prompt evaluation per rotation.

Each local choice and score also includes a confidence value based on the entropy of its probabilities. It measures how concentrated the probabilities are, not whether the answer is right, so check the error rate on your own data before you pick a threshold. The next-token backends' diagnostics report how much probability the model assigned to the allowed answers. A low value means it assigned most of the probability elsewhere.

AnyDecisionModel has six backends:

- MLX runs a small language model on Apple silicon with mlx-swift-lm and downloads it from the Hub.

- MLX decision heads run a model with its own trained decision head, such as Clef.

- Core AI does the same as MLX with a Core AI model on macOS 27 and iOS 27.

- llama.cpp reads decisions from a local GGUF model through llama.swift.

- Jev calls TypeSafe's Jev through the System One API, or any server that implements it, such as Kev.

- Ollama calls a System One decision model on a local Ollama server or in Ollama's cloud. It requires Ollama 0.35.0 or later, or 0.40.5 or later for cloud models.

The next-token backends label answers "yes" and "no", A to Z, or 0 to 9, so a choice can have at most 26 options, and a score at most 10 levels. Decision heads have no such limit.

The PBC wrapper adds another wrinkle, requiring Anthropic to balance a mandate for public benefit with standard fiduciary responsibilities as a publicly traded corporation.

Anthropic's goal, according to its S-1 filing, is "to responsibly develop and maintain advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity."

Other companies operate as PBCs or under similar structures. Warby Parker is a PBC. Privately held Patagonia is registered as similar Benefit Corp. in California. When Google went public in 2004, its informal motto was "don't be evil," which it included in its IPO prospectus and corporate code of conduct, though Google is not a PBC.

Anthropic has long pitched itself as the responsible AI firm and has taken measures to police its own conduct. In 2023, Anthropic set up a "long-term benefit trust" to oversee its business practices. The company also established a "public benefit fund" with about 847 million shares of Class N stock, or roughly 5% of its fully diluted shares as of June 30.

Seven co-founders, including Dario Amodei, have special voting power through a single Class F share equal to 50.1% of total voting power over matters brought to a shareholder vote. Anthropic said in its filing that it believes those seven are "distinctly equipped to be stewards of our mission."

Anthropic gave itself a long leash to act as it sees fit. Operating as a PBC "gives our board of directors the necessary latitude to balance the pecuniary interests of our stockholders, the best interests of those materially affected by our conduct, and the specific public benefit purpose that we promote," the company said in its S-1.

Legal experts say that Anthropic's PBC structure could create tension in how it operates.

"The obligation of directors is to balance achievement of [public] purpose with achievement of profit, and if you ask me what that means – 'to balance' – I would say, 'Good luck,'" said Lawrence Hamermesh, a Delaware corporate law attorney with decades of experience in PBC law.

PBC law is ambiguous on a key word, "balance," he adds, pointing out that there's no fundamental criterion to distinguish when one interest takes precedence over the other.

"It's hard enough in the ordinary case to win a damages claim against directors for having screwed up and having cost the company some money," he said. "It's doubly hard to do so in a public benefit corporation where you also have this defense that, 'Hey, I was doing it to promote our stated public benefit.'"

How Anthropic handles AI safety is a key risk. Anthropic has been at the forefront in sounding alarms about rapidly advancing AI, outlining legions of risks in its S-1. The company has restricted access to some models it deems too dangerous to be released widely in public. That practice could continue, even if it comes at a cost to revenue and profit growth.

Anthropic will also have to balance its PBC mandates with shareholder interests as it continues to raise capital. The company has multiple share classes with varying degrees of control, shareholder, and voting rights. Investors typically charge more for capital in situations that dilute their control from one-share/one vote, points out John Coates, professor of law and economics at Harvard Law School.

"If the company needs to continue to raise capital over the foreseeable future, as seems to be true of Anthropic, the company will know they need to pay attention to investors' interests for much of that future," he said in an email to CNBC. "It's interesting to read it right up front in an S-1," Gil Luria, head of technology research at DA Davidson, said. "Governance limits shareholder influence: as a public benefit corporation, the board must weigh its mission alongside returns, an independent trust elects a majority of directors, and the co-founders hold 50.1% of the vote," he said.

Granted, Anthropic could alter or scrap its PBC status, and there's precedent for doing so. Shoe-maker-turned-AI-company Allbirds, for instance, dropped its PBC designation as part of a restructuring in June.

## 12. Anthropic、独自の“創薬活動”に着手 社員1人のバイオ企業を買収、小児難病の治療薬研究を進める

- **Fonte:** ITmedia AI+
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-28 01:07
- **Idioma:** ja
- **Relevância:** 7.0
- **Link:** https://www.itmedia.co.jp/news/article/2610/28/2000002661/

米Anthropicは10月27日（現地時間）、新薬開発に取り組む6組織との提携の進捗とあわせて、自社でも創薬活動を進めていると発表した。小児の希少な筋ジストロフィーの治療法を開発してきたバイオ企業を買収し、その研究を社内で続けている。

買収した米Mithril Medicineは、創業者のコーディ・トランバーガー氏が1人で運営してきた会社。トランバーガー氏はAI「Claude」を使って外部の11の研究機関との契約や物流、実験の段取りを取りまとめてきた。また、ほかの業務の多くもClaudeに任せているとしている。この体制で、2種類の筋ジストロフィーのうち1種類について、治療法のアイデアからマウスで有望な結果を得るまでを18カ月足らずで進めたという。

Anthropicによれば、対象の1つ「LAMA2関連先天性筋ジストロフィー」は、筋線維とその周りを覆う膜をつなぎ止めるタンパク質が欠けて起こる小児の病気で、根本的な治療法はまだないという。Mithrilは2つの標的に同時に結合する「二重特異性抗体」を使い、筋肉に残っている別のタンパク質と筋線維を橋渡しして、膜を固定し直す薬の開発を進めてきた。同じ仕組みは、もう1つの対象である「α-ジストログリカノパチー」にも応用できる可能性もあるとしている。

Anthropicが公開した技術報告書によると、この病気のモデルマウスで小規模な予備試験を実施し、試作した2種類の抗体のうち1種類を6週間投与したマウスでは、前脚の筋力と握力が、薬を含まない液を投与したマウスの約2倍だったという。

試作抗体に続く候補の絞り込みでも、作業の多くをClaudeが担っている。113種類の候補抗体を外部の受託研究機関（CRO）5社で評価する計画策定では、341ページに及ぶ製造データを読み込み、8分足らずで全候補を「そのまま試験に回す」「精製し直してから回す」「見送る」に振り分けた。受託研究機関などに送るメールもClaudeが下書きし、担当者が手直しした上で送ったという。

ただ、Claudeが誤るケースもあった。抗体の量を相手の希望より少なく書いた依頼など、少なくとも3件は担当者の確認をすり抜けて受託研究機関に届いた。報告書は、人の確認ではどの候補をどう試験するかといった判断の誤りには気付けても、数量などの細部や前提の誤りは見落としやすいとみている。

トランバーガー氏はAnthropicに移った後もこの研究を続けており、複数の新たな創薬プログラムを立ち上げる計画だ。候補薬が今後の試験でも有望さを保てば、早ければ2028年後半に人での臨床試験を始められる見込みだとしている。

Anthropicは6月30日（現地時間）に研究者向けのAI作業環境「Claude Science」を発表した際、自社でも創薬に乗り出す方針を示していた。対象は、従来の製薬・バイオ企業が開発対象として魅力を感じてこなかった疾患で、人での試験に入る前の「前臨床段階」の研究に取り組むとしていた。

## 13. Anthropic just launched tools to show companies what their AI spending is buying

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-28 16:19
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/28/anthropic-just-launched-tools-to-show-companies-what-their-ai-spending-is-buying/

The financials Anthropic shared with investors this week suggest that its AI coding tool, Claude Code, is every bit the business success the frontier lab wanted. The company recorded an operating profit in the second quarter, and 900% net revenue retention — that is, spending from the same customers in Q3 2025 grew nine times in Q3 2026.

Anthropic has focused on getting enterprise users to stick around and Wednesday announced a new suite of reporting tools designed to give those customers a more granular understanding of the value they get from their token spend.

Using internal analytics and employee surveys, managers can view a team’s work over a given date range, what the team produced, how much it cost, and where its workflows broke down. Anthropic says it won’t use this data for model training by default. The reports will also offer recommendations on how to improve the results.

This is a clear attempt to demonstrate the return on investment of Anthropic’s tools and to show customers how to improve it — a major concern among large customers. Anthropic is also creating pooled group budgets to allow teams to better manage token spending.

Businesses have been hunting for that kind of information as they figure out how best to deploy AI tools. While usage among venture-backed firms is driving token spend, most of the Fortune 500 are not seeing major gains from deploying AI.

Jellyfish, a company that makes tools for software developers, released a new report yesterday based on the use of agentic coding tools by 276,000 of its customers. Claude Code and other tools built on Anthropic models remain the most widely used in the survey, responsible for nearly a third of all proposed code changes submitted for review. More importantly, spending on those tools has grown 10x since the beginning of the year, with their cost now equal to about 2% of total engineering cost.

“Even if you’re saying 2% is still small, that 10x growth is growth that most CFOs didn’t plan for,” Nicholas Arcolano, the chief scientist at Jellyfish, told TechCrunch. We’re certainly getting to the point where people are realizing, okay, it’s not enough to just spend a bunch. You now have to optimize it, make it work.”

Anthropic’s new reports speak to that challenge. Anthropic leads among enterprise customers, but what makes an agentic coding tool truly sticky is difficult to identify. The release of OpenAI’s Astra model this summer seemed like a potential threat to Anthropic, but so far it hasn’t slowed Anthropic’s adoption numbers.

“It took a long time for us to see Codex really pop in our data at all, and it has come up with Astra [OpenAI’s latest model],” Nikolas Albarran, the AI product lead at Jellyfish, told TechCrunch. Looking at X, he continued, you’d get the distinct impression that the competition is more heated. “Everyone in the Valley is like, ‘Oh, Codex is better.’” In reality, Albarran continued, “Anthropic was just there with great enterprise motions, and they had an incumbent weight of their own — this stuff just worked and worked well, and a lot of people just put a lot of effort into standardizing workflows around it. And there’s not a big enough reason to switch.”

It’s not just a question of models and workflows, either. The realities of business mean that once a frontier lab has gotten its hooks into a customer, switching is harder than it is compared to an individual programmer or small organization. Albarran points to the investment all the major AI labs are making in forward-deployed engineering (FDE) organizations, teams that embed AI consultants inside customer organizations to help them get off the ground. These new reporting tools can be seen as a kind of FDE-lite, a lighter version of that hands-on help, for Anthropic customers.

“We’re at a point now where it’s mature enough, where once you’re in, it’s going to be harder and harder to switch because you’ve spent the effort,” he said. “Even if they’re all mostly the same, they’re all going to be slightly different, have their own slight quirks of how they handle agent.md files, etc., and so that cost to switch is not zero.”

## 14. ‘Elonville’: US residents say Musk’s xAI offered to buy homes if they don’t sue over datacenter noise

- **Fonte:** The Guardian (IA)
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-28 12:32
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.0
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/28/datacenter-noise-lawsuit-elon-musk-mississippi

People in a Mississippi neighborhood, many of whom dislike a nearby power plant central to Elon Musk’s artificial intelligence ambitions, have received a stark offer – to sell their houses to his company and make way.

Real estate agents have been going door to door in Southaven, Mississippi, on behalf of xAI, touting a deal to purchase houses as long as the owners agree to not join a lawsuit against the company over the power plant’s noise. (Musk’s AI company officially became SpaceXAI in July and has lately been rebranded as SpaceXSI but to neighbors of the datacenter in Mississippi, it remains xAI.)

This plan could potentially swallow up almost the entire neighborhood of Colonial Hills, on the western flank of Southaven. A realtor told one resident there is a target to purchase 418 homes, with 18 homeowners already agreeing to sell, according to a recording of a conversation heard by the Guardian. xAI did not respond to questions over these numbers and its plans for the neighborhood and has previously said it intends to be be a good neighbor; it denies all claims in the noise lawsuit.

The homes, the realtor and the local mayor say, will house xAI staff as the company continues its explosive expansion in the area, with three of its datacenters springing up in Southaven and adjoining Memphis, Tennessee, in just the past two years. This frantic pace, and the all-hours noise from gas turbines installed without permits to power the datacenters, has dismayed some locals.

“It sounds like an airplane engine but it’s continuous, it’s incredibly loud,” said Elizabeth Rose, a piano teacher, of the xAI site which sits about two miles from her home. “I’ve been woken up at 2am by it, I’ve been woken up at 3am by it. I sometimes have to just get away from it because it makes me so angry.”

Rose said she was shocked when the power plant, a former Duke Energy site housing 69 temporary gas turbines to feed the new datacenters, began its low-level growl in August last year. “All of a sudden there was a plant there,” she said. “And now I get the feeling they are trying to expand the buyout to the entire neighborhood. It will become Elon Musk’s little playground, basically. Elonville.”

The market value offers for these homes, in an area where the average house price is about $180,000, is a comparatively small outlay in creating the world’s largest cluster of AI supercomputers as envisioned by Musk, a technologist who is the world’s richest person and a major Republican donor.

Since July 2024, xAI has constructed three vast datacenters – called Colossus I, Colossus II and Macrohardrr – in the Memphis and Southaven conurbation, which sprawls across Tennessee’s border with Mississippi. Musk is currently spending up to four nights a week in an Airstream trailer in the Memphis area as he personally oversees the build-out of the datacenters to further develop the Grok AI chatbot.

“We’re seeing the fastest growth I’ve ever seen in my life for Grok bot,” Musk said during a SpaceX earnings call this month. “I’ve never seen anything grow this fast.”

Musk has spent about half a billion dollars helping elect Republicans in the last two election cycles and his zeal for datacenters is shared by Donald Trump, who has said anyone who opposes such development will become “backwards and poor”.

As is common in the broader grassroots backlash against datacenters, however, some residents in Southaven say they have been trampled upon in this gold rush. Three locals, anguished by the din of expansion, filed a lawsuit in June complaining of “near-constant noise, vibrations and other nuisance-level harms” from the nearby xAI power plant.

Jason Haley, one of the three plaintiffs, said he never considered himself an activist or political in any way but had been driven to distraction by the noise of the gas turbines that sit about half a mile from his home, which he has owned for the past 20 years.

“There’s a high-pitched hum and then another sound like an airplane over your house,” he said. “It’s been so torturous I can’t sleep, I just leave the house or turn on some music until I fall asleep completely exhausted. It drives you insane. You also lose hope because you’re being ignored or dismissed. There’s no escape.”

Exasperated by fruitless exchanges with Southaven’s mayor and enraged by Tate Reeves, the Republican, pro-AI Mississippi governor, claiming that curbs on datacenters are “surrendering the future of technology to China”, Haley lodged the lawsuit and started posting videos on social media of himself monitoring the rumbling noise levels.

“I thought I’d make some noise of my own because of this bullshit,” said Haley. “I didn’t know anything about Elon, still don’t know the man. But our governor saying I don’t love America because I don’t like being tortured by noise? That is just asinine and ignorant. We’ve got to hold people accountable when they are harming other people, I don’t care who you voted for, who you pray for or who your favorite team is.”

The allegations of heedless noise are denied by xAI, which has previously said that “we care deeply about being good neighbors” and has varied its power output and built sound-dampening walls. In a letter handed to nearby residents in Colonial Hills, MZX, the xAI subsidiary that operates the site, states that it “requires property for its business purposes” and is prepared to buy nearby houses with a contractual condition – that they do not join Haley’s class-action lawsuit.

This offer is starting to see results. Since 9 October, five neighborhood homes have been bought by MZX, according to DeSoto county records. Gavin Foust, who lives near Haley and said his elderly neighbors have just agreed to sell, predicts more will follow.

“They are ideal neighbors and planned to live their rest of their lives in that house but they want to get out of Dodge now because they don’t want to be bothered by these turbines,” said Foust, who added he won’t accept the offer himself due to its requirement not to sue.

“My hearing was damaged when I was in the army but I can still hear this thing, it’s like a jet engine. Why would you put it in a city? Why push people out? It’s reckless and shameful.”

Foust, a paramedic, grew up in the unassuming western reaches of Southaven, a district filled with affordable, 1960s homes and quiet, close-knit communities. This serenity has been upended by the noise from the xAI power plant, he said, as well as its air pollution.

Dozens of temporary, truck-sized gas turbines have been running at the xAI premises without permits required by federal law, the US Environmental Protection Agency has determined. The EPA has also said the permit requirements are a matter for the state to enforce.

While no on-site air pollution monitoring has been done, the Southern Environmental Law Center estimated the toxic emissions from these turbines, which are typically highly polluting, have caused $44m in annual health costs.

In April, environmental groups and the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) sued in federal court to stop use of the turbines, only for the US Department of Justice to unsuccessfully try to block the lawsuit by arguing xAI technology has been used to wage war on Iran and is therefore required for national security reasons.

“It’s been pretty shady from the get-go,” said Foust. “A lot of people in this neighborhood suffer from conditions exacerbated by all the nitrogen oxide, formaldehyde and other garbage put out by these turbines. For kids with asthma or elderly people with COPD [chronic obstructive pulmonary disease] it’s a mess all round. It’s pretty frustrating.”

In July, xAI agreed with state regulators to transform the power plant into a newer, permanent gas-fired facility by next summer, which should lessen the noise coming from the temporary turbines. Given the company’s repeated use of gas turbines without a federal permit and separate fines it may face for storm water violations, though, opponents remain wary.

“I’ve had hopes all along that haven’t come through,” said Haley. “The same company said it wasn’t polluting the air. I’m still hopeful but I’m not confident.”

The opposition to the datacenter influx hasn’t been universal, Haley acknowledged. Some locals aren’t too bothered by the noise while others actively champion the influx of cash into one of the poorest states in the union. Local businesses have experienced a surge in new activity, with the mayor hailing the datacenters for helping deliver new jobs and opportunity to the area.

“This development will produce unprecedented tax revenue for DeSoto county taxpayers that will enhance the quality of life for all,” said Darren Musselwhite, Southaven’s mayor, who oversaw a land swap deal with xAI that resulted in a new police and firefighter training center for the city.

“The buyout is not intended to be a company city at this point,” Musselwhite said of the property purchases. “This has never been mentioned to me. The buyout has multiple purposes which are to provide homes for some employees and a goodwill gesture from a good corporate citizen for those who perceive the industrial development near them as a negative.”

Musselwhite said dealing with the noise was a “top priority” for him but that city monitors had not detected a breach of a local noise ordinance limit of 70 decibels. Critics point to evidence that lower levels of noise, of 50 decibels or more like that recorded near the power plant, may be linked to adverse health impacts if persistent.

For those who can’t stand this noise, there is little to celebrate. Haley said the controversy had drawn the community together and made him question how AI is being “pushed down everyone’s throat”. He added: “Put it this way, I’m now registered to vote.” Rose, the piano teacher, said her roommate would accept three or four times the value of their home but she would still be opposed to such a sale.

“I wouldn’t take the offer if it was up to me, just to make things difficult for Elon,” she said. “I hate AI, I never understood why it was invented. I refuse to use it because I know touching it will cause that plant to crank up.”

## 15. Voice AI in vogue: Nvidia releases new open transcription model

- **Fonte:** TechCrunch AI
- **Publicado em:** 2026-10-28 12:00
- **Idioma:** en
- **Relevância:** 6.0
- **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/28/voice-ai-in-vogue-nvidia-releases-new-open-transcription-model/

Nvidia on Wednesday released Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, a free, open-weight model that transcribes audio in up to 40 languages. It also released version 2 of Granary, its open source speech dataset.

Nvidia boasts that Nemotron 3.5 can translate 31 languages to and from English, handle recordings up to an hour long, and tell up to eight speakers apart. It claims to beat rivals on benchmarks, including Hugging Face’s Open ASR Leaderboard.

These tools target developers building apps for business meetings and medical transcription, two of AI’s most established footholds in the workplace. Nvidia says developers can use the model, the updated Granary dataset, and tools like its NeMo Speech model to customize apps to recognize an industry’s jargon in each language. One drawback: Transcribe works with recorded audio, not livestreams.

Still, the company promises Transcribe, at 30 billion parameters, can run on a single chip, from the Jetson AGX Thor robotics controller and DGX Spark chip powering new laptops, to Blackwell datacenter chips. It joins Nvidia’s open-weight voice models, including the super small yet powerful Parakeet (1.1 billion parameters).

Nvidia’s latest voice entry comes during a busy period of AI voice releases: Mistral shipped Voxtral 3 earlier this month; ElevenLabs launched Realtime for instant speech in voice agents; and Salesforce released Launa-Omni, its voice model.
