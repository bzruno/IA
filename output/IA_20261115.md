## 2. Hugging Face and OpenRouter join forces to make open-weight models easy to run

- **Fonte:** Hugging Face
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-15 21:00

Hugging Face and OpenRouter are teaming up to make open-weight models easy to run. Every major open-weight release is already being published on the Hugging Face Hub, and OpenRouter serves those models at scale, routing each request across 80+ providers based on live measurements of each one. Together, every open-weight model on the Hub that a provider serves can be called with your Hugging Face token, through the same API, with the best provider picked for each request.

Today, we are switching Inference Providers from our own custom router to OpenRouter's. Requests made to language models now go through OpenRouter, which picks the best provider for each call based on live price, speed and reliability, and retries on another provider if the first one fails. Your code stays the same.

The router supports OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses APIs and Anthropic's Messages API for every chat model, whatever the provider behind it. Pick your coding agent or SDK and a model, then copy the steps, or copy the whole setup as a prompt for your agent:

Setup guides for Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Pi and Hermes Agent

The same Hugging Face token also gives you storage with Storage Buckets, compute with Jobs and dedicated inference with Inference Endpoints. PRO, Team and Enterprise plans include monthly credits for compute and inference.

On OpenRouter, open-weight models went from 26.8% of the tokens served in October 2025 to 71.7% at the end of September 2026, after peaking at 77.5% in July (OpenRouter, October 2026). Over the same period, weekly open-weight volume grew more than 60-fold, against almost tenfold for closed models. This week, the most used model on OpenRouter is an open-weight one, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017, with 59.2 trillion tokens (rankings).

Open-weight models cost about four times less on average: $0.28 per million input tokens and $1.62 per million output tokens, against $1.27 and $6.36 for closed models.

The best open-weight models still trail the best proprietary ones on the hardest benchmarks. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, Kimi K3.1 Pro, the best open-weight model, scores 46 against 65 for Claude Fable 5.2, at about a seventh of the cost per task ($3.68 against $26.54). For a lot of workloads, that trade-off is worth it.

OpenRouter serves more than 800 trillion tokens a month to over 10 million users, across 600+ models from 80+ providers. Its routing is based on live measurements of every provider across that traffic.

OpenRouter recently rebuilt its provider routing: instead of only looking at the lowest list price, it sends each request to the provider with the best mix of price, speed and reliability.

When a provider slows down or starts failing, traffic moves away from it until it recovers, and a failed try is retried on the next best provider inside the same call. In OpenRouter's measurements, the new routing cut capacity errors by 46% and improved time to first token by 16.9% and throughput by 16.3%.

Before, Inference Providers picked one provider for your request and returned its error if it failed. Now, the request is retried on another provider. You can still choose a policy or a provider with a suffix on the model id:

zai-org/GLM-5.3            # default: best mix of price, speed and reliability

zai-org/GLM-5.3:fastest    # highest throughput

zai-org/GLM-5.3:cheapest   # lowest price

zai-org/GLM-5.3:novita     # only Novita

Routing is also cache-aware: a conversation stays on the provider that holds its prompt cache. To make it explicit, send a stable UUID per conversation in the X-HF-Session-Id header (see the prompt caching guide).

Pricing doesn't change: you pay the provider's price on your Hugging Face bill, and Hugging Face doesn't add a markup on top of it. PRO users get $2 of credits every month, and Team and Enterprise organizations get $2 per seat. See Pricing and Billing for the details.

Image, video, and audio generation continue to work exactly as before, directly through Hugging Face Inference Providers without going through OpenRouter. You can keep using the same token and API, and access tens of thousands of LoRAs.

On model pages, you can chat with the model right away in the widget. Requests to language models now go through OpenRouter and are billed to your Hugging Face account or organization, so you can no longer use your own API key for a provider with these models. You can still pick a provider with a suffix on the model id (model:provider) or turn providers off in your settings, and your code stays the same. Apple is debuting a new study to understand how people use AI for health. But it isn’t quite following other AI startups just yet; it wants to study how clinically grounded answers can help people.

The iPhone maker is partnering with healthcare-focused AI company OpenEvidence to launch a new study app. Apple is inviting users in the U.S. over 21, using iPhone 15 or later, and running iOS 26 or later, with Apple Intelligence enabled. The company is aiming to run this study with up to 200,000 participants for more than 12 months.

The new app will allow users to ask health-related questions, as they do with ChatGPT or Claude, with answers grounded in clinical data, from sources including The New England Journal of Medicine, Journal of the American Medical Association, and Nature. Apple said that the app will try to give simplified answers to everyday medical questions. Users can rate those answers and provide feedback about the responses; they’ll also be asked to participate in monthly surveys.

Participants can share data from HealthKit such as resting heart rate, activity, sleep, or lab results to get more personalized and pointed suggestions from the app. The company said the app can also help prepare participants better for a doctor’s visit, and it will guide users to specific providers for answers related to concerning medical issues.

Apple specified that the data will be anonymized before sending it to a secure third-party cloud provider, and it won’t be used to train any kind of AI models. The company said that OpenEvidence deletes the query and response data after 24 hours.

“At Apple, we believe responsible innovation starts with scientific rigor and clinical grounding. Health is deeply personal, and conducting a dedicated research study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend, and evaluate health AI in everyday life,” Sumbul Desai, M.D., Apple’s vice president of Health and Fitness, said in a statement, adding, “Insights from this study will help advance the broader scientific conversation and ensure that as technology evolves, we continue building trusted tools that genuinely support people wherever they are on their health journey.”

The app is available to download starting today for U.S. users. It’s not clear if Apple wants to release AI-powered answering to its Health app or Siri yet. The potential is huge — earlier this year, OpenAI said users were asking over 300 million health-related queries each week.

Between rising interest rates, a nationwide AI backlash, a major political shift in Washington, D.C., and the sudden ouster of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, the tech industry is under mounting strain.

Now comes the most important tech IPO in years, and a defining event for the burgeoning but still young artificial intelligence market.

With Anthropic expected to debut on the Nasdaq on Thursday, the stakes are sky-high for almost every major tech company. Advanced Micro Devices, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company are collectively investing $26 billion at the IPO price, which is currently expected at $70 to $78 a share. Some of those companies are existing investors, as is early backer Amazon, whose stake could be in the hundreds of billions of dollars.

In addition to all the cash on the line, tech giants also need Anthropic's spending for many years to come. The Claude creator and its top rival, OpenAI, are driving an outsized amount of the AI buildout, and are burning historic amounts of money in the process.

Anthropic has outstanding spending commitments of over $900 billion, according to its latest prospectus. It's on the hook to spend at least $110 billion with Amazon over the course of a decade, and another $400 billion with other companies for cloud capacity, according to filings and partner disclosures. The lab also made $300 billion in commitments with data center and chip partners. Anthropic intends to lay out $125 billion to $150 billion for AI chips from Google, though delivery won't start until 2028.

"The numbers are just staggering," Joe Fath, a partner at venture firm Eclipse and former T. Rowe Price portfolio manager, said in an interview. "It's hard to get your head wrapped around it."

Cloud partners Amazon, Google and Microsoft account for 31% of contractual obligations, Anthropic said in its prospectus. Last year, Amazon built an Indiana data center just for Anthropic, using custom chips.

By going public now, Anthropic can raise a large portion of the money it needs to pay for the technology and infrastructure required for building and hosting its frontier models and expanding portfolio of services. The company recorded a net loss of $104 billion through the first nine months of 2026. The IPO could yield over $90 billion in proceeds, on top of Anthropic's $132 billion in existing cash, including short-term investments.

The length of Anthropic's contracts is also unique. Typically such contracts last five years or less, while Anthropic's agreement with Amazon extends for twice that amount of time.

"Realistically, anything past three to four years, you're basically throwing darts in the dark," said Corey Quinn, chief cloud economist at spend management software vendor Duckbill. "It is almost a certainty that it will be renegotiated long before the culmination of that contract."

Meanwhile, a data center for Anthropic that starts leasing in 2027 has a 20-year term. For both space and computing, Anthropic is splurging now so it doesn't end up short on capacity long into the future.

"In the past, we have significantly underestimated our compute requirements, which left us capacity constrained and impacted both product development and deployment," Anthropic said in the prospectus.

Anthropic and Amazon didn't provide a comment.

It's a tough time for Anthropic to hit the Nasdaq, with the offering coming as tech investors are desperate for some good news. The Nasdaq is down to start November after dropping 5.3% in October, its worst month since March 2025.

Rising interest rates pushed investors out of debt-heavy neoclouds, which have become a critical piece of the infrastructure buildout. And in the midterm elections earlier this month, Democrats won sweeping victories in both houses of Congress and in key gubernatorial races, while dozens of ballot measures were passed that will potentially limit new data center development across the country.

Then came the Nvidia crisis. Shares of the chipmaker at the center of the AI boom tumbled 13% this week to their lowest since July 2025, after Huang's multiple reported affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy, led to his ouster early Thursday. Huang was replaced on an interim basis with CFO Colette Cress, who faces a major test on Tuesday, two days before the Anthropic debut, as Nvidia is set to report quarterly results.

The ongoing Nvidia fallout and the Anthropic IPO create quite the split-screen moment for tech's heavyweights, which are closely tied to both companies.

Google will be paying particularly close attention.

The company is set to own about 13% of Anthropic after the IPO, and is backstopping the lab's $19 billion in lease commitments to Terawulf, which will supply data center space in Kentucky. If Anthropic goes insolvent, Google will step in and pay the remaining rent or a termination fee, Terawulf said.

"Everybody knows that Anthropic is going to be the future of Google," said Subhadeep Chatterjee, a former Google cloud partnerships leader who now advises companies.

Google didn't provide a comment.

One major impetus for Anthropic to enter costly and long-term guarantees is that OpenAI is being equally aggressive, and capacity is limited, particularly for graphics processing units. Going long can mean locking in rates as the industry confronts questions about whether data center development will slow.

"You will need to be in a rush to get infrastructure, and if you are not going to be there, you will be out of business," said Eli Mansoor, CEO of OskaQ Consulting, which advises companies on cloud negotiations. "It used to be that prices always go down, but this is changing, especially in the GPU world."

Big financial firms are lining up to help Anthropic get what it wants. Bank of America, Blackstone, Citigroup and Morgan Stanley have offered $63 billion to buy racks full of Broadcom AI chips that Anthropic will rent. Broadcom has provided a backstop with a maximum liability of $43 billion.

That's a lot of silicon and a massive bet for a five-year-old company that's never lived through a technology downturn.

"It's not a risk-free prospect, but I would call it smart investing," said David Tollen, founder of Sycamore Legal, where he specializes in IT contracts. "They're investing, in a sense, in themselves. They're putting money into something that if it succeeds, they get a piece of the action."

## 6. OpenAI startet ChatGPT Wallet: KI-Agenten kaufen für Nutzer ein

- **Fonte:** Heise
- **Publicado em:** 2026-11-15 11:56
- **Relevância:** 5.0
- **Link:** https://www.heise.de/news/OpenAI-startet-ChatGPT-Wallet-KI-Agenten-kaufen-fuer-Nutzer-ein-11525597.html?wt_mc=rss.red.ho.ho.atom.beitrag.beitrag

OpenAI hat am Freitag seine neue Bezahlmethode ChatGPT Wallet vorgestellt. Damit können die eigenen KI-Agenten des Unternehmens, die sogenannten Dots, im Namen der Nutzerinnen und Nutzer einkaufen.

Das Unternehmen entwickelte die Funktion gemeinsam mit American Express, Mastercard und Visa. Karteninhaber können so ihre bestehenden Kreditkartenprämien und Schutzleistungen beibehalten.

ChatGPT Wallet wird zunächst nur ChatGPT-Pro-Abonnenten in den USA ab 18 Jahren zur Verfügung stehen. So will das Unternehmen die Funktionsweise des Wallets testen und es gemeinsam mit Unternehmen, Banken und Zahlungsnetzwerken verbessern.

So funktioniert das ChatGPT Wallet

Die Dots stellte das Unternehmen Ende September vor. Die interaktiven Agenten sollen diverse Aufgaben ihrer Nutzer proaktiv erledigen und koordinieren. Mit dem ChatGPT Wallet sollen die Dots nun auch einkaufen können.

Um das ChatGPT Wallet zu nutzen, müssen Nutzer einmalig ihre Kartendaten angeben. Anschließend beauftragen sie ihren Agenten damit, nach einem Produkt zu suchen. Vor dem Kauf bekommt der Nutzer die Gelegenheit, die Artikel, den Betrag und die ausgewählte Karte zu überprüfen. Schließlich genehmigt er den Kauf mithilfe einer biometrischen Sicherheitsüberprüfung, etwa via Face ID oder Fingerabdruckscan.

OpenAI erklärt, dass die Dots keinen direkten Zugriff auf die Kartendaten der Nutzer erhalten. Stattdessen stellen die Zahlungsnetzwerke dem Agenten Zahlungstoken zur Verfügung, die eine für die jeweilige Transaktion spezifische Kartennummer, einen Sicherheitscode und ein Ablaufdatum enthalten. ChatGPT Wallet gibt die tatsächlichen Kartendaten somit nicht an den Händler oder den Agenten weiter. Die Funktionsweise ähnelt damit dem Vorgehen von Google Pay oder Apple Pay.

Unterstützung von Unternehmen

OpenAI hofft so, einen agentengesteuerten Handel zu etablieren. Das Unternehmen geht davon aus, dass Nutzer künftig Einkäufe vollständig an Agenten delegieren könnten. Sie müssten lediglich Vorgaben festlegen, wie die Höhe der Ausgaben oder einen Zeitraum, und den Rest übernehme der Agent.

Viele Websites und Zahlungssysteme seien noch nicht auf agentische Käufe vorbereitet und könnten Käufe über ChatGPT Wallet ablehnen. Um den agentischen Handel voranzutreiben, will OpenAI Unternehmen aktiv beim Übergang unterstützen.

Neben dem ChatGPT Wallet kündigt OpenAI deshalb weitere Werkzeuge und Anleitungen für Unternehmen an, die es in seinem „Commerce Partner Playbook“ bereitstellt. Darin erklärt es, wie Firmen sicherstellen können, dass Einkäufe durch KI-Agenten nicht versehentlich abgelehnt werden oder wie die Zusammenarbeit mit Zahlungsanbietern verbessert werden könne. Dafür arbeitet OpenAI nach eigenen Angaben bereits mit Betrugsabwehrspezialisten wie Forter, Riskified, Sardine und Signifyd sowie mit Zahlungsdienstleistern wie Adyen, Checkout.com und Stripe zusammen.

The company is conducting the Apple Health AI Usage Study in collaboration with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform used by healthcare professionals that generates answers based on medical information from sources like The New England Journal of Medicine. Participants will use a new iOS app to ask medical questions and then rate the responses based on criteria including accuracy, detail, and relevance.

Apple’s new study looks to improve the effectiveness of AI health tools

Nvidia did not comment on questions about Huang’s affairs or when the company learned about his involvement with the alleged spy. The Chinese government has denied any involvement in the affair or efforts at corporate espionage, with a Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesperson earlier this week calling the reports “nothing but a smear against China”.

Silicon Valley’s long history of espionage

The fallout from allegations that China conducted a high-profile foreign intelligence operation against Huang has had global implications on the market and tech industry, with the Nasdaq falling 4% immediately after Huang’s resignation and Nvidia losing hundreds of billions in market value this week. Yet security experts say that while the impact of the operation is unique, the tactics and goals are not.

Silicon Valley, flush with incredibly valuable intellectual property and trade secrets, has long been a prized target for foreign spying operations. Throughout the cold war, Soviet agents conducted a decades-long effort to steal technological secrets from US tech firms, including major chip companies, that involved a complex web of international espionage.

During the 1970s and 80s, Soviet intelligence officers operated out of the Russian consulate in San Francisco as they carried out spying operations, according to former FBI special agent Rick Smith, who spent over two decades in the Bay Area working in counterintelligence. US officials estimated at the time that the Moscow Kremlin had mobilized thousands of spies in the country with the intent to steal intellectual property and help the Soviet Union copy American technology they could not produce themselves.

“Gaining access to our advanced technology continues to be their top priority,” William Casey, director of the CIA, warned in 1985.

Other countries and foreign nationals have also launched their own corporate espionage operations. North Korean spies created two businesses in the US with the intent of spreading malicious software to infect cryptocurrency developers, Reuters and cybersecurity firms reported last year. In October, US authorities arrested a Saudi national accused of planting a sophisticated listening device within the headquarters of OpenAI.

In recent years, however, it’s China’s government that has been most prominently accused of a multilayered effort to steal technological capabilities and IP from US tech firms.

A report from the nonprofit Center for Strategic and International Studies found at least 224 incidents of Chinese espionage in the US between 2000 and 2022, with over half of the plots directed at the theft of commercial technologies. In January, a federal jury in California convicted a former Google engineer of stealing confidential company information to benefit the Chinese government.

Some operations linked to China have even involved honeypot-style schemes where a suspected spy has pursued personal or sexual relationships with politicians and executives. The Washington Post obtained declassified FBI documents in August that showed that former California congressman Eric Swalwell admitted to “occasionally hooking up” with suspected Chinese operative Christine Fang.

The alleged Chinese operation involving Huang stood out to security experts, however, for both the extremely high-profile target and its apparent success.

Huang was first seen with Alice Huang, a Chinese executive who ran the Singapore-based Megaspeed datacenter firm, in late 2023. The two were together at a bar in Taiwan in 2024 and again at a conference in 2025, by which time the US commerce department was investigating whether Megaspeed was diverting AI chips to China in violation of export controls. In spring of this year, state secretary Marco Rubio oversaw a classified memo reporting that the Nvidia CEO was in a relationship with a suspected Chinese spy based on electronic surveillance of Alice Huang, according to the New York Times. Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang ultimately appears to have helped facilitate the transfer of billions of dollars in advanced Nvidia chips to China.

“As an American, it’s not good, but I’m impressed with their skills,” Smith, the former FBI counterintelligence official, said. “What a target and a tremendous job by the Chinese.”

Huang was not only the head of what was once the world’s most valuable company, but he is also a close ally of Donald Trump who accompanied the president at White House dinners and on Trump’s official state visit to China. Huang has been an influential voice on tech policy, especially on export control and open source AI model issues, although it is unclear whether his extramarital relationship had any bearing on his views. Trump backed Huang after the scandal, calling him an “honorable guy”.

The Trump administration is also facing questions over why Alice Huang’s firm Megaspeed was spared from being placed on a list of companies that pose national security concerns, Axios reported, despite intelligence outlining issues with the company. US commerce secretary Howard Lutnick is under pressure to resign over perceived inaction in response to problems with Megaspeed.

Nvidia has since announced that it hired a top US law firm to conduct an investigation into Huang’s relationship with the suspected spy, while Trump stated that the FBI would launch its own probe into the affair. Huang has agreed to testify before Congress and he now faces a lawsuit in California accusing him of misleading shareholders.

Easy access and social engineering

In order to combat the wave of corporate espionage and foreign influence campaigns, many companies have turned to intelligence and threat assessment firms to harden their security – several of which say they are expecting more demand for their services after the Nvidia scandal. These firms hold training and conduct investigations into what makes a company vulnerable, often focusing on the human elements of security.

“Everybody thinks it’s like what we see in Hollywood – surreptitious entry and listening devices and bugs and tails,” said Eduardo Jany, head of global security for a major US corporation. “It’s really more about social engineering.”

Malicious actors will seek out staff at companies that hold positions of influence or valuable information, frequently using what is publicly available through social media and data brokers to learn their personal information and create targeted plans. An employee with a substance abuse or gambling problem provides one avenue of exploitation, experts said, while a staffer who is lonely and seeking out relationships offers another kind of vulnerability.

Attempts at corporate espionage can also often come in the guise of business opportunities, as it appears was potentially the case with Nvidia and Huang, with spies posing as fellow tech executives or financiers.

“They’re looking at easy access and proximity from conferences and customer relationships,” Jany said. “They sidle up to our executives at any events or trade shows, or even make promises of investments.”

Corporations will sometimes hire firms to conduct what’s called penetration testing to check for weak points in their security, which involves using simulated attacks to uncover real vulnerabilities. One security expert described an example of his firm following an employee to the parking lot of a grocery store in order to gain access to their car and take a corporate ID card left hanging from their rearview mirror.

Dealing with C-suite executives like Huang tends to pose its own unique challenges for security and threat assessment firms, experts said. Top leadership at companies possesses far more autonomy and authority than the average employee, making it harder for underlings to confront them about misconduct or potential risks. CEOs can also be hostile to seeing themselves as fallible, with consultants having to sensitively approach the idea that their personal relationships may not be what they seem.

“These are really smart, accomplished people and you don’t want to come across telling them that they don’t know how to behave themselves or that they’re not sophisticated enough to know that they’re being taken advantage of,” said Brian Stephens, president of security risk advisory at the CEO advisory firm Teneo.

Executives can also protest that they’re not important enough to be a target or that everything about them is already public.

“It’s a defense mechanism to being told that the way you live your life is going to have to change,” said Elizabeth Buckley, Teneo’s head of cyber and technical solutions.

In addition to educating staff on protecting themselves from being exploited, security and risk experts emphasized that companies need robust mechanisms to flag concerns about misconduct or outside threats. One question facing Nvidia is who within the company knew about Huang’s relationships and whether any concerns were raised internally. Several corporate intelligence and security experts said it is unfathomable that someone at Nvidia would not be tracking the CEO’s whereabouts and who he was meeting with, especially on trips abroad.

Yet for all the training, oversight and security protocols companies can put in place, experts said that no amount of safeguards can totally replace personal judgment. While looming investigations will seek to answer what went wrong at Nvidia to lead to the current disaster, Huang’s downfall was of his own making.

“Jensen Huang lost his mind here,” Smith, the former counterintelligence agent, said. “But that often happens with romantic relationships.”
